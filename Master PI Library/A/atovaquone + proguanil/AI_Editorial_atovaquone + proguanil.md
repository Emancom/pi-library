# Editorial Product Information: atovaquone + proguanil

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **atovaquone + proguanil**
- Chemical class: Not specified in available sources
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Prophylaxis of Plasmodium falciparum malaria in adults and children weighing ≥11 kg.
- Treatment of Plasmodium falciparum malaria in adults and children aged ≥3 years.
- Regional note: Indication statements as above are based on the available sources; if any jurisdiction-specific differences exist they were not specified in the provided material.

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General administration instructions
- Take daily with food or a milky drink to enhance absorption of atovaquone.
- Take at the same time each day.
- If vomiting occurs within 1 hour of dosing, repeat the dose.
- Tablets should preferably be swallowed whole. If dosing young children is difficult, tablets may be crushed and mixed with a small amount of milk and consumed immediately.
- If patients are unable to tolerate food, tablets can be administered but systemic exposure of atovaquone will be reduced.

### Prophylaxis
- Start 1–2 days before entering a malaria-endemic area and continue daily until 7 days after leaving the area.
- Adults (body weight >40 kg): one dose per day (corresponding to the adult-strength combination tablet described in source material).
- Children (by body weight):
  - 11–20 kg: 1 × low-strength tablet daily
  - 21–30 kg: 2 × low-strength tablets daily
  - 31–40 kg: 3 × low-strength tablets daily
  - >40 kg: adult dose (1 × adult-strength tablet daily)

### Treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria
- Adults: four adult-strength tablets as a single daily dose for three consecutive days.
- Children (by body weight; administered as adult-strength tablets in the provided sources for treatment):
  - 11–20 kg: 1 × adult-strength tablet once daily for 3 days
  - 21–30 kg: 2 × adult-strength tablets once daily for 3 days
  - 31–40 kg: 3 × adult-strength tablets once daily for 3 days
  - >40 kg: 4 × adult-strength tablets once daily for 3 days

### Missed dose
- If vomiting occurs within 1 hour of dosing, repeat the dose. For other missed-dose guidance specific instructions were not provided in the available sources.

### Titration
- Not applicable / Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to atovaquone, proguanil hydrochloride, or any component of the formulation.
- **Prophylaxis contraindication**: severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min) — do not use for prophylaxis in patients with severe renal impairment.
- Prior severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) to atovaquone/proguanil: patients who have developed SCARs (including Stevens–Johnson syndrome, DRESS, erythema multiforme) with prior use must not receive atovaquone/proguanil again.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed / prominent warnings
- **Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs)** including Stevens–Johnson syndrome, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and erythema multiforme have been reported. If signs/symptoms suggestive of SCARs appear, discontinue atovaquone + proguanil immediately and provide alternative therapy.

### General warnings and precautions
- Not evaluated for treatment of cerebral malaria or other severe/complicated malaria (e.g., hyperparasitaemia, pulmonary oedema, renal failure); not recommended for such presentations.
- Safety and efficacy in prophylaxis or treatment have not been established in paediatric patients weighing <11 kg.
- In the event of recrudescence of P. falciparum infection or failure of chemoprophylaxis, treat with a different blood schizonticide.
- Treatment of P. vivax with atovaquone + proguanil alone commonly results in relapse; patients with P. vivax or P. ovale infections and those with intense exposure require additional anti-hypnozoite therapy (e.g., primaquine).
- Absorption of atovaquone is reduced without food; if patients cannot tolerate food, systemic exposure will be lower.
- In patients with acute malaria who have vomiting or diarrhoea, consider alternative therapy; if used, closely monitor parasitaemia.
- Co-administration with other antimalarial drugs has not been evaluated — use caution.
- Parasitaemia should be closely monitored when co-administered with metoclopramide or tetracycline.
- Co-administration with rifampicin or rifabutin is not recommended.
- Caution when initiating or withdrawing therapy in patients on coumarin anticoagulants due to possible potentiation of anticoagulation by proguanil.

### Monitoring requirements
- Closely monitor parasitaemia in patients with vomiting/diarrhoea or when co-administered with metoclopramide or tetracycline.
- Exercise clinical vigilance for cutaneous reactions and systemic hypersensitivity; discontinue immediately if SCAR suspected.
- Exercise caution and monitor anticoagulation (e.g., INR) when initiating or stopping atovaquone + proguanil in patients on coumarin anticoagulants.
- Other specific laboratory monitoring instructions (e.g., routine scheduled LFT monitoring) were not explicitly specified in the available sources.

## 8. Drug Interactions

### Key interaction considerations (reported in the available sources)
- Proguanil may potentiate the anticoagulant effect of warfarin and other coumarin anticoagulants. Exercise caution when initiating or withdrawing atovaquone + proguanil in patients on continuous coumarin therapy; monitor anticoagulation.
- Concomitant tetracycline, metoclopramide, rifampicin and rifabutin have been associated with significant decreases in plasma concentration of atovaquone; clinical consequences may include reduced antimalarial efficacy.
  - Concomitant tetracycline and atovaquone/proguanil reduced atovaquone plasma concentrations but did not affect cure rates in one clinical setting; nonetheless, close parasitological monitoring is advised.
  - Concomitant rifampicin or rifabutin: co-administration not recommended.
- Concomitant administration of atovaquone with indinavir resulted in a 23% decrease in indinavir Cmin in healthy volunteers; use caution when co-prescribing due to reduced indinavir trough levels.
- Co-administration with efavirenz or boosted protease inhibitors may reduce atovaquone (and proguanil) concentrations by as much as 75%; because decreased concentrations may reduce antimalarial efficacy, concomitant administration should be avoided whenever possible.
- In clinical studies (non-malaria indications) small decreases in atovaquone concentrations were associated with concomitant use of: paracetamol, benzodiazepines, aciclovir, opiates, cephalosporins, anti-diarrhoeal agents and laxatives. Clinical significance is unknown.
- No change in steady-state atovaquone plasma concentrations was observed with: fluconazole, clotrimazole, ketoconazole, antacids, systemic corticosteroids, non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs, anti-emetic drugs (excluding metoclopramide), and H2‑antagonists.
- No information available in the provided sources regarding interactions with terfenadine or cisapride.
- Atovaquone is highly protein-bound (>99%) but did not displace other highly protein-bound drugs in vitro; clinically significant displacement interactions are unlikely.

### Management notes
- Avoid concomitant administration with rifampicin/rifabutin and, if possible, with efavirenz or boosted protease inhibitors.
- If co-administration with agents known to reduce atovaquone concentrations is unavoidable, monitor parasitaemia closely and consider alternative antimalarial therapy.
- Monitor INR/other coagulation parameters when starting or stopping atovaquone + proguanil in patients on coumarin anticoagulants.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

### Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category / classification: [Australian] Category B2 (as provided in the available sources).
- Safety summary:
  - The safety of the combination in human pregnancy has not been established; limited data available.
  - There is no information on effects of atovaquone administration during human pregnancy in the available sources.
  - Foetal death and malformation have been rarely reported in association with proguanil use; the relationship is uncertain and the number of reports is low relative to overall use.
  - Animal studies: embryo‑foetal development studies with the combination did not indicate teratogenic potential in rats (up to 50:20 mg/kg/day) or rabbits (up to 100:40 mg/kg/day) at exposures described in the source material; higher-dose atovaquone in rabbits was associated with foetal effects in presence of maternal toxicity.
  - Use in pregnancy should be considered only if expected maternal benefit outweighs foetal risk.

### Breastfeeding
- Atovaquone: not known whether excreted into human milk; rat data indicate milk concentrations ~30% of maternal plasma.
- Proguanil: excreted in human milk in small quantities.
- Recommendation from available sources: breastfeeding is not recommended during treatment with atovaquone + proguanil.

### Fertility
- Atovaquone: no human data on fertility; animal studies did not show effects on reproductive potential at exposures up to the levels stated in the available sources.
- Proguanil: some animal data indicate potential impairment of fertility/early embryonic loss, but clinical relevance is considered limited given extensive human experience at recommended doses.
- Combined effects on fertility in animals: Not specified in available sources beyond statements above.

## 10. Use in Special Populations

### Paediatric use
- Prophylaxis: approved for children weighing ≥11 kg (dosing by body weight as described in Section 4).
- Treatment: approved for children aged ≥3 years (weight-based dosing provided in Section 4).
- Safety and efficacy not established in children <11 kg.

### Elderly
- Pharmacokinetic data indicate no dosage adjustment is required in the elderly.

### Renal impairment
- Mild to moderate renal impairment: pharmacokinetic studies indicate no dosage adjustment is required.
- Severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min):
  - Prophylaxis: contraindicated.
  - Treatment: alternatives to atovaquone + proguanil should be recommended whenever possible.

### Hepatic impairment
- Mild to moderate hepatic impairment: pharmacokinetic studies indicate no dosage adjustment is required.
- Severe hepatic impairment: not studied; use with caution and no dosing recommendation available.

## 11. Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action
- Proguanil: the proguanil component acts by inhibition of parasitic dihydrofolate reductase (as specified in the available sources).
- Atovaquone: mechanism of action was not specified in the available sources.

### Pharmacokinetics (as specified in the available sources)
- Absorption: Atovaquone absorption is enhanced by food or milk; absorption and systemic exposure are reduced when taken without food or in presence of diarrhoea/vomiting.
- Distribution: Atovaquone is highly protein-bound (>99%).
- Metabolism / Excretion: Not fully specified in the available sources.
- Drug–drug effects on levels:
  - Concomitant efavirenz or boosted protease inhibitors may reduce atovaquone and proguanil concentrations by up to ~75%.
  - Rifampicin, rifabutin, tetracycline, and metoclopramide associated with reduced atovaquone concentrations.
  - Concomitant atovaquone reduced indinavir Cmin by ~23% in healthy volunteers.
- Milk excretion: rat data show atovaquone concentrations in milk ~30% of maternal plasma; proguanil is excreted in human milk in small amounts.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Not specified in detail in the available sources beyond the antimalarial indications and proguanil’s antifolate action.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Prophylaxis trials:
  - Placebo-controlled trials and active-controlled trials versus mefloquine or chloroquine/proguanil were conducted; adverse event profiles for atovaquone + proguanil were generally similar to placebo or comparators, with fewer neuropsychiatric and gastrointestinal adverse reactions compared with mefloquine and chloroquine/proguanil in some studies.
  - Example trial sizes reported in available tables: prophylaxis placebo-controlled (n ≈ 232 atovaquone/proguanil vs n ≈ 241 placebo); active-controlled trials included several hundred participants per arm (numbers shown in trial tables).
- Treatment trials:
  - Phase III treatment trials showed adverse events that were largely similar between atovaquone + proguanil and comparator antimalarial regimens, suggesting many reported adverse events were related to underlying malaria rather than study drugs.
  - Reported treatment-associated laboratory abnormalities included increases in ALT, AST and bilirubin in a proportion of patients (e.g., ALT ~6%, AST ~5%, bilirubin ~2% in pooled adult treatment data).
- Paediatric trials:
  - Paediatric safety data presented in pooled tables; commonly reported (>2%) events in treated children included dehydration, tinnitus, and anorexia.
- Limitations:
  - Some trial data regarding post-marketing events and complete adverse event listings were not provided in the available materials; the source material was truncated and did not contain a complete worldwide post-approval safety listing.
