### Preparation and administration instructions
- Pharmacy bulk package format (mixed contents provide multiple single doses): transfer contents of the secondary vial into the primary vial and mix prior to dispensing individual doses.
- Each single dose must be withdrawn from the mixed bulk solution using an appropriate sterile transfer device; after the first penetration of the bulk vial closure, complete dispensing from that bulk vial within 4 hours.
- Add the prescribed single dose to at least 100 mL of intravenous dextrose or saline solution prior to infusion for each patient.
- After dilution into an intravenous infusion bag, refrigerate unless used immediately and use the diluted infusion within 24 hours.
- Minimize exposure to light during preparation and administration because some vitamins (notably A, D, and riboflavin) are light sensitive.
- Do not administer undiluted intravenously (may cause dizziness, faintness, and tissue irritation).
- Do not add directly to intravenous fat emulsions.
- Visually inspect solution for particulate matter and discoloration prior to administration.
- If physical or chemical incompatibilities with co‑administered agents are identified, avoid admixture or Y‑site administration.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- None specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions (clinical)
- **Aluminum toxicity**: This product contains aluminum that may be toxic, particularly with prolonged parenteral administration in patients with impaired renal function or in premature neonates. Pediatric patients (especially premature neonates) and patients with renal impairment are at increased risk. Research indicates accumulation associated with CNS and bone toxicity when parenteral aluminum exposure exceeds approximately 4–5 mcg/kg/day; tissue loading may occur at lower rates. Periodic monitoring of aluminum levels is recommended during prolonged therapy.
- **Allergic reactions to thiamine**: Intravenous thiamine can cause allergic reactions ranging from urticaria, shortness of breath, wheezing and angioedema to rare anaphylaxis. Be prepared to manage hypersensitivity reactions.
- **Hypervitaminosis A**: Patients with renal failure or liver disease may be at increased risk for vitamin A toxicity; monitor vitamin A blood levels periodically and use caution when supplementing vitamin A in these populations. Supplemental vitamin A may be required for low‑birth weight infants, but monitor levels.
- **Decreased anticoagulant effect of warfarin**: Vitamin K in the product can antagonize warfarin; monitor prothrombin time/INR and adjust warfarin dose as needed.
- **Interference with diagnosis of megaloblastic anemia**: Administration of folic acid and cyanocobalamin can mask deficiencies when evaluating megaloblastic anemia. Avoid administration prior to blood sampling intended to diagnose folate or B12 deficiency.
- **Potential for vitamin deficiencies or excesses**: Periodically monitor blood vitamin concentrations during parenteral multivitamin therapy, particularly when the product is the sole source of vitamins or when given long term. May not correct long‑standing deficiencies; additional doses of specific vitamins may be required.
- **Interference with urine glucose testing**: Ascorbic acid can cause false negative urine glucose results.
- **Vitamin E overdose in infants receiving additional vitamin E**: Additional oral or parenteral vitamin E supplementation in infants receiving this product may produce elevated vitamin E blood concentrations and possible vitamin E toxicity. Avoid additional vitamin E in infants.
- **Risk of low vitamin A levels due to adherence to plastic**: Vitamin A concentrations may be reduced after storage/administration due to adherence to plastic; monitor vitamin A levels and consider additional therapeutic vitamin A dosing when indicated, especially in low‑birth weight infants.
- **Risk of E‑Ferol syndrome in low‑birth weight infants**: Polysorbates present in the formulation have been associated with E‑Ferol syndrome (thrombocytopenia, renal dysfunction, hepatomegaly, cholestasis, ascites, hypotension, metabolic acidosis) in low‑birth weight infants. No cases directly attributed to this product were reported in the available source, but be aware of the risk.

### Monitoring requirements
- Periodically monitor blood vitamin concentrations during parenteral use to detect deficiencies or excesses.
- Monitor aluminum levels during prolonged parenteral administration, particularly in patients with renal impairment and premature neonates.
- Monitor renal function, calcium and phosphorus in patients with renal impairment.
- Monitor vitamin A blood levels in patients with renal or hepatic impairment and in low‑birth weight infants.
- Monitor prothrombin time/INR in patients receiving warfarin concomitantly.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Avoid admixture or coadministration with moderately alkaline solutions (e.g., sodium bicarbonate) and certain alkaline drugs (e.g., acetazolamide sodium, aminophylline, ampicillin sodium, tetracycline HCl, chlorothiazide sodium) because of physical incompatibility.
- Do not mix with calcium salts (e.g., calcium gluconate) where folic acid stability may be affected.
- Vitamin A and thiamine may react with bisulfite solutions.
- Avoid adding directly to fat emulsions.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Effect on co‑administered drugs
- Warfarin (antagonism): Vitamin K in the product may decrease the anticoagulant effect of warfarin → Monitor INR/prothrombin time and adjust warfarin dose as appropriate.
- Antibiotics: Thiamine, riboflavin, pyridoxine, niacinamide, and ascorbic acid may decrease activities of erythromycin, kanamycin, streptomycin, doxycycline, and lincomycin.
- Bleomycin: Ascorbic acid and riboflavin may inactivate bleomycin in vitro and could reduce bleomycin activity.
- Levodopa: Pyridoxine may increase peripheral metabolism of levodopa, decreasing levodopa blood levels and possibly reducing efficacy.
- Phenytoin: Folic acid may increase phenytoin metabolism and lower serum phenytoin concentrations, potentially increasing seizure risk.
- Methotrexate: Folic acid may decrease patient response to methotrexate therapy.

### Effect of other drugs on vitamin levels
- Hydralazine, isoniazid: Concomitant use may increase pyridoxine (vitamin B6) requirements.
- Phenytoin: May decrease serum folic acid concentrations.

### Management notes
- For warfarin coadministration, monitor INR and adjust anticoagulant dosing as needed.
- Consider monitoring relevant drug levels (e.g., phenytoin) and clinical response when starting or stopping concomitant vitamins.
- Avoid admixture or co‑administration where physical/chemical incompatibilities exist (see Warnings and Precautions).

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Risk summary: Administration of the approved recommended dose in parenteral nutrition is not expected to cause major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes when used as recommended. Pregnant patients should follow recommended daily allowances for pregnancy because vitamin requirements may exceed those of nonpregnant patients.
- Clinical considerations: Deficiency of essential vitamins (e.g., folic acid) is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes (e.g., neural tube defects); parenteral multivitamin supplementation should be considered when oral/enteral intake is inadequate.
- Animal reproduction studies: Not conducted with the combination product in available sources.
- Background population risks: The available source states general background risk estimates for major birth defects (2–4%) and miscarriage (15–20%) in the general U.S. population; interpret in clinical context.

### Breastfeeding
- Risk summary: Vitamins present in the product are also present in human milk. Administration of recommended doses in parenteral nutrition is not expected to cause harm to a breastfed infant.
- There is no information on effects on milk production in the available source.
- Lactating patients should follow recommended daily allowances appropriate for lactation as their requirements may exceed nonlactating patients.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Approved for prevention of vitamin deficiency in pediatric patients up to 11 years of age receiving parenteral nutrition. [US]
- Not studied in pediatric patients older than 11 years in the available source.
- Premature neonates are at particular risk of aluminum toxicity; monitor aluminum levels periodically.
- Low‑birth weight infants may require supplemental vitamin A; however, risk of E‑Ferol syndrome associated with polysorbates should be considered.
- Additional vitamin E supplementation in infants receiving this product is not recommended because it may produce elevated vitamin E blood concentrations and toxicity.

### Geriatric (elderly) use
- Not specified in available sources (no specific dosing adjustments or studies reported).

### Renal impairment
- The product has not been studied formally in patients with renal impairment.
- Monitor renal function, calcium, phosphorus and vitamin A levels in patients with renal impairment due to increased risk of aluminum accumulation and potential hypervitaminosis A.

### Hepatic impairment
- The product has not been studied formally in patients with hepatic impairment.
- Monitor vitamin A levels in patients with liver disease because they may be at higher risk of hypervitaminosis A.
