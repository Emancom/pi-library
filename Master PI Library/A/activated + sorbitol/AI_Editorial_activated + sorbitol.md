# Editorial Product Information: activated + sorbitol

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **activated + sorbitol**
- Chemical class:
  - Activated charcoal: adsorbent
  - Sorbitol: osmotic cathartic
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: oral; via nasogastric or orogastric tube

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval:
  - [Australian] First approval recorded as 25 July 2018
- Date(s) of last revision/update of the source PI:
  - [Australian] Last revision recorded as 08 September 2021
- Withdrawal, suspension, restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- If registration dates for jurisdictions other than those listed above are required: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of poisoning and drug overdosage after oral ingestion.
- Use is intended to adsorb unabsorbed drug/toxin in the gastrointestinal tract to reduce systemic absorption.
- Specific indication nuances:
  - Use for general acute oral poisonings (no regional variation stated).
  - Use in repeat-dose activated charcoal protocols: repeat-dose charcoal may be indicated for certain toxins (see Section 4), but this particular activated + sorbitol product is not intended for repeat-dose administration (see Sections 4 and 5).

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General principles
- Administer as soon as possible after oral ingestion; greatest likelihood of benefit when given within one hour of ingestion.
- Activated charcoal adsorbs only that portion of a poison not yet absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract.
- If the patient has undergone emesis or gastric lavage, activated + sorbitol may still be administered afterward.
- Prior to administration, shake the suspension vigorously for at least 30 seconds.
- Can be administered orally or via nasogastric/orogastric tube.
- Dilution for tube administration: due to viscosity, dilute with water as required. As a guide, for tubes <12 French gauge a minimum dilution of 0.1 parts water to 1 part product is recommended; the optimal dilution depends on tube gauge/length, syringe size and operator technique.

### Dosing — single dose
- Adults and children aged 12 years and over:
  - Single dose: 1 g activated charcoal per kg body weight (equivalent described in the source as 5 mL of the suspension per kg where that formulation correspondence is applicable), to a maximum single dose of 50 g activated charcoal.
- Children aged 1 to 11 years:
  - Single dose: 1 g activated charcoal per kg body weight (same maximum single dose of 50 g).
- Infants less than 1 year of age:
  - Contraindicated (see Section 5 and Section 4.3).

### Repeat doses
- Some poisonings (for example, life‑threatening ingestions of carbamazepine, dapsone, phenobarbitone, quinine or theophylline) may benefit from repeat-dose activated charcoal therapy in general.
- Important product-specific limitation: more than a single dose of this activated + sorbitol product is contraindicated for use during repeat-dose activated charcoal regimens (i.e., do not use multiple doses of this sorbitol-containing preparation when repeat-dose charcoal is required).
- If repeat charcoal therapy is being considered (with an appropriate charcoal product that does not contain a cathartic or that is approved for repeat dosing), monitor fluid and electrolyte balance closely.

### Administration notes / precautions during administration
- Do not administer together with systemically active emetics (e.g., ipecac) — activated charcoal can adsorb their active components rendering them ineffective. Emetics may be used to induce vomiting prior to charcoal administration only when appropriate; avoid induced emesis in patients who are drowsy, unconscious, convulsing, or likely to become drowsy within 30 minutes.
- Patients at risk of aspiration (absent/impaired gag reflex, coma, severe CNS depression, recent large ingestions of CNS depressants or seizure‑provoking agents) require airway protection (for example, cuffed endotracheal tube) prior to charcoal administration.
- Consider withholding this product for an adequate time interval prior to extubation to reduce risk of vomiting/aspiration.

### Missed dose
- Not applicable for single‑use, acute poisoning management. Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Poisonings where activated charcoal adsorption is ineffective or inadequate:
  - Strong acids and alkalis (caustics)
  - Ferrous sulfate and other iron salts
  - Cyanides
  - Tolbutamide and other sulfonylureas
  - Malathion
  - Dicophane (dichlorvos/related organophosphate — as listed in source)
  - Lithium
  - Ethanol
  - Methanol
  - Ethylene glycol
  - Hydrocarbons
- Patients with an unprotected airway (e.g., absent airway protection in obtunded or comatose patients) or with a gastrointestinal tract that is not anatomically intact.
- Patients with significant fluid or electrolyte abnormalities.
- Infants less than one year of age (contraindicated due to risk of excessive catharsis and dehydration).
- Use of repeat doses of this activated + sorbitol product is contraindicated (do not administer multiple doses of this sorbitol‑containing preparation during repeat‑dose charcoal therapy).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / highlighted warnings
- **Risk of aspiration and aspiration pneumonia; airway protection should be ensured in patients with impaired consciousness or depressed gag reflex. Fatalities have been reported following aspiration.**

### General warnings and precautions
- Aspiration of charcoal slurry and gastric contents is potentially serious; vomiting of charcoal and sorbitol increases aspiration risk.
- Consider delaying or withholding administration prior to extubation to reduce risk of post‑extubation vomiting and aspiration.
- Where a specific antidote exists for the poison, that antidote should generally be used first; do not administer a specific antidote concurrently with activated charcoal as the antidote itself may be adsorbed and inactivated by charcoal.
- Activated charcoal adsorbs many orally administered drugs; any essential concurrent medication should be administered parenterally.
- The presence of sorbitol produces a cathartic effect; diarrhoea may lead to dehydration and electrolyte disturbances.
- Use with caution in patients with ileus, absent or decreased bowel sounds, or who have ingested large amounts of medications that impair peristalsis.
- Avoid concomitant use of agents that decrease gut motility (examples noted in source: atropine, morphine, verapamil) where possible because of increased risk of gastrointestinal obstruction with repeat charcoal dosing.
- Activated charcoal can adsorb minerals, vitamins, enzymes and amino acids from the gastrointestinal tract; consider nutritional implications if clinically relevant.
- Only a single dose of this sorbitol‑containing activated charcoal product should be used when repeat-dose activated charcoal therapy is required — selection of an appropriate charcoal product without a cathartic is recommended for repeat-dose protocols.

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor fluid balance and electrolytes in all patients receiving this product, particularly:
  - Patients receiving repeated charcoal doses (if alternative charcoal used)
  - Children (especially young children)
  - Elderly patients
  - Patients with pre-existing fluid/electrolyte abnormalities
- Monitor for signs of aspiration (respiratory distress, hypoxia, cough, fever) and for signs of gastrointestinal obstruction following administration.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Elderly: increased susceptibility to fluid and electrolyte disturbances due to cathartic effect; monitor closely and avoid multiple doses of this product.
- Paediatric use: contraindicated in infants <1 year; children 1–11 years may receive a single dose but require close monitoring of fluid and electrolytes.
- Effects on laboratory tests: Not specified in available sources.

## 7. Adverse Effects
- Summary from available sources: Few serious adverse reactions or complications reported with single doses; however, several serious adverse events (including fatalities) have been associated with aspiration or with complications of repeat-dose use (intestinal obstruction, charcoal bezoars, severe dehydration and electrolyte imbalance in infants and children). Faecal discolouration (black stools) occurs frequently.

- Frequency information from source materials:

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic / adsorption interactions
- Activated charcoal can adsorb many orally administered drugs, antidotes and other orally ingested substances, reducing systemic availability.
- Examples and implications:
  - If an antidote exists (specific to the poisoning), that antidote should be administered first; concurrent administration with activated charcoal may result in adsorption and inactivation of the antidote.
  - Any required concurrent systemic medications should be given parenterally to avoid adsorption.
  - Activated charcoal may adsorb minerals, vitamins, enzymes and amino acids from the GI tract.

### Pharmacodynamic interactions / other interactions
- Systemically active emetics (e.g., ipecac): charcoal adsorbs their active components making them ineffective; do not administer such emetics concurrently with charcoal. Emetics may be used before charcoal when clinically appropriate and safe.
- Concomitant use of drugs/supportive agents that reduce gut motility (examples listed in source: atropine, morphine, verapamil) increases the risk of gastrointestinal obstruction, particularly with repeat-dose charcoal regimens. Avoid if possible.

### Clinically significant interactions and management
- If oral therapy with essential medications is required in the period after charcoal administration, consider alternative routes (intravenous, intramuscular) to ensure therapeutic efficacy.
- When a specific antidote must be used, avoid simultaneous administration with activated charcoal unless the antidote is administered by a route not subject to adsorption (i.e., parenteral).

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Data: little data available on use in pregnancy.
- Pharmacologic considerations:
  - Activated charcoal is not absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and is not expected to pose direct fetal risk.
  - Sorbitol's cathartic action may cause diarrhoea, with potential for maternal electrolyte disturbance or dehydration.
- Recommendation:
  - Use during pregnancy only when clearly necessary; balance the risk of the poisoning against potential maternal fluid/electrolyte effects.

### Breastfeeding
- Data: little data available on use during lactation.
- Pharmacologic considerations:
  - Activated charcoal and sorbitol are not systemically absorbed and are not expected to be excreted into breast milk.
  - Maternal dehydration or electrolyte imbalance from the cathartic effect could adversely affect the breastfeeding mother.
- Recommendation:
  - Use only when clinically indicated; monitor maternal hydration and electrolytes.

### Fertility
- Effects on fertility: No data available in the sources.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Infants less than 1 year: **contraindicated** due to risk of excessive catharsis, dehydration and electrolyte disturbance.
- Children 1–11 years: single‑dose administration permitted at 1 g activated charcoal per kg (max 50 g); careful monitoring of fluid and electrolytes required.
- Repeat doses: not recommended in children; repeat dosing should be used only when necessary and with monitoring of fluids and electrolytes.

### Elderly
- Elderly patients may be more susceptible to fluid and electrolyte disturbances from sorbitol‑induced catharsis.
- Only a single dose of this sorbitol‑containing product should be used when repeat-dose charcoal therapy is being administered; monitor fluid and electrolyte status closely.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Activated charcoal: acts by physical adsorption of drugs and toxic agents onto its large surface area within the gastrointestinal tract, thereby reducing systemic absorption.
- Sorbitol: acts as a cathartic (osmotic laxative) that accelerates gastrointestinal transit and counteracts charcoal‑induced constipation; decreased transit time does not appear to reduce charcoal adsorption of many toxins.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: activated charcoal and sorbitol are not systemically absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract.
- Distribution / metabolism / elimination: Not specified in available sources beyond lack of systemic absorption.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Adsorptive capacity: activated charcoal is effective for adsorption of many drugs (examples cited in source: aspirin, barbiturates, tricyclic antidepressants, digoxin, amphetamines, morphine, cocaine, digitalis, phenothiazines).
- Adsorptive capacity is insufficient for certain substances (see Section 5: Contraindications list) such as iron salts, cyanides, certain sulfonylureas, some alcohols and hydrocarbons.

### Preclinical safety data
- Genotoxicity: No data available in sources.
- Carcinogenicity: No data available in sources.
