## 5. Contraindications
List includes all contraindications reported in the available sources:
- Current thrombophlebitis or thromboembolic disorders.
- Past history of deep vein thrombophlebitis or thromboembolic disorders.
- Cerebral vascular disease or coronary artery disease (current diagnosis or history).
- Current diagnosis of, or history of, breast cancer (which may be hormonally sensitive).
- Undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding.
- Cholestatic jaundice of pregnancy or jaundice with prior pill use.
- Hepatic adenomas or hepatocellular carcinoma.
- Concurrent use of hepatitis C combination regimens containing ombitasvir/paritaprevir/ritonavir, with or without dasabuvir — due to potential for clinically significant ALT elevations. [US]
- Women over 35 years of age who smoke (use of combination oral contraceptives is contraindicated in this population due to increased risk of serious cardiovascular events).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / highlighted warning
- **WARNING: CIGARETTE SMOKING AND SERIOUS CARDIOVASCULAR EVENTS** — Cigarette smoking increases the risk of serious cardiovascular events in users of combination oral contraceptives. The risk increases with age, particularly in women over 35 years of age, and with the number of cigarettes smoked. For this reason, combination oral contraceptives, including desogestrel + ethinyl, are contraindicated in women who are over 35 years of age and smoke.
### General warnings and precautions
- Thromboembolic and thrombotic disease:
  - Use is associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (superficial venous thrombosis, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism). Epidemiologic data cited in available sources indicate increased relative risks (case‑control and cohort study findings) and a higher risk with certain third‑generation progestogens; however, absolute risks are small in healthy women without risk factors.
  - Oral contraceptives should be discontinued at least 4 weeks prior to elective surgery associated with an increased thromboembolic risk and for 2 weeks after such surgery, and should be discontinued during and following prolonged immobilization if feasible.
  - Because the immediate postpartum period is associated with increased thromboembolism risk, oral contraceptives should be started no earlier than 4 weeks after delivery in women who elect not to breastfeed.
- Cardiovascular disease:
  - Increased risks of myocardial infarction have been attributed to oral contraceptive use, especially in smokers and women with other coronary risk factors (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, obesity, diabetes).
- Cerebrovascular disease:
  - Increased relative and attributable risks of thrombotic and hemorrhagic stroke have been observed; highest risk among older (>35 years), hypertensive women who also smoke.
- Dose‑related risk:
  - There is a positive association between estrogen/progestogen amount and vascular risk. Use the lowest effective hormonal exposure compatible with contraceptive efficacy.
- Persistence of risk:
  - Some studies (with higher‑estrogen formulations) have demonstrated persistence of certain vascular risks for several years after discontinuation in specific subgroups; applicability to lower‑dose regimens is uncertain.
- Malignant neoplasms:
  - Benign hepatic adenomas have been associated with oral contraceptive use; rupture of hepatic adenomas may lead to life‑threatening intra‑abdominal hemorrhage. Long‑term use has been associated with a very small increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma in some studies; absolute risk is very low.
  - Epidemiologic studies have not consistently shown an association between combined oral contraceptives and breast cancer overall; some studies report a small increase in risk among current or recent users.
  - Some studies suggest an association between oral contraceptive use and cervical intra‑epithelial neoplasia in some populations; confounding by sexual behavior has been noted.
- Hepatic enzyme elevations:
  - Co‑administration with certain hepatitis C direct‑acting antiviral combinations (ombitasvir/paritaprevir/ritonavir ± dasabuvir) is associated with clinically significant ALT elevations; concomitant use is contraindicated [US].
- Metabolic effects:
  - Oral contraceptives can alter lipids (some progestogens may lower HDL) and carbohydrate metabolism (reports of glucose intolerance). Monitor metabolic risk factors as clinically indicated.
- Effects on sex hormone‑binding globulin and testosterone:
  - Etonogestrel is highly protein bound to SHBG and, together with ethinyl estradiol, results in increased SHBG and lower serum free testosterone compared to baseline.
### Monitoring requirements
- Baseline and periodic assessment of blood pressure is recommended (specific intervals not specified in available sources).
- Monitor for signs and symptoms of thromboembolism (e.g., unilateral leg swelling/pain, sudden chest pain/dyspnoea), stroke, hepatic dysfunction (severe abdominal pain, jaundice), and other serious adverse events.
- No formal, specific laboratory monitoring schedule is specified in available sources.
### Specific clinical precautions
- Use with caution in women with cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, obesity).
- Avoid use in women with active or past hormone‑sensitive malignancy.
- Discontinue if signs of hepatic dysfunction develop (jaundice, severe abdominal pain).
- Consider risk–benefit in older women and those with prolonged prior exposure to high‑estrogen formulations.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Ethinyl estradiol undergoes significant presystemic conjugation and hepatic metabolism; sulfate and glucuronide conjugates can undergo enterohepatic recirculation. These metabolic pathways can be the basis for drug interactions.
- Desogestrel is rapidly converted to its active metabolite etonogestrel by intestinal mucosa and first‑pass hepatic metabolism.
- Co‑administration with hepatitis C direct‑acting antiviral regimens that include ombitasvir/paritaprevir/ritonavir, with or without dasabuvir, is contraindicated due to risk of significant ALT elevations. [US]
- The available sources state that interactions between desogestrel/ethinyl estradiol and other drugs have been reported in the literature, but formal drug–drug interaction studies for this product were not conducted; no comprehensive interaction table is provided in the available sources.
### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Combination oral contraceptives may compound pre‑existing cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes), increasing thrombotic and ischemic risk.
### Clinically significant interactions and management notes
- Discontinue oral contraceptives at least 4 weeks before and for 2 weeks after elective surgery associated with increased thromboembolic risk, and during and after prolonged immobilization, if feasible.
- Concomitant use with the specified hepatitis C antivirals is contraindicated [US].
- For other potential interacting drugs (e.g., enzyme inducers or inhibitors), specific guidance is: Not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Combination oral contraceptives act primarily by suppression of gonadotropins. The principal contraceptive mechanism is inhibition of ovulation. Additional contraceptive mechanisms include changes in cervical mucus (impeding sperm penetration) and alterations of the endometrium (reducing likelihood of implantation).
- Desogestrel is a progestogen prodrug rapidly converted to its active metabolite etonogestrel, which has high progestational activity with minimal intrinsic androgenicity in preclinical studies.
### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption:
  - Desogestrel is rapidly and almost completely absorbed and converted to etonogestrel; relative bioavailability compared with a solution is approximately 100% when measured as etonogestrel serum levels.
  - Ethinyl estradiol is rapidly and almost completely absorbed. Relative bioavailability reported for the combination tablet (0.02 mg EE) is approximately 93%; for the 0.01 mg EE tablet the relative bioavailability is approximately 99%.
  - The effect of food on bioavailability was not evaluated in the available sources.
- Distribution:
  - Etonogestrel is approximately 99% protein bound, primarily to sex hormone‑binding globulin (SHBG).
  - Ethinyl estradiol is approximately 98.3% protein bound, mainly to plasma albumin; ethinyl estradiol does not bind to SHBG but induces SHBG synthesis.
- Metabolism:
  - Desogestrel is rapidly and extensively metabolized by hydroxylation in the intestinal mucosa and first‑pass hepatic metabolism to etonogestrel. Additional metabolites (e.g., 3α‑OH‑desogestrel, 3β‑OH‑desogestrel) have been identified and may undergo glucuronide and sulfate conjugation.
  - Ethinyl estradiol undergoes substantial presystemic (gut wall) conjugation (phase II). Ethinyl estradiol that escapes gut conjugation undergoes phase I oxidation and hepatic conjugation; major phase I metabolites include 2‑OH‑ethinyl estradiol and 2‑methoxy‑ethinyl estradiol. Conjugates excreted in bile may be subject to enterohepatic recirculation.
- Elimination:
  - Etonogestrel and ethinyl estradiol are excreted in urine, bile, and feces.
  - At steady state (Day 21 in the reported multiple‑dose study), elimination half‑life:
    - Etonogestrel: 27.8 ± 7.2 hours.
    - Ethinyl estradiol (0.02 mg from combination tablet): 23.9 ± 25.5 hours.
    - Ethinyl estradiol (0.01 mg tablet): 18.9 ± 8.3 hours.
- Pharmacokinetic time course:
  - Plasma concentrations of etonogestrel and ethinyl estradiol reached steady state by Day 21 during repeated dosing in the reported study; AUC for etonogestrel at steady state on Day 21 was approximately 2.2 times that on Day 1 of the third cycle in the study population.
### Pharmacodynamics
- Etonogestrel (active metabolite) exhibits high progestational activity; the clinical relevance of minimal intrinsic androgenicity observed in preclinical models is unknown.
- Combination therapy (etonogestrel + ethinyl estradiol) increases SHBG, reducing free testosterone levels.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Pharmacokinetic study:
  - Multiple‑dose pharmacokinetics were determined during the third cycle in 17 subjects. Plasma concentrations of etonogestrel and ethinyl estradiol reached steady state by Day 21. The AUC for etonogestrel at steady state was approximately 2.2 times the Day 1 AUC in the third cycle. Mean elimination half‑life values at steady state are reported in Section 11.
- Contraceptive efficacy:
  - The available sources state that oral contraceptives are highly effective for prevention of pregnancy. A population table adapted from literature (Hatcher et al.) was included in the available sources showing method‑specific typical‑use and perfect‑use pregnancy rates; for combined oral contraceptives the first‑year typical‑use pregnancy rate is cited as approximately 5% with a 1‑year continuation percentage of 71% in the provided table.
