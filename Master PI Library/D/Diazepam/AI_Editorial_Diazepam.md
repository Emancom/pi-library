# Editorial Product Information: Diazepam

## Editorial Note
- The following jurisdictions' Product Information (PI) documents contributed to this editorial: **Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)**.
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview of diazepam for healthcare professionals, synthesising clinical, regulatory, and pharmacological data.
- **Note:** This synthesis is an editorial reference and does not replace the official PI required for prescribing in any specific jurisdiction. 

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Diazepam, a classical benzodiazepine derivative.
- **Chemical Class:** Benzodiazepine.
- **Brand Names:** **Antenex** [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** N05BA01 (Not explicitly stated in source, but standard for the class).
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - **Oral Tablets:** 2 mg, 5 mg, and 10 mg [Australian].
- **Routes of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Alphapharm Pty Ltd (trading as Viatris) [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Anxiety Disorders:** Management of anxiety disorders or short-term relief of anxiety symptoms. Note: Anxiety associated with daily stress typically does not require anxiolytic treatment [Australian].
- **Acute Alcohol Withdrawal:** Symptomatic relief of acute agitation, tremor, impending or acute delirium tremens, and hallucinosis [Australian].
- **Muscle Spasm:** Adjunctive relief of reflex muscle spasm due to local pathology (inflammation, injury, trauma) [Australian].
- **Spasticity:** Management of spasticity caused by upper motor neuron lesions, including cerebral palsy, paraplegia, athetosis, and stiff-man syndrome [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adult)
- **Usual Adult Dosage:** 5 mg to 40 mg daily in divided doses [Australian].
- **Ambulatory Patients:** 2 mg three times daily, or 5 mg in the evening with 2 mg once or twice during the day [Australian].
- **Muscle Spasm:** 10 mg to 30 mg daily [Australian].
- **Hospital Treatment (Tension/Excitation):** 10 mg to 15 mg three times daily until acute symptoms subside [Australian].

### Administration Instructions
- Dosage must be highly individualised for maximum benefit.
- Prescriptions should generally be limited to short-term use (2 to 4 weeks). Continuous long-term use is not recommended due to the risk of tolerance and dependence [Australian].

### Missed Dose Instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 4. Contraindications
**Diazepam is contraindicated in patients with:**
- Known hypersensitivity to benzodiazepines or any excipients [Australian].
- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) with incipient respiratory failure [Australian].
- Severe respiratory insufficiency [Australian].
- Sleep apnoea syndrome [Australian].
- Myasthenia gravis [Australian].
- Severe hepatic impairment (risk of precipitating hepatic encephalopathy) [Australian].
- Dependence on CNS depressants (excluding acute alcohol withdrawal management) [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
### General Warnings
- **CNS Depression:** Patients must be warned against operating heavy machinery or driving until they are certain the drug does not affect them. Effects may persist the following day [Australian].
- **Tolerance and Dependence:** Use may lead to physical and psychological dependence. Risk increases with dose, duration, and in patients with a history of substance abuse [Australian].
- **Withdrawal:** Abrupt cessation can trigger withdrawal symptoms (insomnia, anxiety, headaches, and in severe cases, convulsions or psychosis). Tapering is mandatory [Australian].
- **Psychiatric Reactions:** Paradoxical reactions (agitation, rage, hallucinations) may occur, particularly in children and the elderly. Treatment should be discontinued if these occur [Australian].
- **Suicide Risk:** Should not be used as monotherapy for depression or anxiety associated with depression, as it may precipitate suicide [Australian].

### Specific Clinical Precautions
- **Opioid Concomitant Use:** Concomitant use with opioids may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death. Limit to the minimum effective dose and duration [Australian].
- **Medical History:** Extreme caution is required in patients with a history of alcohol or drug abuse [Australian].
- **Lactose Intolerance:** Tablets containing lactose should be avoided in patients with rare hereditary galactose intolerance or glucose-galactose malabsorption [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic Interactions
- **CYP3A4 and CYP2C19 Inhibitors:** Drugs that inhibit these enzymes increase diazepam plasma concentrations, potentially leading to excessive sedation. Examples include:
    - **Grapefruit juice:** Increases AUC 3.2-fold [Australian].
    - **Azole antifungals:** Fluconazole, voriconazole, and ketoconazole [Australian].
    - **Proton Pump Inhibitors:** Omeprazole and esomeprazole (but not lansoprazole or pantoprazole) [Australian].
    - **SSRIs:** Fluvoxamine (3-fold AUC increase) and fluoxetine [Australian].
    - **Cimetidine:** Reduces clearance by 40-50% [Australian].
- **Enzyme Inducers:** Rifampicin (decreases AUC by 77%) and carbamazepine accelerate diazepam clearance, reducing efficacy [Australian].

### Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- **CNS Depressants:** Enhanced sedative and respiratory depressant effects when combined with alcohol, other benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, antidepressants, hypnotics, anticonvulsants, and anaesthetics [Australian].
- **Opioids:** Increased risk of fatal respiratory depression [Australian].
- **Methadone:** May enhance euphoria and increase the risk of abuse [Australian].
- **Levodopa:** Reversible loss of control of Parkinson's disease has been reported [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** **Category C** [Australian]. Diazepam crosses the placenta. Use in the first trimester may increase the risk of congenital malformations. High doses during labour can cause "floppy infant syndrome" (hypotonia, poor sucking, respiratory depression, and hypothermia) [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Not recommended. Diazepam and its metabolites are excreted in breast milk (approx. 10% of maternal blood concentration) and may cause drowsiness and feeding difficulties in the infant [Australian].
- **Fertility:** Animal studies show decreased pregnancies at high doses (22-fold MRHD). No adverse effects at twice the MRHD [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Oral use is not recommended in infants under 6 months. Children 6 months to 14 years require weight-based or age-specific dosing (0.1 to 0.3 mg/kg) and careful clinical assessment [Australian].
- **Elderly/Debilitated:** Increased sensitivity to sedative effects; higher risk of ataxia and confusion. Initiate with 2 mg twice daily or half the usual adult dose to prevent accumulation [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Contraindicated in severe cases. In mild to moderate cases, use the lowest possible dose and monitor liver function tests regularly [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Initiate with very low doses and monitor closely; elimination half-life may be shortened in renal failure, but accumulation of metabolites may occur [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of Action
Diazepam is a positive allosteric modulator of the GABA-A receptor. It enhances the inhibitory effects of GABA by increasing transmembrane chloride ion flux, resulting in anxiolytic, sedative, muscle relaxant, and anticonvulsant properties [Australian].

### Pharmacokinetics
- **Absorption:** Rapid and complete; peak plasma levels in 30–90 minutes [Australian].
- **Distribution:** Highly lipid-soluble; crosses the blood-brain barrier and placenta. 98–99% protein bound [Australian].
- **Metabolism:** Hepatic via CYP3A4 and CYP2C19. Primary active metabolite is desmethyldiazepam (long-acting), with minor active metabolites temazepam and oxazepam [Australian].
- **Elimination:** Biphasic. Diazepam half-life is 24–48 hours; desmethyldiazepam half-life is 40–100 hours [Australian].
