# Editorial Product Information: delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol + cannabidiol

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol + cannabidiol**
- Chemical class: Cannabinoids (mixed delta-9-THC and cannabidiol botanical extracts)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oromucosal (oral mucosal spray)
- Note: Do not include brand names or formulation/container details in this document.

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources.
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources.
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Symptomatic improvement of moderate to severe spasticity due to multiple sclerosis (MS) in patients who have not responded adequately to other anti‑spasticity medications and who demonstrate clinically significant improvement during an initial trial of therapy.
- Where indication approvals differ by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources beyond the MS spasticity indication.

## 4. Dosage and Administration

General dosing (adult)
- Concentration: Each mL of product contains approximately 27 mg delta‑9‑tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and 25 mg cannabidiol (CBD). Each 100 microlitre actuation (spray) contains approximately 2.7 mg THC and 2.5 mg CBD.
- Maximum daily dose: Do not exceed 12 sprays in any 24‑hour period.
- Maximum consecutive sprays: No more than 7 sprays within any 3‑hour period.
- Median dose observed in clinical trials: 8 sprays per day.

Dosing by indication
- MS spasticity: Individual titration to optimal symptom relief (see titration schedule). Maintain the optimum dose achieved. Re‑titrate if clinical condition or concomitant medications change, or if tolerability issues arise.

Titration schedule (example titration used in clinical experience)
- A gradual upward titration over up to 2 weeks is recommended to reach the optimal dose. The following pattern illustrates a typical stepwise increase used in clinical trials; individualised titration is required and increases may continue by one spray per day up to the 12‑spray/day maximum:
  - Day 1: evening 1 spray (total 1)
  - Day 2: evening 1 spray (total 1)
  - Day 3: evening 2 sprays (total 2)
  - Day 4: evening 2 sprays (total 2)
  - Day 5: morning 1 / evening 2 (total 3)
  - Day 6: morning 1 / evening 3 (total 4)
  - Day 7: morning 1 / evening 4 (total 5)
  - Day 8: morning 2 / evening 4 (total 6)
  - Day 9: morning 2 / evening 5 (total 7)
  - Day 10: morning 3 / evening 5 (total 8)
  - Day 11: morning 3 / evening 6 (total 9)
  - Day 12: morning 4 / evening 6 (total 10)
  - Day 13: morning 4 / evening 7 (total 11)
  - Day 14: morning 5 / evening 7 (total 12)
- Allow at least 15 minutes between individual sprays.
- The afternoon/evening dose may be taken between 16:00 and bedtime; the morning dose may be taken between waking and midday. Once optimal dose established, sprays may be spread across the day per individual response.

Administration instructions
- Intended for oromucosal use only.
- Prime the spray prior to first use and if not used for more than 21 days: shake gently, remove protective cap, hold upright, actuate 2–3 times into tissue until a fine spray appears.
- Shake before use; direct spray to varying sites on the oromucosal surface and change application site each time to minimise local irritation.
- Do not mix with other medicinal products (no compatibility studies available).

Missed dose
- Not specified in available sources.

Trial and review
- A supervised initial trial is required. Reassess response at approximately 4 weeks. If clinically significant improvement in spasticity‑related symptoms is not achieved during the trial, discontinue therapy. In clinical trials, clinically significant improvement was defined as ≥20% improvement on a 0–10 patient‑reported numeric rating scale.

Special populations dosing notes
- Children/adolescents (<18 years): Not recommended due to lack of safety and efficacy data.
- Elderly: No specific dose adjustment specified; caution advised due to increased susceptibility to CNS adverse reactions.
- Hepatic impairment: Can be administered to patients with mild hepatic impairment without dose adjustment; use cautiously in moderate and severe hepatic impairment and monitor frequently.
- Renal impairment: No specific studies; effects may be exaggerated or prolonged — frequent clinical evaluation recommended.

## 5. Contraindications
- Hypersensitivity to cannabinoids or to any excipients contained in the product.
- Known or suspected personal or family history of schizophrenia or other psychotic illness.
- History of severe personality disorder or other significant psychiatric disorder (other than depression related to the underlying condition).
- Breastfeeding (contraindicated due to substantial concentration of cannabinoids in milk and potential adverse developmental effects in infants).
- Other contraindications specific to other jurisdictions: Not specified in available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

Boxed/critical warnings
- **Do not exceed the maximum recommended dose. Higher doses increase the risk of serious psychiatric adverse events, including psychosis, hallucinations, delusions, and suicidal or homicidal ideation.**

General warnings and precautions
- Individual responses vary widely; specialist assessment (e.g., neurologist or rehabilitation physician experienced in spasticity management) is recommended prior to initiation and for reassessment after approximately 4 weeks.
- Psychiatric adverse events (see Section 7) are more frequent than with placebo. Patients with a personal or family history of psychotic illness must not receive therapy. Monitor closely patients with a history of depression; discontinue if clinically significant worsening occurs.
- Suicidal ideation has occurred; if suspected, stop treatment immediately and monitor until resolution.
- Caution in patients with epilepsy or recurrent seizures until further information available.
- Caution in patients with serious cardiovascular disease; alterations in pulse rate and blood pressure have been observed with initial dosing; fainting episodes have been reported.
- There is an increased risk of falls in patients whose spasticity is reduced but who lack adequate muscle strength to maintain posture or gait.
- Additive sedative and muscle‑relaxant effects may occur with concomitant use of benzodiazepines, baclofen, hypnotics, sedatives, alcohol or other agents with sedating properties; counsel patients regarding impaired coordination and increased fall risk.
- The product contains ethanol (approximately 50% v/v); each actuation contains up to 0.04 g ethanol; cumulative exposure at typical doses is small but may be harmful in severe alcohol use disorder.
- Regular inspection of oral mucosa is recommended for long‑term use because stinging, ulceration and other local oral effects have been reported. Interrupt therapy if lesions or persistent soreness develop until fully resolved.
- Withdrawal: abrupt cessation of long‑term treatment has not produced a consistent withdrawal syndrome; some patients report transient disturbances of sleep, emotion or appetite.
- Women of childbearing potential: may reduce effectiveness of systemic hormonal contraceptives; advise additional non‑hormonal reliable barrier contraception during therapy and for three months after discontinuation.
- Laboratory tests: No clinically relevant effects on routine laboratory tests were observed in clinical trials.

Monitoring requirements
- Baseline psychiatric history and ongoing psychiatric monitoring during therapy.
- Monitor blood pressure and pulse during initiation and titration.
- Reassess spasticity severity and response after ~4 weeks (discontinue if inadequate response).
- Frequent clinical evaluation recommended in patients with moderate–severe hepatic impairment or significant renal impairment.
- Monitor oral mucosa periodically during long‑term use.

Specific clinical precautions
- Avoid driving, operating machinery, or performing hazardous tasks until response to therapy and individual susceptibility to CNS effects are known.
- Counsel patients to avoid alcohol while beginning therapy or during dose changes due to additive CNS effects.

Serious adverse effects highlighted
- Severe psychiatric events including psychosis and suicidal ideation (serious). Serious psychiatric events were observed, particularly at higher-than‑recommended doses (e.g., transient psychosis in healthy volunteers at 18 actuations twice daily).
- Syncope/fainting episodes.
- Increased fall risk leading to possible injury.

Additional notes
- In clinical trials elderly patients (>65 years) were approximately three times more likely to experience CNS adverse events on active treatment than on placebo; more than one third of elderly subjects on active treatment experienced a CNS adverse event.
- Dependence: No consistent pattern of withdrawal after abrupt cessation; dependence considered unlikely based on clinical trial experience (no dose escalation and low self‑reported intoxication).

## 8. Drug Interactions

Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Metabolism: THC and CBD are metabolised in the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes; approximately one‑third of parent drugs and metabolites are excreted in urine, remainder in faeces. Several THC metabolites may be psychoactive.
- Food effects: Co‑administration with food increases systemic exposure:
  - THC: mean Cmax 1.6‑fold and AUC 2.8‑fold higher with food versus fasting.
  - CBD: mean Cmax 3.3‑fold and AUC 5.1‑fold higher with food versus fasting.
- CYP inhibition/induction (in vitro and in vivo observations):
  - In vitro reversible inhibition reported for CYP3A4, CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9 and CYP2C19 at high concentrations; time‑dependent inhibition of CYP3A4 at clinically relevant concentrations is reported.
  - In vitro induction at mRNA level of CYP1A2, CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 could occur at clinically relevant concentrations.
  - Practical implications: Co‑administration with sensitive substrates of these enzymes may result in increased or reduced plasma concentrations of concomitant drugs; review dosing regimens when initiating or stopping co‑therapy.
- Specific interaction studies:
  - Concomitant ketoconazole (CYP3A4 inhibitor): increased THC Cmax 1.2‑fold and AUC 1.8‑fold; 11‑OH‑THC Cmax and AUC increased ~3–3.6‑fold; CBD Cmax and AUC increased ~2‑fold. If strong CYP3A4 inhibitors are started or stopped, consider re‑titration.
  - Concomitant rifampicin (strong CYP3A4 inducer): reduced THC Cmax and AUC by ~40% and 20% respectively; 11‑OH‑THC Cmax and AUC reduced by ~85% and 87% respectively; CBD Cmax and AUC reduced by ~50% and 60% respectively. If strong inducers are started or stopped, consider re‑titration.
  - Concomitant omeprazole (CYP2C19 inhibitor): no notable change in pharmacokinetic parameters observed.
- UGT enzymes: In vitro inhibition of UGT1A9 and UGT2B7 at clinically relevant concentrations reported; use caution with concomitant medications primarily metabolised by these UGTs (e.g., propofol, certain antivirals). Patients with glucuronidation disorders (e.g., Gilbert's) may have increased bilirubin and require caution.
- P‑glycoprotein / transporters: In vitro data do not indicate marked effects on a range of ABC transporters at clinically relevant concentrations; an intestinal P‑gp inhibition by CBD cannot be excluded—exercise caution with P‑gp substrates such as digoxin.
- Alcohol: Concomitant alcohol may increase CNS effects (impaired coordination, concentration) and risk of falls; co‑use should be avoided particularly during initiation or dose changes.

Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Additive sedation and muscle‑relaxant effects possible with benzodiazepines, baclofen, hypnotics, sedatives and alcohol. Monitor and adjust concomitant sedating medications as appropriate.
- Potential additive effect with other muscle relaxants may increase fall risk.

Clinically significant interactions and management
- Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., itraconazole, ritonavir, clarithromycin): may increase exposure — consider dose re‑titration and monitoring.
- Strong CYP3A4 inducers (e.g., rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, St John's Wort): may reduce exposure — consider dose re‑titration and monitoring.
- Hormonal contraceptives: Enzyme induction potential may reduce effectiveness of systemically acting hormonal contraceptives — recommend additional barrier contraception during therapy and for three months after discontinuation.
- Digoxin and other P‑gp substrates: use caution; monitor drug levels or effects where appropriate.
- Propofol and certain antivirals (UGT substrates): exercise caution due to potential UGT inhibition.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category: [Australian] Category B2.
- Clinical recommendation: Should not be used during pregnancy unless potential benefits outweigh potential risks to the fetus/embryo.
- Animal data: No evidence of teratogenicity in rats and rabbits at tested doses, but maternal toxicity and reduced fetal weights/ossification variants were observed in rabbits at high doses. In rats, perinatal exposure produced reduced pup body weight gain and slight impairment of reflex (NOEL identified in animal studies). Clinical significance for humans is uncertain.

Breastfeeding
- High concentrations of THC and CBD are present in milk of lactating animals after dosing; cannabinoids are lipophilic and concentrate in breast milk.
- Due to potential adverse developmental effects in infants and likely significant cannabinoid transfer into milk, breastfeeding is contraindicated.

Fertility
- Animal fertility studies: Fertility in rats was unaffected at oral doses up to 12.5 mg/kg/day of each active component; some inconsistent findings in male reproductive parameters in other animal studies at high doses with uncertain clinical significance.

## 10. Use in Special Populations

Paediatric use
- Not recommended for use in children and adolescents under 18 years due to lack of safety and efficacy data.

Elderly use
- No specific dose adjustment established. Elderly patients may be more susceptible to CNS adverse reactions (e.g., dizziness, somnolence); caution advised with regard to personal safety (hot food/drinks, falls).
- Clinical trial experience: subjects >65 years experienced CNS adverse events more frequently on active therapy compared with placebo; over one third of elderly subjects on active treatment had a CNS adverse event.

Renal impairment
- No formal pharmacokinetic studies in severe renal impairment. THC and CBD and metabolites are partially renally excreted (~one‑third). Effects may be exaggerated or prolonged in significant renal impairment; frequent clinical evaluation recommended.

Hepatic impairment
- THC and CBD are hepatically metabolised. A single oromucosal dose (4 sprays; ~10.8 mg THC/10 mg CBD) was tolerated in subjects with hepatic impairment in a specific study.
- Dosing guidance: can be administered to patients with mild hepatic impairment without dose adjustment; use cautiously in moderate and severe hepatic impairment and monitor frequently.

## 11. Pharmacology

Mechanism of action
- Not specified in available sources.

Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Oromucosal administration produces systemic exposure to THC and CBD; co‑administration with food increases systemic exposure (THC and CBD Cmax and AUC increases as noted in Section 8).
- Distribution: Lipophilic compounds that concentrate in fatty tissues and breast milk; specific volume of distribution not specified in available sources.
- Metabolism: Metabolised primarily by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes; active metabolites (including 11‑OH‑THC) may have psychoactive properties. Possible involvement of UGT pathways for glucuronidation.
- Elimination: Approximately one‑third of parent drugs and metabolites excreted in urine; remaining excreted in faeces.
- Special populations: Pharmacokinetic alterations may occur in hepatic or renal impairment; data limited.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Initial response assessment: Clinical trial protocols included an initial supervised trial with reassessment after approximately 4 weeks. Clinically significant response in trials was defined as ≥20% improvement in spasticity‑related symptoms on a 0–10 patient‑reported numeric rating scale.
- Dose findings: Median dose in clinical trials for patients with multiple sclerosis was 8 sprays/day; most patients respond at doses up to and including 12 sprays/day.
- Safety signals in trials:
  - Psychiatric adverse events were more frequent on active treatment than placebo (17.6% vs 7.8% overall).
  - Specific adverse event rates in trials included disorientation 4.1% (active vs 0.8% placebo), depression 2.9% (active vs 2.0% placebo), euphoric mood 2.2% (active vs 0.9% placebo), dissociation 1.7% (active vs 0.1% placebo).
  - Elderly participants (>65 years) had higher rates of CNS adverse events on active therapy compared with placebo.
- Volunteer safety data: High dosing in healthy volunteers (e.g., 18 actuations twice daily) produced serious psychiatric adverse events in some subjects (transient psychosis in 4 of 41 volunteers), underscoring the importance of not exceeding recommended doses.
- Long‑term use: Abrupt withdrawal after long‑term administration has not produced a consistent withdrawal syndrome; transient disturbances (sleep, emotion, appetite) were reported in some patients. No consistent pattern of dose escalation during long term use was observed.
