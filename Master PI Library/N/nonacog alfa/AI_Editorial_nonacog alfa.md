# Editorial Product Information: nonacog alfa

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **nonacog alfa**
- Chemical class: Recombinant coagulation factor IX produced in a Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell line
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous (IV) infusion
- Note: The product is recombinant and is not derived from human blood; it contains no added human or animal plasma-derived components. The potency is measured in international units (IU) against the WHO International Standard for factor IX concentrate. Specific activity is ≥ 200 IU per mg protein. Polysorbate-80 is present in the reconstituted solution.

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of source PIs: Not specified in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Control and prevention of hemorrhagic episodes in patients with congenital haemophilia B (factor IX deficiency), including control and prevention of bleeding in surgical settings.
- Not indicated for:
  - Treatment of deficiencies of coagulation factors other than factor IX (e.g. factors II, VII and X).
  - Treatment of haemophilia A patients with inhibitors to factor VIII.
  - Reversal of coumarin-induced anticoagulation.
  - Treatment of bleeding due to low levels of liver-dependent coagulation factors.

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General principles
- Treatment should be supervised by a physician experienced in haemophilia management.
- Dosing must be individualized based on severity of factor IX deficiency, site and extent of bleeding, clinical condition, age, and measured pharmacokinetic parameters (recovery, half-life).
- Monitoring of factor IX activity is recommended to ensure achievement of target levels, particularly for surgical procedures.
- The preparation is for single-patient use only.

### Method for calculating dose
- Number of factor IX IU required (IU) = Body weight (kg) × Desired factor IX increase (% or IU/dL) × Reciprocal of observed recovery (IU/kg per IU/dL)
- Observed recovery estimates from available data:
  - Patients ≥ 15 years: mean recovery ≈ 0.8 IU/dL per IU/kg (SD ±0.2; range 0.4–1.4). Use reciprocal ≈ 1.2 IU/kg per IU/dL.
  - Patients < 15 years: mean recovery ≈ 0.7 IU/dL per IU/kg (SD ±0.3; range 0.2–2.1). Use reciprocal ≈ 1.4 IU/kg per IU/dL.
- In practice, doses should be titrated to measured factor IX activity. Some patients (particularly those with low recovery) may require up to 2× the empirically calculated dose.

### General dosing (examples and schedules)
- On-demand treatment (adults / general): initial empiric doses commonly used and subsequently titrated. An initial regimen for routine use or prophylaxis may be approximately 20–50 IU/kg.
- Maintenance prophylaxis: may be given on a regular schedule (commonly 2–3 times per week). In one study of routine secondary prophylaxis, the mean dose was 40 IU/kg twice weekly (range 13–78 IU/kg).
- Inhibitor presence: higher doses may be required; bleeding control may be impractical with high-titre inhibitors (typically >5 Bethesda Units).

### Dosing guide for bleeding episodes and surgery
- Goal is to keep circulating factor IX activity (IU/dL or %) at or above the stated targets for the indicated time period. Typical dosing interval 12–24 hours; adjust according to monitoring.

| Type of haemorrhage | Target circulating factor IX activity (%) | Dosing interval (hours) | Typical duration (days) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Minor (e.g., uncomplicated haemarthrosis, superficial soft tissue) | 20–30 | 12–24 | 1–2 |
| Moderate (e.g., intramuscular with compartment risk, mucous membrane bleeding, dental extraction, haematuria) | 25–50 | 12–24 | Until bleeding stops and healing begins (≈2–7) |
| Major (e.g., pharynx, retropharynx, retroperitoneum, central nervous system bleeds, major surgery) | 50–100 | 12–24 | 7–10 |

### Administration instructions
- Reconstitution: reconstitute with the supplied diluent (0.234% sodium chloride solution) and gently rotate vial until powder is dissolved. After reconstitution, withdraw solution into syringe; solution should be clear and colourless. Discard if particulate matter or discoloration present.
- Use the reconstituted solution as soon as possible, and within 3 hours of reconstitution if not used immediately.
- Administration: intravenous injection over several minutes; infusion rate should be guided by patient comfort. Use appropriate sterile infusion set and vial adapter.
- Special handling notes:
  - Polysorbate-80 in the reconstituted product may increase extraction of DEHP from PVC devices; consider this during preparation and administration.
  - Agglutination of red blood cells in tubing or syringe has been observed; limit blood entering tubing and avoid blood in syringes. If agglutination occurs, discard tubing/syringe and product and resume with new set.
  - Continuous infusion: safety and efficacy have not been established; post-marketing reports of thrombotic events have occurred with continuous infusion, particularly in critically ill neonates.

### Titration and monitoring
- Perform factor IX activity assays and calculate pharmacokinetic parameters (recovery, half-life) as clinically indicated to adjust dose and frequency.
- For surgical and major bleeding, measure factor IX levels to maintain target activity.

### Missed dose
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to nonacog alfa or to any constituent of the formulation.
- Known hypersensitivity to hamster proteins (product produced in Chinese hamster ovary cell line) — may be contraindicated in patients with prior allergic reaction to hamster protein.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed / Prominent warnings
- Not specified as a boxed warning in available sources. The following are critical safety precautions:

### General warnings and precautions
- **Thrombosis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC):** Although recombinant factor IX contains only factor IX, thromboembolic events and DIC have been reported historically with factor IX–containing complex products; exercise caution in patients with liver disease, postoperative patients, neonates, patients with fibrinolysis, patients at risk for thromboembolism, and patients with DIC.
- **Continuous infusion:** Safety and efficacy of continuous infusion have not been established. Post-marketing reports include thrombotic events (including life‑threatening superior vena cava syndrome in critically ill neonates) associated with continuous infusion via central catheters.
- **Inhibitor development:** Neutralizing antibodies (inhibitors) to factor IX can develop. Monitor patients for inhibitor development. Patients with inhibitors may be at increased risk for allergic/anaphylactic reactions upon re-exposure.
  - High-titre inhibitors (>5 Bethesda Units) often make control of bleeding with factor IX impractical; consider bypassing agents (activated prothrombin complex concentrate or activated factor VII) under specialist guidance.
  - Inhibitors of low titre (<5 BU): product may be considered if clinical response (rise in factor IX activity) is observed and treatment occurs in a controlled medical setting with capacity to manage anaphylaxis.
  - Patients with major deletions in the factor IX gene may have increased risk of inhibitor formation and acute hypersensitivity reactions; such patients should be closely monitored, especially during initial exposures.
  - Immune tolerance induction with factor IX products has been associated with nephrotic syndrome in patients with haemophilia B and inhibitors; safety and efficacy of immune tolerance induction with this product are not established.
- **Hypersensitivity / anaphylaxis:** Allergic reactions including anaphylaxis have been reported. Initial administrations (~first 10–20 infusions) should be observed in a setting with facilities for emergency treatment of hypersensitivity. If an allergic reaction occurs, stop or reduce infusion rate and evaluate for inhibitors.
- **Pediatrics:** Safety and efficacy have been demonstrated in previously treated and previously untreated children; however, monitor for inhibitors and hypersensitivity signs.
- **Elderly:** Clinical trials contained insufficient numbers of subjects ≥65 years to determine if they respond differently; individualize dosing.
- **Laboratory effects:** Temporary correction of prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT/ PTT) may occur; no effect on normal prothrombin time was observed in available measurements. Limited data showed no significant increase in coagulation activation markers in small samples.

### Monitoring requirements
- Baseline and periodic factor IX activity assays for dose titration and to verify achievement of therapeutic targets.
- Monitor for signs of thrombosis and DIC in at-risk patients or suspected overdose.
- Monitor for development of inhibitors (clinical and laboratory assessment).
- Observe for hypersensitivity signs during and after infusion, particularly during initial exposures.

## 10. Use in Special Populations

### Paediatric use
- Safety and efficacy demonstrated in both previously treated and previously untreated children in available clinical data.
- Pharmacokinetic recovery is age-dependent: children <15 years had mean recovery ≈ 0.7 IU/dL per IU/kg (reciprocal ≈ 1.4 IU/kg per IU/dL) with wider variability (range 0.2–2.1).
- Younger patients may require shorter dosing intervals or higher doses.

### Elderly
- Clinical studies did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged ≥65 to determine different responses; dose individualization recommended.

## 11. Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action
- Factor IX is a vitamin K–dependent coagulation protein that is activated by the factor VII/tissue factor complex in the extrinsic coagulation pathway; activated factor IX participates in the intrinsic pathway leading to thrombin generation and clot formation. ### Pharmacokinetics
- Potency is expressed in international units (IU) against WHO standard.
- Specific activity ≥ 200 IU/mg of protein.
- In clinical pharmacokinetic evaluations:
  - Recovery (increment in circulating factor IX activity per IU/kg administered):
    - Patients ≥15 years: mean ≈ 0.8 IU/dL per IU/kg (SD ±0.2; range 0.4–1.4).
    - Patients <15 years: mean ≈ 0.7 IU/dL per IU/kg (SD ±0.3; range 0.2–2.1).
  - Recovery with nonacog alfa was observed to be lower than that seen with a high-purity plasma-derived factor IX product in a small crossover study.
- Other detailed pharmacokinetic parameters (e.g., half-life, volume of distribution, clearance) are not specified in available sources.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Temporary correction of prolonged partial thromboplastin time (PTT) is observed after administration.
- No effect on normal prothrombin time reported in available measurements.
- Limited assessments of coagulation activation markers did not show significant increases in small patient samples.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Pharmacokinetic crossover study: An 11-patient randomized crossover comparison versus a high-purity plasma-derived factor IX showed lower in vivo recovery for nonacog alfa.
- Clinical efficacy studies: In clinical trials, patients switched from plasma-derived products initially received the same doses previously used with plasma-derived factor IX; approximately half required dose increases to achieve therapeutic response even without inhibitors. Dose titration using factor IX activity assays was emphasized.
- Previously treated patient data: In one study of patients previously treated with plasma-derived products, 1 of 65 developed a low-titre, transient inhibitor (maximum 1.5 BU), who continued treatment without anamnestic rise or anaphylaxis; recovery and half-life returned to baseline.
- Previously untreated patients: In a study of previously untreated patients (n=63), two patients developed high-titre inhibitors after 7 and 15 exposure days; both had allergic manifestations temporally associated with inhibitor development.
- Secondary prophylaxis study: In 19 previously treated patients (mean age 27.5 years; range 4–56) with moderate to severe haemophilia B, routine secondary prophylaxis averaged 40 IU/kg twice weekly for an average of 18 months; three patients had no haemorrhages during that period and the other 16 averaged 13 haemorrhages each.
- Safety signals from post-marketing surveillance include thrombotic events (including severe cases associated with continuous infusion in neonates) and renal infarct in an isolated case; causal relationships are uncertain.
