# Editorial Product Information: non-standardized allergenic extracts

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **non-standardized allergenic extracts**
- Chemical class: Not specified in available sources.
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources.
- Route(s) of administration: percutaneous (prick/puncture), intradermal, subcutaneous.

## 2. Regulatory History
- Initial U.S. approval/registration: 1968.
- Most recent documented PI revision note in available sources: January 2023 (major change to Warnings and Precautions section regarding anaphylaxis following false negative food allergen skin tests).
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources.
- Other jurisdictional registration dates or last-update dates: Not specified in available sources.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Skin test diagnosis of patients with a clinical history of allergy to one or more specific non-standardized allergens.
- Allergen immunotherapy for reduction of allergen-induced allergic symptoms when clinical allergy is confirmed by appropriate positive skin test or by in vitro testing for allergen-specific IgE antibodies.
- Note: Food extracts have not been proven safe or effective for allergen immunotherapy (indication/limitation explicitly stated).

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General principles
- For percutaneous (prick/puncture), intradermal, or subcutaneous administration only.
- Stock concentrates are provided as aqueous stock concentrates (up to 1:10 w/v or up to 40,000 PNU/mL) and 50% glycerin stock concentrates (up to 1:20 w/v). There is no direct potency correlation between weight/volume and PNU across products.
- Inspect parenteral preparations visually for particulate matter or discoloration before use; discard if present.
- Use sterile diluents as specified for the intended test or immunotherapy procedure (glycerin-free diluents for intradermal testing; glycerin-free or 10% glycerin-saline diluents for immunotherapy; 50% glycerin diluent for percutaneous testing when indicated).

### Preparation: dilution series
- 10-fold dilution series (typical method): 0.5 mL of concentrate + 4.5 mL sterile diluent to produce successive 10-fold dilutions. Example relative strengths (illustrative series from cited tables):
  - Concentrate (example reference): 1:10 w/v or 1:20 w/v — example PNU/mL 20,000
  - 1: 1:100 (1:200) — example PNU/mL 2,000
  - 2: 1:1,000 (1:2,000) — example PNU/mL 200
  - 3: 1:10,000 (1:20,000) — example PNU/mL 20
  - 4: 1:100,000 (1:200,000) — example PNU/mL 2
  - 5: 1:1,000,000 (1:2,000,000) — example PNU/mL 0.2
  - 6: 1:10,000,000 (1:20,000,000) — example PNU/mL 0.02
  - Note: the example PNU values are derived from the illustrative table; individual product concentrates may differ and there is no direct cross-correlation between w/v and PNU across products.

- 5-fold dilution series (alternative method): 1 mL of concentrate + 4 mL sterile diluent to produce successive 5-fold dilutions. Example relative strengths:
  - Concentrate (example reference): 1:10 or 1:20 w/v — example PNU/mL 20,000
  - 1: 1:50 (1:100) — example PNU/mL 4,000
  - 2: 1:250 (1:500) — example PNU/mL 800
  - 3: 1:1,250 (1:2,500) — example PNU/mL 160
  - 4: 1:6,250 (1:12,500) — example PNU/mL 32
  - 5: 1:31,250 (1:62,500) — example PNU/mL 6.4
  - 6: 1:156,250 (1:312,500) — example PNU/mL 1.28
  - Note: there is no direct potency correlation across the table between PNU/mL and w/v.

### Diagnostic testing

Percutaneous (prick/puncture) testing
- Use glycerinated extract at the highest available stock concentration for routine prick testing.
- In patients suspected to be at greater risk for systemic reaction, use diluted preparations (10-fold or 5-fold dilutions) starting from the glycerinated stock concentrate.
- Technique: place one drop of extract plus appropriate controls on the skin and pierce through the drop using the chosen device per manufacturer technique. Read and record responses at 15–20 minutes.
- Controls: positive control commonly a glycerinated histamine solution (e.g., 6 mg/mL or 1 mg/mL histamine base); negative control commonly 50% glycerin-saline. Positive control wheal should be ≥3 mm larger than negative control. Negative control should be small or absent (wheal <2 mm, erythema <5 mm).
- Record both wheal and flare; measure largest diameter and orthogonal width and calculate average diameter as described in typical skin-testing methodology.

Intradermal testing
- For intradermal testing dilute stock concentrate to approximately 1:100 to 1:1,000 v/v for initial testing (use normal or buffered saline or saline with human serum albumin [HSA] as diluent).
- If initial intradermal dose is negative, progressively stronger dilutions may be used up to maximum recommended strength of 1:25 v/v of the extract concentrate solution.
- Inject 0.02 mL intradermally per test site.
- Apply dilution algorithms conservatively and interpret results in the context of clinical history and controls.

### Immunotherapy (subcutaneous)
- Administer by subcutaneous injection in the lateral upper arm or thigh; avoid intravenous administration.
- Stock concentrates for immunotherapy may be aqueous or 50% glycerin; dilute in normal saline, buffered saline, HSA-saline, or 10% glycerin-saline depending on patient tolerability.
- Typical schedule:
  - Build-up: injections usually given 1–2 times per week until maintenance achieved.
  - Maintenance: interval progressively increased (e.g., 2 weeks, then 3 weeks, then 4 weeks).
  - Minimum recommended course: commonly 3–5 years depending on clinical response.
- Initial dose guidance:
  - Patients at greater risk for systemic reaction: start at 0.05 mL of a 1:20,000,000 to 1:2,000,000 w/v dilution.
  - Patients not suspected to be at greater risk: may start at 0.1 mL of a 1:200,000 to 1:20,000 w/v dilution.
- Dose escalation:
  - Increase each injection by no more than 50% of the previous dose.
  - Select the maximum tolerated maintenance dose based on clinical response and tolerability.
  - Doses larger than 0.2 mL of a stock concentrate are rarely given because 50% glycerin diluents can cause injection discomfort.
- Dose modification guidelines:
  - Withhold or reduce dose for symptomatic rhinitis/asthma exacerbation, infection with fever, recent excessive environmental allergen exposure, large local reactions persisting >24 hours (repeat previous dose or reduce next dose), or any systemic reaction.
  - Reduce subsequent dose by at least 75% after any systemic reaction. Reduce next dose by at least 50% after local adverse reactions; proceed cautiously thereafter.
  - Repeated systemic adverse reactions are grounds to stop attempts to increase dose.
- Changing extract lot or manufacturer:
  - Because manufacturing processes and raw material sources differ, interchangeability is not assured.
  - When switching to a different lot or manufacturer reduce starting dose (generally 50–75% of previous dose), evaluate patient history and prior tolerance, and rebuild dose with intervals not exceeding one week.
- Unscheduled gaps:
  - Patients may lose tolerance during prolonged intervals between injections; reduce or repeat dose when gaps occur based on prior concentration, history of systemic reactions, and the duration of the gap. The suggested approach is not based on controlled evidence and should be individualized.
- Changing diluent stabilizer (e.g., to HSA-stabilized diluent):
  - Consider lowering dose when switching from non-stabilized diluent to HSA-stabilized diluent because of differences in stability.

### Missed dose instructions
- Specific stepwise missed-dose algorithms beyond the general guidance on unscheduled gaps are not specified in available sources; individual physicians should use an appropriate protocol based on concentration, prior reactions, and time since last dose.

## 5. Contraindications
- Severe, unstable, or uncontrolled asthma.
- History of any severe systemic or severe local allergic reaction to an allergen extract.
- Any additional jurisdiction-specific contraindications: Not specified in available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed/Highlighted warning
- **Severe allergic reactions (including anaphylaxis)**: Non-standardized allergenic extracts can cause life-threatening systemic reactions, including fatal anaphylaxis. Do not administer to patients with severe, unstable, or uncontrolled asthma. Observe patients in the office for at least 30 minutes after administration. Emergency treatments and personnel trained in their use must be immediately available.

### General warnings and precautions
- Patients at increased risk for severe systemic reactions include those with extreme sensitivity to the allergen, those on accelerated ("rush") build-up schedules, those switching extract lots or manufacturers, those receiving high doses, and those with recent or concurrent exposure to similar environmental allergens.
- High-risk patients have experienced fatal reactions; consider using more dilute preparations in patients suspected to be at greater risk.
- Underlying medical conditions that may reduce the ability to survive a systemic allergic reaction (e.g., markedly compromised lung function, unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction, significant arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension) increase risk and may make these products unsuitable.
- Medications that can reduce responsiveness to epinephrine (e.g., beta-adrenergic blockers) or otherwise interact with epinephrine (e.g., alpha-adrenergic blockers, ergot alkaloids) may affect management of anaphylaxis and must be considered before administration.
- Anaphylaxis following false negative food allergen skin test results has been identified as a safety concern (recently updated warning topic). Clinicians should interpret negative food skin tests in the clinical context and consider additional diagnostic methods when indicated.

### Monitoring requirements
- Observe patients for at least 30 minutes after testing or injection for immediate systemic reactions.
- Ensure availability of emergency equipment and medications (including epinephrine) and personnel trained to manage anaphylaxis.
- Monitor for local injection-site reactions and systemic symptoms (wheezing, rhinitis, urticaria, angioedema, hypotension, respiratory compromise).

### Specific clinical precautions
- Use diluted preparations for high-risk patients.
- Avoid administration during acute infection with fever or poorly controlled asthma.
- Be cautious when switching extract lots/manufacturers and when changing diluents/stabilizers.
- Interfering medications (see Drug Interactions section) should be withheld when planning skin testing where feasible.

- Serious adverse reactions: systemic reactions including anaphylaxis, which can be fatal; fatalities have occurred particularly in high-risk patients.
- Reporting: Report suspected adverse reactions via local pharmacovigilance systems as appropriate.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Medications that can interfere with skin test accuracy:
  - Antihistamines and other agents that suppress histamine responses (including topical corticosteroids when applied to test area, topical anesthetics, tricyclic antidepressants) can inhibit skin test responses and produce false-negative results.
- Medications that affect management of systemic reactions:
  - Beta-adrenergic (beta-) blockers: may antagonize cardiostimulatory and bronchodilatory effects of epinephrine, rendering standard treatment for anaphylaxis less effective. Exercise caution; these products may be unsuitable in patients on beta-blockers.
  - Alpha-adrenergic blockers and ergot alkaloids: may also interfere with expected epinephrine responses.
- Other pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions: Not specified in available sources.
- Management notes:
  - Consider withholding interfering medications before skin testing where clinically feasible.
  - For patients on beta-blockers or other agents that blunt epinephrine response, carefully weigh risks and benefits of testing or immunotherapy and ensure resuscitation measures are available.
