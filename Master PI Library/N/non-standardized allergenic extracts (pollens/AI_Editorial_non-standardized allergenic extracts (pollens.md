# Editorial Product Information: non-standardized allergenic extracts (pollens

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **non-standardized allergenic extracts (pollens)**
- Chemical class: Allergenic extract preparations derived from pollen sources (non-standardized)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Percutaneous (prick/puncture), intradermal, subcutaneous

## 2. Regulatory History
- Initial U.S. approval: 1968
- Recent PI revision note: An update addressing "Anaphylaxis Following False Negative Food Allergen Skin Test Results" listed 01/2023.
- Any withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- Other jurisdictional registration dates or last-revision dates: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Skin test diagnosis of patients with a clinical history suggesting allergy to one or more of the specific pollen allergens.
- Allergen immunotherapy for reduction of allergen‑induced allergic symptoms when allergy is confirmed by appropriate positive skin tests or in vitro testing for allergen‑specific IgE antibodies.
- Food extracts: Not proven safe or effective for allergen immunotherapy (i.e., not indicated for therapeutic immunotherapy use).

(Where any regional differences exist in the supplied sources, no contradictory regional indications for pollen extracts were provided.)

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General principles
- For percutaneous, intradermal or subcutaneous use only.
- Stock concentrates are supplied labeled in weight/volume (w/v) and/or protein nitrogen units (PNU)/mL (measure of total protein).
  - Aqueous stock concentrates: up to 1:10 w/v or up to 40,000 PNU/mL.
  - 50% glycerin stock concentrates: up to 1:20 w/v.
- Extracts are diluted with sterile diluents for intradermal testing and for subcutaneous immunotherapy. For percutaneous testing, glycerinated stock concentrate is used; dilute in patients at greater risk for systemic reaction.
- Parenteral products should be inspected visually prior to use; discard if particulate matter or discoloration is present.

### Preparation (dilution series)
- 10-fold dilution series (typical method): add 0.5 mL of concentrate to 4.5 mL sterile diluent to make successive 10-fold dilutions.
  - Example equivalences (illustrative from stock concentrations):
    - Concentrate (stock): 1:10 w/v or 20,000 PNU/mL (undiluted)
    - Dilution 1 (0.5 mL + 4.5 mL): 1:100 w/v or 2,000 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 2: 1:1,000 w/v or 200 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 3: 1:10,000 w/v or 20 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 4: 1:100,000 w/v or 2 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 5: 1:1,000,000 w/v or 0.2 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 6: 1:10,000,000 w/v or 0.02 PNU/mL
  - Note: There is no direct potency correlation across the table between PNU/mL and w/v; use labelled strengths appropriately.
- 5-fold dilution series (alternative): add 1 mL concentrate to 4 mL diluent to make successive 5-fold dilutions.
  - Example equivalences:
    - Concentrate (stock): 1:10 w/v or 20,000 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 1: 1:50 w/v or 4,000 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 2: 1:250 w/v or 800 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 3: 1:1,250 w/v or 160 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 4: 1:6,250 w/v or 32 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 5: 1:31,250 w/v or 6.4 PNU/mL
    - Dilution 6: 1:156,250 w/v or 1.28 PNU/mL
  - Note: There is no direct potency correlation across the table between PNU/mL and w/v.

### Diagnostic testing

Percutaneous (prick/puncture)
- Use glycerinated extract at the highest available stock concentration for routine percutaneous testing.
- In patients at greater risk for systemic reaction, use 10-fold or 5-fold dilutions of concentrate.
- Technique:
  - Prick test: place one drop of extract with controls on the skin and pierce through the drop with a skin test device (slight lifting motion).
  - Puncture test: place one drop and pierce perpendicular through the drop.
  - Read and record skin test responses 15–20 minutes after application.
- Controls:
  - Positive control: glycerinated histamine solution (6 mg/mL or 1 mg/mL histamine base may be used).
  - Negative control: 50% glycerin‑saline solution.
- Validity criteria:
  - Positive control response should be at least 3 mm larger than negative control response.
  - Negative control should elicit no reaction or only small reaction (<2 mm wheal, <5 mm erythema).
  - If controls are invalid, repeat testing.

Intradermal
- Initial dilutions for intradermal: typically dilute stock concentrate to 1:100 to 1:1,000 (v/v) using sterile diluent (glycerin-free diluent for intradermal).
- If initial intradermal test negative, progressively stronger doses may be used up to a maximum recommended strength of 1:25 (v/v) dilution of extract concentrate.
- Inject 0.02 mL intradermally.
- Use normal or buffered saline, or saline with human serum albumin (HSA) as diluent when specified.

Interpretation of skin reactions
- Measure both wheal and flare. Record largest diameter (length) and perpendicular width at midpoint; average the two diameters to report size.
- Consider wheal and flare as separate entities when interpreting reactivity.
- Correlate skin test results with patient history and known exposures.

### Immunotherapy (subcutaneous)
- For subcutaneous immunotherapy only.
- Stock concentrates for immunotherapy: aqueous (up to 1:10 w/v or 40,000 PNU/mL) and 50% glycerin (up to 1:20 w/v).
- Diluent: normal saline, buffered saline, HSA-saline, or 10% glycerin-saline depending on patient reactivity to diluent.
- Administration:
  - Inject subcutaneously in lateral upper arm or thigh; avoid intravascular injection.
  - Typical injection frequency during build-up: 1–2 times per week until maintenance dose reached. Then increase interval to 2, 3, and finally 4 weeks at maintenance.
  - Minimum course of therapy: may be 3–5 years depending on clinical response.
- Starting doses (guidance):
  - Patients suspected to be at greater risk: start at 0.05 mL of 1:20,000,000 to 1:2,000,000 w/v dilution.
  - Patients not suspected to be at greater risk: may start at 0.1 mL of 1:200,000 to 1:20,000 w/v dilution.
- Dose escalation:
  - Increase each injection by no more than 50% of the previous dose; further increments depend on tolerance and response.
  - Select maximum tolerated maintenance dose individualized to clinical response and tolerance.
  - Doses >0.2 mL of stock concentrate are rarely administered because 50% glycerin diluent may cause discomfort.
- Dose modification / interruption guidance:
  - Withhold or reduce dose for active infection with fever, rhinitis/asthma symptoms, or exposure to large amounts of relevant allergen prior to scheduled injection.
  - Large local reactions >24 hours: consider repeating previous dose or reducing next dose.
  - Any systemic reaction: reduce subsequent dose by at least 75%. Recurrent systemic reactions may warrant cessation of attempts to increase dose.
  - Local adverse reactions: reduce next dose by at least 50% and proceed cautiously thereafter.
  - Unscheduled gaps: tolerance may be lost; reduce dose or repeat build-up steps according to concentration, prior systemic reactions, and time elapsed. No single validated algorithm—use clinical judgment or established clinic protocol.
  - Changing to a different lot or to a product from another manufacturer: reduce starting dose (general recommendation 50–75% reduction) and rebuild; dose intervals should not exceed one week during rebuild. Assess interchangeability case‑by‑case.
  - Switching from non‑stabilized to HSA‑stabilized diluents: consider lowering dose when switching because HSA‑stabilized diluents are more stable.

### Missed dose instructions
- Specific stepwise algorithms for missed doses are not provided; the PI states tolerance can be lost during prolonged intervals and recommends dose reduction or repeat of earlier build-up steps depending on concentration, prior history of systemic reactions, and duration of lapse. Use clinical judgment or institutional protocols.

## 5. Contraindications
- Severe, unstable, or uncontrolled asthma.
- History of any severe systemic or severe local allergic reaction to an allergen extract.
- Any other contraindications specific to jurisdictions: Not specified in available sources beyond the above.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed warning / critical warning
- **Severe allergic reactions, including life‑threatening anaphylaxis, have been reported following administration of non‑standardized allergenic extracts (pollens).**
- **Do not administer these products to patients with severe, unstable, or uncontrolled asthma.**
- **Observe patients in the office for at least 30 minutes following administration. Emergency measures and personnel trained in their use must be immediately available.**
- Patients at increased risk for severe reactions include those with extreme sensitivity to the extract, those receiving accelerated (“rush”) build‑up schedules, those switching to another lot or manufacturer, those receiving high doses, and those with concomitant exposure to similar environmental allergens.
- These products may not be suitable for patients with medical conditions that decrease the ability to survive a systemic allergic reaction (e.g., markedly compromised lung function, unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction, significant arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension).
- Use caution or avoid in patients taking medications that could make them unresponsive to epinephrine or inhaled bronchodilators (see Interactions).

### General warnings and precautions
- Administer extracts only in healthcare settings where personnel and resuscitative equipment are available.
- Observe patients for at least 30 minutes after administration.
- Consider using more dilute preparations for patients suspected to be at greater risk.
- Interference with diagnostic accuracy:
  - Antihistamines and other medications that suppress histamine (including topical corticosteroids, topical anesthetics, and tricyclic antidepressants) can interfere with skin test results. Discontinue such agents per appropriate washout times prior to testing as clinically indicated.
- False negative skin tests for food extracts may lead to unexpected systemic reactions, including anaphylaxis; exercise caution in interpreting negative food skin tests (recent PI revision highlighted this risk).

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor for local and systemic allergic reactions during and after testing or immunotherapy.
- For skin testing, ensure positive and negative controls are valid (positive control ≥3 mm larger than negative control; negative control <2 mm wheal and <5 mm erythema). If controls invalid, repeat test.
- Measure and document wheal and flare diameters 15–20 minutes after percutaneous testing.
- Facilities must be prepared to manage anaphylaxis (epinephrine, airway support, oxygen, intravenous fluids, bronchodilators, etc.).

### Specific clinical precautions
- Exercise caution when administering to patients on beta-adrenergic blockers (may blunt response to epinephrine and bronchodilators). Consider risks vs. benefits.
- Patients on alpha-adrenergic blockers or ergot alkaloids may also have altered responses to epinephrine.
- Consider reducing dosage or avoiding use in patients with serious cardiovascular disease where epinephrine administration could be hazardous.

## 7. Adverse Effects
- The most common adverse reactions during subcutaneous immunotherapy are local injection-site reactions (erythema, itching, swelling, tenderness, pain) occurring in 26–82% of patients.
- Systemic adverse reactions occur in ≤7% of patients receiving subcutaneous immunotherapy and include generalized skin erythema, urticaria, pruritus, angioedema, rhinitis, wheezing, laryngeal edema, and hypotension. These systemic reactions can be fatal.
- Serious systemic adverse reactions including anaphylaxis and fatal reactions have occurred.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Medications that can interfere with skin testing:
  - Antihistamines and other histamine-suppressing agents (including oral antihistamines) can suppress skin-test responsiveness.
  - Topical corticosteroids and topical anesthetics can interfere with skin-test results.
  - Tricyclic antidepressants can interfere with skin-test responsiveness.
- Medications affecting treatment of systemic reactions:
  - Beta-adrenergic blockers: may blunt or prevent response to epinephrine and inhaled bronchodilators; patients on beta-blockers may be unresponsive to usual doses of epinephrine for anaphylaxis.
  - Alpha-adrenergic blockers and ergot alkaloids: may reduce responsiveness to epinephrine.
- Management notes:
  - Consider withholding or appropriately timing interfering medications before diagnostic testing per clinical guidance.
  - Be cautious when initiating immunotherapy in patients taking beta-blockers or other agents that may interfere with emergency treatment; weigh risks and benefits.
