# Editorial Product Information: prilocaine + felypressin

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **prilocaine + felypressin**
- Chemical class:
  - Prilocaine: amide-type local anaesthetic (membrane-stabilising agent)
  - Felypressin: synthetic vasopressin analogue (vasoconstrictor)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: local infiltration and regional (nerve) block for dental and oral surgical procedures
- Note: Do not use this document to infer formulation, packaging, storage, or regulatory scheduling details (not specified here).

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval: 28 February 2008 (as stated in available source)
- Date of last revision/update of the provided source PI: 15 November 2018
- Withdrawal, suspension, restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Production of local anaesthesia for routine dental procedures and oral surgery by infiltration and nerve block techniques (approved indication as stated in provided sources).

## 4. Dosage and Administration
General principles
- Use the lowest dose that produces effective anaesthesia; tailor dose to patient size, vascularity of tissues, and technique.
- Inject slowly with frequent aspiration to reduce risk of inadvertent intravascular injection.
- Multiple injections should be administered at spaced intervals.
- Avoid local anaesthetic techniques in regions of inflammation or sepsis.

Strength information (as provided)
- Typical concentration described in the available source: 3% solution (30 mg/mL prilocaine) with felypressin 0.03 IU/mL (0.54 µg/mL). (Volume-based examples in the source indicate commonly used cartridge volumes; consult product labelling for local presentation details.)

Recommended dosing (average healthy adult ~70 kg) — volumes, onset and approximate duration
- Infiltration
  - Suggested injected volume: ~1 mL
  - Onset (approx): 2 minutes
  - Duration: pulp ~40 minutes; soft tissue ~2.5 hours
- Nerve block
  - Suggested injected volume: ~1.5–2 mL
  - Onset (approx): 4 minutes
  - Duration: pulp ~90 minutes; soft tissue ~3.5 hours

Other dosing guidance
- Typical dental dosage range described: 1–5 mL of the 3% solution (equivalent to approximately 30–150 mg prilocaine).
- Recommended maximum single prilocaine dose cited as a guide: do not exceed 9 mg/kg at any one time; however, individualisation is essential and toxic doses vary widely between patients.
- Note on high-dose risks: methaemoglobinaemia risk increases with higher total doses; human data cited discuss 400 mg and 600 mg total doses in relation to methemoglobin formation (see Adverse Effects and Overdose sections).

Administration instructions
- Always have resuscitative equipment and emergency medicines (including oxygen) immediately available.
- Aspirate before injecting; inject slowly and avoid intravascular administration.
- Avoid prolonged contact of solution with metal surfaces (procedural precaution from source).
- Advise patients to avoid biting or injuring anaesthetised tissues until normal sensation returns.

Missed dose instructions: Not applicable / Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known allergy or hypersensitivity to amide-type local anaesthetics or any component of the injection solution.
- Congenital or idiopathic methaemoglobinaemia.
- Use of local anaesthetic techniques in the presence of inflammation and/or sepsis in the region of the proposed injection.
- Additional note in source: detection of sensitivity by skin testing is of limited value.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
- **Resuscitation readiness:** Resuscitative equipment and medicines, including oxygen, must be immediately available whenever local anaesthetics are used.
- **Injection technique:** Inject slowly with frequent aspiration; space multiple injections.
- **Cardiac conditions:** Use with great caution in patients with severe bradycardia, conduction disturbances, or severe digitalis intoxication.
- **Accumulation risk:** Prilocaine and/or metabolites may accumulate during prolonged or repeated administration in patients with hepatic, renal or cardiac impairment, in the elderly, or in patients in poor general condition. Although unlikely at usual dental doses, caution is advised.
- **Allergy and sensitivity:** Use with caution in patients with known medicine sensitivities. Cross‑sensitivity between ester and amide local anaesthetics is not generally expected, but caution remains warranted.
- **Malignant hyperthermia:** Use with caution in patients with genetic predisposition to malignant hyperthermia; safety not fully established.
- **Hypoxaemia and methaemoglobinaemia:** Large doses of prilocaine may produce methaemoglobinaemia; exercise caution in patients with pre-existing hypoxaemia.
- **Felypressin-specific caution:** Felypressin may cause a rise in blood pressure or coronary constriction if overdosed.
- **Local trauma risk:** Advise patients to avoid inadvertent self-injury (lips, tongue, cheeks) while anaesthetised; postpone ingestion of food until normal sensation returns.
- **Head and neck injections:** Intravascular injection of even small volumes in the head and neck area may cause systemic adverse effects.
- **Use in patients with severe systemic disease:** Administer with caution (or avoid) in severe/untreated hypertension, severe heart disease, advanced diabetes, severe anaemia, circulatory failure, or other major pathological conditions.
- Use in the elderly: elderly patients may require special attention and possible dose reduction to reduce risk of adverse effects.
- Paediatric use: doses should be reduced in proportion to body weight; calculate and individualise the dose.
- Effects on laboratory tests: Not specified in available sources beyond the impact of methaemoglobin on pulse oximetry (see Adverse Effects).

Monitoring requirements
- Monitor for CNS and cardiovascular signs of systemic toxicity during and after administration.
- Consider methaemoglobinaemia in the setting of persistent cyanosis not responsive to oxygen; pulse oximetry readings may be falsely low in the presence of methaemoglobinaemia.

## 7. Adverse Effects
- Reactions to prilocaine in dental doses are described as rare. Psychogenic reactions in anticipation of or during dental procedures are common and may mimic systemic reactions.
- Many adverse effects relate to high plasma concentrations from excessive dosage, rapid absorption, delayed elimination, or inadvertent intravascular injection.
- If methaemoglobinaemia occurs, clinical signs include cyanosis (nails, lips) and reduced oxygen carrying capacity; severity and symptoms are dose related.

Specific notes on frequency and dose-related adverse effects (from available sources)
- Methaemoglobinaemia:
  - Dental doses (1–5 mL of 3% solution; ~30–150 mg prilocaine) make the occurrence of clinically relevant methaemoglobinaemia unlikely in routine practice.
  - Human data cited: at 400 mg prilocaine the incidence of methaemoglobinaemia was not statistically significant; cyanosis between 400–600 mg is extremely rare; at 600 mg methaemoglobin levels formed less than 15% of total haemoglobin (typically not associated with overt respiratory, cardiovascular or CNS symptoms, but cyanosis has been reported).
  - Treatment: a single intravenous injection of 1% methylene blue at 1 mg/kg over 5 minutes typically reverses methaemoglobinaemia within ~15 minutes; do not repeat routinely because high concentrations of methylene blue may oxidise haemoglobin.
- Pulse oximetry: even low concentrations of methaemoglobin may distort pulse oximetry readings and produce falsely low oxygen saturation values.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Anti‑arrhythmic medicines: local anaesthetics of the amide type should be used with caution in patients receiving anti‑arrhythmic medicines (e.g., mexiletine), since toxic effects may be additive.
- Medicines that predispose to methaemoglobin formation: concurrent use of agents known to increase methaemoglobin (e.g., sulfonamides, certain antimalarials and certain nitric compounds) may potentiate prilocaine‑induced methaemoglobinaemia.
- Pharmacokinetic interactions: Not specified in available sources.
- Management notes:
  - Exercise caution and monitor closely when combining with antiarrhythmics.
  - Avoid coadministration with known oxidising agents that increase methaemoglobin where possible; if coadministration is required, monitor for signs of methaemoglobinaemia.
- Jurisdictional interaction differences: Not specified in available sources.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category (as stated in available source): [Australian] Category A.
- Clinical guidance from source:
  - Prilocaine has been used extensively for dental procedures during pregnancy without reports of harm to mother or fetus in the available data; however, safe use during pregnancy with respect to fetal development has not been definitively established.
  - Large doses of prilocaine may cause maternal and fetal methaemoglobinaemia which could lead to fetal hypoxia.
- Other jurisdictions’ pregnancy classification systems: Not specified in available sources.

Breastfeeding
- Prilocaine may pass into human milk in small amounts; generally not expected to pose significant risk to the neonate at usual dental doses.
- It is not known whether felypressin is excreted in breast milk (not specified).
- Recommendation: weigh potential benefits against possible risks; if concern exists, consider alternative measures or monitor neonate.

Fertility

## 11. Pharmacology
Mechanism of action
- Prilocaine: an amide‑type local anaesthetic that stabilises neuronal membranes and reversibly inhibits initiation and conduction of nerve impulses, producing local anaesthesia.
- Felypressin: a synthetic vasopressin analogue (vasoconstrictor) that reduces the rate of systemic absorption of prilocaine from the injection site, thereby prolonging duration of local anaesthesia; compared with adrenaline, felypressin is described as not producing ischaemia distal to the injection site.

Pharmacodynamics
- Prilocaine has a similar onset time and potency to lignocaine (lidocaine) and is described as having lower toxicity than lignocaine in the available source.
- Addition of felypressin increases duration of action by reducing absorption.

Key overdose and emergency management
- Systemic toxicity initially manifests as CNS excitation (nervousness, dizziness, blurred vision, tremor) and can progress to drowsiness, convulsions, unconsciousness, respiratory depression/arrest.
- Cardiovascular toxicity usually manifests as depression (peripheral vasodilation, hypotension, myocardial depression, bradycardia) and can rapidly lead to cardiovascular collapse and cardiac arrest.
- Emergency treatment priorities:
  - Ensure patent airway; support ventilation with oxygen and assisted or controlled respiration as needed.
  - Provide cardiovascular support and prolonged resuscitation if required.
  - If methaemoglobinaemia is suspected and cyanosis persists despite oxygen, administer a single IV dose of 1% methylene blue at 1 mg/kg over 5 minutes; this normally reverses methaemoglobinaemia within about 15 minutes. Do not repeat routinely because large amounts of methylene blue may oxidise haemoglobin. Also rule out other causes of cyanosis (acute hypoxaemia, heart failure).
- For management of specific complications (e.g., arrhythmias caused by sympathomimetics), consult standard critical care/toxicology texts.
