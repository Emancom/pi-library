# Editorial Product Information: Phenobarbital

## Editorial Note
- This editorial synthesis is based on the official Product Information (PI) from the following jurisdiction: **Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)**.
- The purpose of this document is to provide a harmonised editorial overview of phenobarbital for use by healthcare professionals. 
- **Note:** This is an editorial synthesis and does not serve as a substitute for the official Product Information required for prescribing within a specific jurisdiction. At the time of this writing, only the Australian PI was available for synthesis; information regarding other jurisdictions (e.g., US, European, Canadian) is noted as "Not specified in available sources" where applicable.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Phenobarbital (a barbituric acid derivative).
- **Brand Names:** Orion Phenobarbital [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** N03AA02 (Not explicitly stated in source, but standard clinical classification).
- **Dosage Form(s) and Strength(s):** 
    - Oral Liquid (Elixir): 15 mg/5 mL (0.3% w/v) [Australian].
- **Routes of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Orion Laboratories Pty Ltd T/A Evaris Pharma [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Treatment of epilepsy** [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing
Dosage must be strictly individualised based on the patient's age, body weight, and clinical condition. Therapeutic drug monitoring is essential; the target plasma concentration for seizure control is generally **15 to 40 micrograms/mL (65 to 170 micromoles/L)** [Australian].

### Adult Dosage
- **Recommended Range:** 60 mg to 240 mg per day.
- **Frequency:** Administered in 2 to 3 divided doses. Due to its long half-life, it may be administered as a single daily dose once stabilised [Australian].

### Paediatric Dosage
- **Recommended Range:** 1 mg to 6 mg per kg of body weight per day.
- **Frequency:** Administered in 2 to 3 divided doses [Australian].

### Administration and Titration
- **Withdrawal:** Phenobarbital must be withdrawn gradually to prevent the precipitation of seizures or status epilepticus. A suggested taper involves reducing the total daily dose by 30 mg or by 10% daily, provided the patient tolerates the reduction [Australian].
- **Elderly/Debilitated:** Lower doses are recommended as these patients may exhibit paradoxical excitement, confusion, or depression at standard doses [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
Phenobarbital is contraindicated in:
- Known hypersensitivity to barbituric acid derivatives [Australian].
- Porphyria [Australian].
- Severe respiratory depression or pulmonary insufficiency [Australian].
- Renal or hepatic impairment [Australian].
- Sleep apnoea [Australian].
- Uncontrolled diabetes mellitus [Australian].
- Severe anaemia due to folate deficiency [Australian].
- Hyperkinetic children [Australian].
- Alcoholism, drug dependency, or suicidal potential [Australian].
- Presence of uncontrolled pain (may cause paradoxical excitement) [Australian].
- Elderly patients exhibiting nocturnal confusion or restlessness from sedative-hypnotics [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Dependence and Tolerance:** Prolonged use leads to physical dependence and tolerance. Abrupt discontinuation can cause severe withdrawal symptoms, including hallucinations, tachycardia, and status epilepticus [Australian].
- **Dermatological Reactions:** Mild reactions occur in 1–3% of patients. However, serious and potentially fatal reactions, including **Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS)** and **Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)**, have been reported. Treatment must be stopped at the first sign of a rash [Australian].
- **Haematological Effects:** Long-term therapy requires blood count monitoring due to rare reports of megaloblastic anaemia (linked to folate deficiency) and thrombocytopenia [Australian].
- **Bone Health:** Prolonged use may lead to rickets or osteomalacia due to increased metabolism of Vitamin D [Australian].
- **CNS Depression:** Concurrent use with other CNS depressants (alcohol, narcotics, etc.) increases sedation risk [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
| Frequency | Adverse Effect |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Very Common (>10%)** | Mild skin reactions (maculopapular, morbilliform) (1-3% incidence noted in source) [Australian]. |
| **Common (1–10%)** | Drowsiness, sedation (may diminish with continued use); in neonates: drug dependency and Vitamin K deficiency symptoms [Australian]. |
| **Uncommon (0.1–1%)** | Agitation, confusion, hyperkinesia, ataxia, nightmares, psychiatric disturbances, hallucinations, hypotension, syncope, bradycardia, severe respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, folate deficiency, hypocalcaemia [Australian]. |
| **Rare (<0.1%)** | Peripheral vascular collapse, exfoliative dermatitis, SJS, TEN, toxic hepatitis, jaundice, rickets, osteomalacia, agranulocytosis [Australian]. |

## 7. Drug Interactions
Phenobarbital is a potent inducer of hepatic microsomal enzymes (CYP450 system, including CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP1A2) and may significantly reduce the efficacy of the following:
- **Anticoagulants:** Decreased response; prothrombin time must be monitored [Australian].
- **Anticonvulsants:** Accelerates metabolism of carbamazepine and lamotrigine. Phenytoin levels may be increased or decreased [Australian].
- **Sodium Valproate:** Inhibits phenobarbital metabolism (increasing levels) while phenobarbital increases valproate's hepatotoxic metabolites [Australian].
- **Oral Contraceptives:** Reduced efficacy; breakthrough bleeding may occur [Australian].
- **Corticosteroids:** Diminished systemic effects; may exacerbate asthma [Australian].
- **Anti-infectives:** Reduced levels of doxycycline, metronidazole, chloramphenicol, griseofulvin, and certain protease inhibitors [Australian].
- **Others:** Reduced efficacy of digoxin, beta-blockers, theophylline, cyclosporin, and paracetamol (with increased hepatotoxicity risk) [Australian].
- **St John’s Wort:** May decrease phenobarbital levels [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy Category:** **Category D** [Australian]. 
- **Risk Summary:** Readily crosses the placenta. Associated with a 6–7% risk of major malformations (craniofacial, cardiac, digital defects, cleft lip/palate). Prenatal exposure is linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes and lower IQ. 
- **Clinical Management:** Use only if benefits outweigh risks. Use the lowest effective dose. Supplement with Vitamin K1 (prophylactically for mother and neonate) and folic acid. Neonates must be monitored for withdrawal and bleeding [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** **Not recommended.** Phenobarbital is excreted in breast milk and may cause CNS depression in the infant [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Use with caution; may exacerbate hyperkinetic behaviour. Use is contraindicated in children already exhibiting hyperkinesis [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Higher risk of paradoxical excitement and confusion. Increased risk of falls and fractures. Dosage should be reduced and titrated slowly [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Contraindicated in severe cases; dose reduction required in mild-to-moderate dysfunction as phenobarbital is excreted renally [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Contraindicated in severe cases; metabolism occurs in the liver, necessitating dose reduction in hepatic dysfunction [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** A nonselective CNS depressant. It enhances and/or mimics the synaptic action of GABA, increases the motor cortex's threshold for electrical stimulation, and depresses synaptic transmission [Australian].
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption:** 70–90% absorbed; peak levels reached in ~1 hour (slower if lipid solubility is low) [Australian].
    - **Distribution:** 45% protein bound; high concentrations in the brain, liver, and kidneys [Australian].
    - **Metabolism:** Primarily hepatic; potent enzyme inducer [Australian].
    - **Elimination:** Half-life is 90–100 hours (adults), 65–70 hours (children), and significantly prolonged in neonates. 25–50% excreted unchanged in urine [Australian].
