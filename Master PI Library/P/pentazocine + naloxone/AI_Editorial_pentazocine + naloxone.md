# Editorial Product Information: pentazocine + naloxone

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): pentazocine + naloxone
- Chemical class:
  - Pentazocine: benzazocine (benzomorphan) opioid (mixed agonist–antagonist)
  - Naloxone: opioid antagonist (morphinan derivative)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 3. Approved Indications
- Management of pain severe enough to require an opioid analgesic and for which alternative treatments are inadequate.
- Regional variations in approved indications: Not specified in available sources beyond the general indication above.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
- General dosing (adult):
  - Product description in available sources indicates each tablet contains pentazocine hydrochloride equivalent to 50 mg pentazocine base and naloxone hydrochloride equivalent to 0.5 mg naloxone base.
  - Specific recommended dose regimens (e.g., dosing interval, maximum daily dose, dose adjustments) are not specified in available sources.
- Dosing by indication: Not specified in available sources.
- Administration instructions:
  - Oral administration.
  - Onset of clinically significant analgesia usually occurs between 15 and 30 minutes after oral dosing; duration of action is usually about 3 hours or longer.
- Titration schedules: Not specified in available sources.
- Missed dose instructions: Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
List of contraindications provided in available sources:
- Significant respiratory depression.
- Acute or severe bronchial asthma in an unmonitored setting or in the absence of resuscitative equipment.
- Known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction, including paralytic ileus.
- Hypersensitivity (e.g., anaphylaxis) to pentazocine, naloxone, or any excipient in the formulation.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- **[US]** WARNING: Serious and life‑threatening risks from use of **pentazocine + naloxone**:
  - Addiction, abuse, and misuse can lead to overdose and death. Assess each patient’s risk prior to prescribing and monitor regularly.
  - Life‑threatening respiratory depression may occur, especially at initiation of therapy or following dose increases.
  - Accidental ingestion (especially by children) can be fatal.
  - Risks from concomitant use with benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants include profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death.
  - Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome may result from prolonged maternal use during pregnancy.
  - Healthcare providers are strongly encouraged to complete a REMS‑compliant education program and counsel patients and caregivers on serious risks, safe use, and to ensure patients receive the Medication Guide.

### General warnings and precautions
- Respiratory depression:
  - Opioid-induced respiratory depression can be severe, life‑threatening, or fatal. Risk is greatest during initiation of therapy and after dose increases; CO2 retention can exacerbate sedative effects.
  - Accidental ingestion of a single dose can be fatal, particularly in children.
  - Opioids can cause sleep‑related breathing disorders including central sleep apnea and hypoxemia; risk is dose‑dependent.
- Addiction, abuse, and misuse:
  - **pentazocine + naloxone** exposes users to risks of addiction, abuse, and misuse; these risks can occur at recommended dosages.
  - Screen for personal or family history of substance abuse and mental illness; counsel and monitor high‑risk patients closely.
  - Consider prescribing or recommending an opioid overdose reversal agent (e.g., naloxone) and educate patients/caregivers in its use.
- Concomitant CNS depressants:
  - Concomitant use with benzodiazepines, other sedative hypnotics, alcohol, antipsychotics, gabapentinoids, and other CNS depressants increases risk of respiratory depression and death. Reserve combined use for patients for whom alternatives are inadequate; use lowest effective doses and shortest durations.
  - If concomitant use is unavoidable, consider reduced initial doses and careful titration.
- Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS):
  - Prolonged maternal opioid use can result in neonatal withdrawal that may be life‑threatening if not recognized and treated. Ensure neonatology expertise is available at delivery; observe and manage newborns per protocols.
- Opioid‑Induced Hyperalgesia (OIH):
  - Increased pain sensitivity or worsening pain may reflect OIH. Consider opioid dose reduction or rotation if OIH is suspected.
- Patients with chronic pulmonary disease:
  - Patients with COPD, cor pulmonale, decreased respiratory reserve, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or pre‑existing respiratory depression are at increased risk of life‑threatening respiratory depression.
- Elderly, cachectic, or debilitated patients:
  - Increased risk of respiratory depression from altered pharmacokinetics and clearance; use caution and consider non‑opioid alternatives.
- Adrenal insufficiency:
  - Cases reported with opioid use, usually after >1 month of therapy. Symptoms may be nonspecific. If suspected, confirm with testing and treat with physiologic corticosteroids; wean opioid to allow recovery.
- Endocrine effects:
  - Opioids can inhibit ACTH, cortisol, and luteinizing hormone and can lead to androgen deficiency with prolonged use (e.g., decreased libido, impotence, amenorrhea, infertility).
- Immunologic effects:
  - Opioids may have modest immunosuppressive effects; clinical significance unknown.
- Withdrawal and dependence:
  - Physical dependence can occur; abrupt cessation may precipitate withdrawal.
- Antagonist effect on other opioids:
  - Pentazocine weakly antagonizes morphine and meperidine analgesia and incompletely reverses their cardiovascular, respiratory, and behavioral depression.
- Naloxone component:
  - Orally administered naloxone at the 0.5 mg level stated in the formulation has no pharmacologic activity; presence of naloxone is intended to deter parenteral misuse by antagonizing parenterally administered pentazocine.
- Miscellaneous:
  - Pentazocine can cause peripheral vasodilation leading to orthostatic hypotension or syncope. Histamine release manifestations (pruritus, flushing, sweating, red eyes) may occur.
  - Pentazocine may reduce gastrointestinal motility and increase smooth muscle tone; may cause constipation, delayed gastric emptying, spasm of sphincter of Oddi, and transient elevations in serum amylase; opioid‑induced esophageal dysfunction has been reported.

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor for:
  - Respiratory status and signs of respiratory depression, especially during initiation/titration and when used with other CNS depressants.
  - Signs of misuse, abuse, addiction, and diversion.
  - Signs of neonatal opioid withdrawal in infants born to exposed mothers.
  - Signs/symptoms of adrenal insufficiency and endocrine dysfunction; conduct diagnostic testing if suspected.
  - Pain control, signs of opioid‑induced hyperalgesia, and gastrointestinal adverse effects.
  - In elderly or debilitated patients, monitor closely for sedation and respiratory depression.
- Specific laboratory monitoring: Not specified in available sources beyond diagnostic testing to confirm adrenal insufficiency if suspected.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Concomitant CNS depressants:
  - Additive or synergistic sedation and respiratory depression with benzodiazepines, other opioids, alcohol, non‑benzodiazepine sedative‑hypnotics, anxiolytics, tranquilizers, muscle relaxants, general anesthetics, antipsychotics, and gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin).
  - Observational data indicate increased risk of drug‑related mortality when benzodiazepines are combined with opioids; similar risks expected with other CNS depressants.
  - Management: Reserve concomitant prescribing for patients without alternatives; use lowest effective doses and shortest durations; if already taking one class, reduce initial dose of the other and titrate carefully. Consider prescribing an opioid overdose reversal agent and counsel patients/caregivers.
- Opioid antagonism and mixed agonist–antagonist interactions:
  - Pentazocine is a partial mu agonist and kappa agonist and can weakly antagonize the analgesic effects of full opioid agonists such as morphine and meperidine; pentazocine produces incomplete reversal of cardiovascular, respiratory, and behavioral depression induced by those agonists.
  - The naloxone included in the oral formulation is intended to deter parenteral misuse; orally administered naloxone at the stated amount has no pharmacologic activity.

### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Specific cytochrome P450 or transporter interactions: Not specified in available sources.
- Effect of hepatic or renal impairment on pentazocine/naloxone exposure: Pentazocine is metabolized in the liver and excreted primarily in urine; specific dosing adjustments for renal or hepatic impairment are not specified in available sources.

### Clinically significant interactions and management notes
- Concomitant benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants: See management above (use lowest effective dosages, shorten duration, monitor closely, consider naloxone).
- Other opioids: Pentazocine may reduce the effect of full opioid agonists—use caution when converting from or to other opioids; overestimation of potency when switching can cause fatal overdose.
- Overdose reversal agents: Have access to opioid overdose reversal agents (e.g., naloxone) and educate caregivers and household members on recognition of overdose and use of reversal agents.

Observe newborns for signs and manage per established protocols.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Pentazocine: Mixed agonist–antagonist at opioid receptors — partial agonist at the mu opioid receptor and agonist at the kappa opioid receptor. Produces analgesia and central nervous system effects characteristic of opioids.
- Naloxone: Opioid antagonist; included in the oral combination primarily to antagonize pentazocine if the product is misused parenterally. When administered orally at the stated dose (0.5 mg), naloxone has no pharmacologic activity according to available sources.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Respiratory system: Pentazocine depresses respiration via direct action on brainstem respiratory centers, reducing responsiveness to increases in CO2 and electrical stimulation.
- Pupillary effects: Pentazocine causes miosis; mydriasis can occur in overdose secondary to hypoxia.
- Gastrointestinal: Decreases GI motility; increases smooth muscle tone in stomach antrum and duodenum; delays small intestinal transit; decreases propagative peristalsis in colon and may cause spasm leading to constipation.
- Cardiovascular: Produces peripheral vasodilation, which may lead to orthostatic hypotension or syncope; histamine release may manifest as pruritus, flushing, red eyes, and sweating.
- Endocrine: Inhibits ACTH, cortisol, and LH; stimulates prolactin and growth hormone secretion; may affect pancreatic insulin and glucagon secretion; prolonged use may affect gonadal function.
- Immune: Modest immunosuppressive effects have been described; clinical significance unknown.
- Antagonist interactions: Pentazocine weakly antagonizes analgesic effects of morphine, meperidine, and phenazocine; incomplete reversal of their depressant effects has been reported.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Onset of analgesia typically 15–30 minutes after oral administration.
- Time to peak: Mean time to peak plasma concentration approximately 1.7 hours (range 0.5–4 hours) in studies cited in available sources.
- Elimination half‑life: Mean plasma elimination half‑life approximately 3.6 hours (range 1.5–10 hours).
- Metabolism: Metabolized in the liver; oxidative metabolism of terminal methyl groups and glucuronide conjugation reported.
- Excretion: Primarily renal excretion; approximately 60% of dose eliminated within 24 hours. Pentazocine crosses the placenta.
- Naloxone: Orally at 0.5 mg has no pharmacologic activity; parenteral naloxone at comparable doses antagonizes pentazocine and other opioid agonists.
