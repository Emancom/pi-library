# Editorial Product Information: Potassium Citrate

## Editorial Note
- This editorial synthesis is based on official Product Information (PI) from the following jurisdiction: **United States (FDA)**.
- The purpose of this document is to provide a harmonised editorial overview for healthcare professionals, integrating clinical data into a concise, scannable format.
- **Note:** This is an editorial synthesis and does not substitute for the official PI required for prescribing within a specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should consult local regulatory documents for legal prescribing requirements.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Potassium citrate (Citrate salt of potassium).
- **Brand Name(s):** **Urocit-K** [US].
- **ATC Code:** A12BA02 (Potassium citrate).
- **Dosage Form(s) and Strength(s):** Extended-release wax-matrix tablets: 5 mEq (540 mg), 10 mEq (1080 mg), and 15 mEq (1620 mg) [US].
- **Route(s) of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Mission Pharmacal Company [US].

## 2. Approved Indications
Potassium citrate is indicated for the management of the following conditions [US]:
- Renal tubular acidosis (RTA) with calcium stones.
- Hypocitraturic calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis of any etiology.
- Uric acid lithiasis, occurring with or without calcium stones.

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing Principles
The clinical objective of therapy is to restore normal urinary citrate (target >320 mg/day, aiming for a mean of 640 mg/day) and increase urinary pH to a target range of 6.0 to 7.0 [US].

### Dosing by Indication (Adults)
- **Severe Hypocitraturia (Urinary citrate <150 mg/day):** Initiate at 60 mEq/day. Dosage may be administered as 30 mEq twice daily or 20 mEq three times daily [US].
- **Mild to Moderate Hypocitraturia (Urinary citrate >150 mg/day):** Initiate at 30 mEq/day. Dosage may be administered as 15 mEq twice daily or 10 mEq three times daily [US].

### Administration Instructions
- Tablets must be taken with meals or within 30 minutes of a meal or bedtime snack [US].
- **Important:** Tablets must be swallowed whole. Patients should be instructed not to crush, chew, or suck the tablet to avoid mucosal irritation and ensure the extended-release mechanism functions correctly [US].
- Therapy should be accompanied by a diet that limits salt intake and encourages high fluid intake (target urine volume ≥2 liters/day) [US].

### Titration and Monitoring
- Doses exceeding 100 mEq/day have not been studied and should be avoided [US].
- **Monitoring:** 24-hour urinary citrate and/or urinary pH should be measured every four months to evaluate treatment adequacy and guide dose adjustments [US].
- **Laboratory requirements:** Monitor serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, CO2), serum creatinine, and CBC every four months. More frequent monitoring is required for patients with cardiac disease, renal disease, or acidosis [US].

## 4. Contraindications
Potassium citrate is contraindicated in the following patient populations [US]:
- **Hyperkalemia:** Patients with existing hyperkalemia or conditions predisposing to it (e.g., chronic renal failure, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, acute dehydration, adrenal insufficiency, extensive tissue breakdown, or strenuous exercise in unconditioned individuals).
- **Gastrointestinal (GI) Transit Delay:** Patients with conditions that delay tablet passage through the GI tract (e.g., delayed gastric emptying, esophageal compression, intestinal obstruction/stricture, or use of anticholinergic medications).
- **Peptic Ulcer Disease:** Due to the ulcerogenic potential of potassium salts.
- **Active Urinary Tract Infection (UTI):** Particularly infections with urea-splitting organisms; increased urinary pH may promote further bacterial growth, and citrate may be degraded by bacterial enzymes.
- **Renal Insufficiency:** Specifically patients with a GFR <0.7 mL/kg/min, due to the risks of hyperkalemia and soft tissue calcification.

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Hyperkalemia:** In patients with impaired potassium excretion mechanisms, administration can cause fatal hyperkalemia and cardiac arrest. This may develop rapidly and be asymptomatic. Avoid use in patients with severe myocardial damage or heart failure [US].
- **Gastrointestinal Lesions:** Solid-dose potassium chloride products have been associated with stenotic and/or ulcerative lesions of the small bowel and death. While experience with potassium citrate is limited, similar frequency (estimated at 1 per 100,000 patient-years) should be anticipated. Discontinue immediately if severe vomiting, abdominal pain, or GI bleeding occurs [US].
- **Monitoring Requirements:** Periodic ECGs should be performed, especially in patients at higher risk for electrolyte imbalances [US].

## 6. Adverse Effects
- **Common (1–10%):** Minor GI complaints, including abdominal discomfort, vomiting, diarrhea, loose stools, or nausea. These symptoms are often related to local GI irritation and may be mitigated by taking doses with food or reducing the dose [US].
- **Postmarketing Experience:** Intact tablet matrices may occasionally be found in the feces [US].
- **Serious Adverse Effects:** Potential for upper GI bleeding, bowel perforation, or obstruction. Fatal hyperkalemia leading to cardiac arrest is a critical risk in predisposed patients [US].

## 7. Drug Interactions
- **Potassium-Sparing Diuretics (e.g., triamterene, spironolactone, amiloride):** Concomitant use should be avoided as it can produce severe, life-threatening hyperkalemia [US].
- **Anticholinergics:** May slow GI transit time, increasing the risk of local GI irritation and ulceration from potassium salts [US].
- **RAAS Inhibitors (ACE Inhibitors, ARBs, Aliskiren):** These agents produce potassium retention; serum potassium must be monitored closely during co-administration [US].
- **NSAIDs:** May reduce renal synthesis of prostaglandin E and impair the renin-angiotensin system, leading to potassium retention. Monitor potassium levels closely [US].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** [US: Category not assigned/Animal studies not conducted]. It is unknown if potassium citrate can cause fetal harm or affect reproductive capacity. Administer to pregnant women only if clearly needed [US].
- **Breastfeeding:** The normal potassium content of human milk is approximately 13 mEq/L. It is unknown if supplementation affects this content. Use only if clearly needed [US].
- **Fertility:** Data not specified in available sources.

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Safety and effectiveness in children have not been established [US].
- **Elderly Use:** Data not specifically detailed; however, caution is advised due to the higher prevalence of renal impairment and cardiac disease in this population.
- **Renal Impairment:** Contraindicated if GFR is <0.7 mL/kg/min [US]. Close monitoring is required for any degree of renal insufficiency due to hyperkalemia risk.

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Oral potassium citrate is metabolised to an alkaline load. This increases urinary pH and raises urinary citrate by augmenting renal citrate clearance (modifying renal handling) without significantly altering serum citrate levels. 
- **Pharmacodynamics:** 
    - Increased urinary citrate complexes with calcium, reducing the activity of calcium ions and the saturation of calcium oxalate [US].
    - Citrate inhibits the spontaneous nucleation of calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate (brushite) [US].
    - Increased urinary pH increases the ionization of uric acid to the more soluble urate ion [US].
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption/Onset:** In patients with normal renal function, urinary citrate rise begins within 1 hour of a single dose and lasts 12 hours [US].
    - **Steady State:** With multiple doses, peak citrate excretion is reached by the third day [US].
    - **Elimination:** Upon withdrawal, urinary citrate levels begin to return to pre-treatment levels within the first day [US].

## 11. Clinical Trials
- **RTA with Calcium Stones:** In a small non-randomized study (n=9), potassium citrate (60–80 mEq/day) reduced the stone formation rate from 13 ± 27 to 1 ± 2 per year over two years [US].
- **Hypocitraturic Calcium Oxalate Nephrolithiasis:** A study of 89 patients showed that doses of 30–100 mEq/day sustained a rise in urinary citrate (from subnormal to 400–700 mg/day) and urinary pH (from 5.6–6.0 up to ~6.5), significantly reducing stone formation [US].
- **Uric Acid Lithiasis:** In 18 adult patients, therapy (30–80 mEq/day) raised urinary pH from 5.3 ± 0.3 to 6.2–6.5. Only one stone was formed during the 5-year study period [US].
