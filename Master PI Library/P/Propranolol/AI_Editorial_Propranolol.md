# Editorial Product Information: Propranolol Hydrochloride

## Editorial Note
- This editorial synthesis is based on the official Product Information (PI) provided by the following regulatory authority: **Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)**.
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview for healthcare professionals, consolidating clinical, pharmacological, and regulatory data into a single reference.
- **Disclaimer:** This document is an editorial synthesis and serves as a clinical resource only. It is not a substitute for the official, jurisdiction-specific Product Information required for prescribing within a particular region.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Propranolol hydrochloride (a non-selective beta-adrenoreceptor blocking agent).
- **Brand Names:** APO-Propranolol [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** C07AA05.
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - **Oral Tablets:** 10 mg and 40 mg [Australian].
- **Routes of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Arrotex Pharmaceuticals Pty Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
Based on the available [Australian] documentation, **propranolol** is indicated for:
- Management of hypertension.
- Treatment of angina pectoris.
- Prophylaxis of migraine.
- Management of essential tremor (including familial and senile variants).
- Control of cardiac dysrhythmias, including intrinsic dysrhythmias, those associated with thyrotoxicosis or anxiety, and drug-induced dysrhythmias (e.g., digitalis or adrenaline toxicity).
- Treatment of hypertrophic subaortic stenosis.
- Management of phaeochromocytoma (strictly in conjunction with an alpha-receptor blocker).
- Secondary prevention following suspected or definite myocardial infarction.
- Management of Fallot’s tetralogy.

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Instructions
Tablets may be administered independently of food intake [Australian].

### Adult Dosing
- **Hypertension:** Initially 40 mg twice daily. Maintenance usually ranges between 120–320 mg/day. Doses exceeding 320 mg/day lack established safety data [Australian].
- **Angina Pectoris & Essential Tremor:** Initially 40 mg two to three times daily. Effective ranges are typically 80–160 mg/day for tremor and 120–320 mg/day for angina [Australian].
- **Migraine Prophylaxis:** Initially 40 mg twice daily. Effective range is typically 80–160 mg/day. If no response occurs within three months, therapy should be discontinued [Australian].
- **Dysrhythmias, Anxiety Tachycardia, & Hypertrophic Subaortic Stenosis:** Most patients respond to 10–40 mg administered three or four times daily [Australian].
- **Phaeochromocytoma (with alpha-blockade):** 
    - Preoperative: 60 mg/day in divided doses for three days.
    - Maintenance: 30 mg/day in divided doses [Australian].
- **Myocardial Infarction:** Start with 40 mg four times daily for 2–3 days, then transition to 80 mg twice daily to facilitate compliance [Australian].

### Titration
Doses should generally be increased at weekly intervals based on clinical response [Australian].

### Missed Dose
Not specified in available sources.

## 4. Contraindications
- **Cardiovascular:** Congestive heart failure; right ventricular failure secondary to pulmonary hypertension; significant right ventricular hypertrophy; sick sinus syndrome; sinus bradycardia (less than 45–50 bpm); second and third-degree A-V block; hypotension; severe peripheral arterial circulatory disturbances; Prinzmetal's angina [Australian].
- **Respiratory:** History of bronchial asthma, airways obstruction, or a tendency toward bronchospasm [Australian].
- **Metabolic:** Patients prone to hypoglycemia (e.g., after prolonged fasting or restricted counter-regulatory reserves); metabolic acidosis [Australian].
- **Other:** Hypersensitivity to the drug; shock (cardiogenic or hypovolaemic); untreated phaeochromocytoma; anaesthesia with myocardial depressant agents (e.g., ether, chloroform) [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Abrupt Withdrawal:** **Propranolol** must not be discontinued suddenly in patients with ischaemic heart disease. Abrupt cessation may exacerbate angina or precipitate myocardial infarction. Tapering should occur over 8–14 days [Australian].
- **Cardiac Failure:** Beta-blockade may precipitate heart failure in susceptible patients. While traditionally contraindicated, experimental use in heart failure should only occur under specialist supervision [Australian].
- **Diabetes and Hypoglycaemia:** **Propranolol** may mask the symptoms of acute hypoglycaemia (especially tachycardia) and may prolong the hypoglycaemic response to insulin [Australian].
- **Thyrotoxicosis:** The drug may mask clinical signs of hyperthyroidism. Withdrawal should be managed carefully to avoid a thyroid storm [Australian].
- **Peripheral Circulation:** May exacerbate symptoms of peripheral vascular disease or Raynaud's phenomenon [Australian].
- **Anaphylaxis:** Patients with a history of severe anaphylaxis may be more reactive to repeated allergen challenge and may be unresponsive to standard doses of adrenaline [Australian].
- **Ocular/Skin Reactions:** While rare, the "practolol syndrome" (oculo-mucocutaneous syndrome) should be considered, involving rashes and dry eyes [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
**Propranolol** is generally well-tolerated, with most effects being transient.

- **Common (1–10%):** Fatigue, lassitude, bradycardia, cold extremities, exacerbation of Raynaud's phenomenon, sleep disturbances, and vivid dreams/nightmares [Australian].
- **Less Frequent/Rare:**
    - **Gastrointestinal:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, flatulence, abdominal pain, anorexia [Australian].
    - **Neurological:** Dizziness, hallucinations, mood changes, "muzziness," paraesthesia [Australian].
    - **Haemopoietic:** Thrombocytopenic purpura, agranulocytosis, eosinophilia [Australian].
    - **Respiratory:** Bronchospasm, laryngospasm [Australian].
    - **Other:** Alopecia, impotence, dry eyes, psoriasis-like skin reactions, visual disturbances [Australian].
- **Serious Adverse Effects:** Congestive heart failure, severe bradycardia/heart block, and fatal bronchospasm [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- **Calcium Antagonists:** Use with verapamil or diltiazem may cause severe hypotension, bradycardia, and asystole. Use dihydropyridines (e.g., nifedipine) with caution [Australian].
- **Antiarrhythmics:** Increased risk of myocardial depression with Class IA (disopyramide, quinidine), Class IB (mexiletine, lignocaine), Class IC (flecainide), and Class III (amiodarone) agents [Australian].
- **Digitalis Glycosides:** Potential for increased atrioventricular conduction time [Australian].
- **Sympathomimetics:** Adrenaline may counteract beta-blockers, potentially leading to hypertension and bradycardia [Australian].

### Pharmacokinetic Interactions
- **Rizatriptan:** **Propranolol** increases rizatriptan plasma levels via MAO-A inhibition. A reduced rizatriptan dose of 5 mg is recommended [Australian].
- **Enzyme Inducers/Inhibitors:** Cimetidine and hydralazine increase **propranolol** levels. Rifampicin may decrease levels [Australian].
- **Lignocaine:** Propranolol may increase lignocaine concentrations by ~30%; this combination should generally be avoided [Australian].
- **NSAIDs:** Ibuprofen and indomethacin may reduce the antihypertensive efficacy of beta-blockers [Australian].
- **Alcohol:** May increase plasma levels of **propranolol** [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** [Australian Category C]. **Propranolol** should only be used if essential. It reduces placental perfusion, which may lead to foetal death or premature delivery. Neonates may exhibit bradycardia or hypoglycaemia [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Most beta-blockers pass into breast milk; therefore, breastfeeding is not recommended following administration [Australian].
- **Fertility:** Data not available [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Dosing must be individualised based on cardiac status.
    - **Dysrhythmias/Phaeochromocytoma:** 0.25–0.5 mg/kg 3–4 times daily.
    - **Fallot's Tetralogy:** Up to 1 mg/kg 3–4 times daily.
    - **Migraine:** 10 mg once/twice daily (not recommended under age 7) [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Optimum dose should be determined by clinical response due to conflicting evidence regarding age-related blood levels [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Half-life may be increased; caution is advised during initial dosing. Severe renal disease may require dose adjustments [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Half-life is increased in significant impairment; caution is required when starting therapy [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Competitive, non-selective antagonist of $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ adrenoreceptors. It lacks intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA) and possesses membrane-stabilising properties. The S(-) isomer is the active form [Australian].
- **Pharmacodynamics:** Reduces heart rate, cardiac output, and myocardial oxygen demand [Australian].
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption:** Almost completely absorbed from the gut, but undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism [Australian].
    - **Distribution:** Widely distributed; 93% protein-bound [Australian].
    - **Metabolism:** Entirely hepatic. The 4-hydroxy metabolite (formed only after oral use) is active [Australian].
    - **Elimination:** 95–100% excreted in urine as metabolites. Plasma half-life is 3–6 hours (oral) [Australian].
