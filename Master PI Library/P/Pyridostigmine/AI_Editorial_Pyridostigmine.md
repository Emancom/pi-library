# Editorial Product Information: Pyridostigmine Bromide

## Editorial Note
*   **Contributing Jurisdictions:** This editorial synthesis is based on official Product Information (PI) provided by the **Australian (TGA)**. 
*   **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview of pyridostigmine bromide for healthcare professionals. It synthesises clinical data and regulatory requirements into a single reference.
*   **Disclaimer:** This is an editorial synthesis and does not substitute for the official PI required for prescribing in a specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should consult their local regulatory authority's approved documentation for definitive guidance.

## 1. Product Identification
*   **Active Ingredient:** Pyridostigmine bromide (a quaternary ammonium anticholinesterase).
*   **Brand Names:** Mestinon [Australian].
*   **ATC Code:** N07AA02.
*   **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    *   **10 mg tablets:** White to off-white, round, biconvex [Australian].
    *   **60 mg tablets:** Pale orange, round, biconvex, sugar-coated [Australian].
    *   **180 mg "Timespan" tablets:** Grey-yellow, biplanar, sustained-release, marked ‘M180’ and scored [Australian].
*   **Route of Administration:** Oral.
*   **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** iNova Pharmaceuticals (Australia) Pty Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
*   **Myasthenia Gravis:** For the treatment of muscle weakness and fatigue associated with myasthenia gravis [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adult)
The dosage must be individualised based on clinical response, disease severity, and the patient's current level of physical or emotional stress. 

*   **Standard Tablets (60 mg):** The typical dose is 1 to 3 tablets (60 mg to 180 mg) administered two to four times daily. In severe cases, higher doses may be required [Australian].
*   **Sustained-Release (180 mg Timespan):** 1 to 3 tablets (180 mg to 540 mg) administered once or twice daily. The interval between doses must be at least six hours [Australian].

### Administration Instructions
*   **Timing:** Doses should be adjusted so that peak effect coincides with periods of maximum fatigue (e.g., 30–45 minutes before meals for patients with swallowing difficulties) [Australian].
*   **Refractory State:** Patients may become refractory after long-term treatment. Responsiveness may sometimes be restored by reducing the dose or withdrawing the drug for several days under strict medical supervision [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
*   **Hypersensitivity:** Known allergy to anticholinesterase agents or bromides [Australian].
*   **Mechanical Obstructions:** Presence of mechanical intestinal or urinary tract obstruction [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
### General Warnings
*   **Crisis Differentiation:** Overdosage can lead to a "cholinergic crisis," while under-dosage results in "myasthenia crisis." Both present with increased muscle weakness. Differentiation is critical and may require an edrophonium test. 
*   **Masking Overdose:** Atropine may be used to manage muscarinic side effects, but clinicians are cautioned that this can mask signs of overdosage, potentially leading to an inadvertent cholinergic crisis [Australian].
*   **Aspiration Risk:** Vital capacity should be monitored during dosage titration to ensure adequate respiratory function. Facilities for cardiopulmonary resuscitation and intubation should be available [Australian].

### Specific Clinical Precautions
Use with extreme caution in patients with the following conditions [Australian]:
*   Bronchial asthma.
*   Bradycardia or cardiac arrhythmias.
*   Recent coronary occlusion.
*   Epilepsy.
*   Hyperthyroidism.
*   Peptic ulcer disease.
*   Vagotonia.
*   Megacolon or decreased gastrointestinal motility (risk of accumulation and delayed toxicity).

## 6. Adverse Effects
Mestinon generally presents with a lower incidence of gastrointestinal stimulation compared to other anticholinesterases, but the following may occur:

| Category | Adverse Effects |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Muscarinic** | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, increased peristalsis, increased salivation, increased bronchial secretions, miosis, and diaphoresis [Australian]. |
| **Nicotinic** | Muscle cramps, fasciculations, and muscle weakness [Australian]. |
| **Cardiovascular** | Bradycardia and hypotension [Australian]. |
| **Dermatological** | Rash (occasionally induced by the bromide radical) [Australian]. |

**Serious Adverse Effects:** CNS symptoms (agitation, restlessness), cardiac arrest, respiratory paralysis, and pulmonary oedema are associated with extreme overdosage [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
*   **Depolarising Muscle Relaxants:** Pyridostigmine does **not** antagonise phase I blocks (e.g., suxamethonium, decamethonium) and may prolong them. It can reverse fully established phase II blocks [Australian].
*   **Atropine:** Antagonises muscarinic effects; used to treat toxicity but may mask signs of cholinergic crisis [Australian].
*   **Aminoglycoside Antibiotics:** May interfere with neuromuscular transmission; pyridostigmine doses may require upward adjustment [Australian].
*   **Dexpanthenol:** Theoretically may have additive effects by increasing acetylcholine production [Australian].
*   **Methocarbamol:** May impair the therapeutic response to pyridostigmine [Australian].
*   **Cautionary Medications:** Local/general anaesthetics and anti-arrhythmic agents should be used with caution in myasthenic patients [Australian].
*   **Contraindicated in Overdose:** Patients with anticholinesterase poisoning should not receive aminophylline, morphine, phenothiazine tranquillisers, reserpine, or suxamethonium [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
*   **Pregnancy:** [Australian Category C]. Pyridostigmine crosses the placenta. Safety in human pregnancy is not fully established. Transient muscular weakness has been reported in 10–20% of neonates whose mothers received anticholinesterases [Australian].
*   **Breastfeeding:** Safety during lactation has not been established in humans [Australian].
*   **Fertility:** Data not available [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
*   **Paediatric Use:** Use is not recommended due to insufficient clinical experience in this population [Australian].
*   **Elderly Use:** Specific data are not available in current source documents [Australian].
*   **Renal Impairment:** Because the drug is primarily excreted unchanged by the kidneys, lower doses and careful titration to effect are required [Australian].
*   **Hepatic Impairment:** Not specified in available sources.

## 10. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of Action
Pyridostigmine is a reversible inhibitor of the enzyme cholinesterase. It prevents the hydrolysis of acetylcholine, facilitating the transmission of impulses at the neuromuscular junction and other cholinergic synapses. It also exerts a direct cholinomimetic effect on skeletal muscle [Australian].

### Pharmacokinetics
*   **Absorption:** Poor and variable. Approximately 40% of an oral dose is absorbed, with significant degradation occurring in the GI tract. Peak absorption occurs in the duodenum [Australian].
*   **Distribution:** Distributed to extracellular fluid; does **not** enter the CNS. Plasma levels range from 20 to 60 ng/mL [Australian].
*   **Metabolism:** Extensively metabolised in the liver. The primary metabolite is 3-hydroxy-N-methyl-pyridinium bromide [Australian].
*   **Elimination:** Primarily renal (95% of metabolites excreted in urine) via filtration and secretion [Australian].
*   **Half-life:** Oral half-life is variable, ranging from 1.5 to 4.25 hours [Australian].
