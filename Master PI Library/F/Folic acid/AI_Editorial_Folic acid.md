# Editorial Product Information: Folic Acid

## Editorial Note
- Contributing Jurisdictions: Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)
- This document provides a harmonised editorial overview for healthcare professionals based on the provided Product Information (PI) for **folic acid** injection. 
- **Note:** This is an editorial synthesis and not a substitute for the official, jurisdiction-specific PI. Clinicians should refer to local regulations for prescribing and legal compliance. At the time of this synthesis, only the Australian PI was provided for review.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Folic acid; (2S)-2-[[4-[[(2-amino-4-oxo-1,4-dihydropteridin-6-yl)methyl]amino] benzoyl]amino]pentanedioic acid.
- **Brand Names:** Biological Therapies Folic Acid [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** Not specified in available sources.
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - Injection: 5 mg in 1 mL [Australian].
- **Routes of Administration:** Intramuscular (IM), subcutaneous (SC), and intravenous (IV).
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Biological Therapies [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- Correction of folate deficiency where oral intake is insufficient or contraindicated due to malabsorption disorders, gastrointestinal pathologies, alcoholism, chronic infection, or exfoliative dermatitis.
- Treatment of megaloblastic anaemia, provided the condition is confirmed to be due to folate deficiency only.
- Prophylaxis and prevention of folic acid deficiency during pregnancy and lactation.

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adult)
- **Megaloblastic Anaemia:** 1–5 mg daily. Dosage should be adjusted based on the severity of the anaemia and the presence of malabsorption.
- **Prophylaxis (Pregnancy/Lactation):** 500 micrograms daily has been shown to prevent deficiency in well-nourished patients.

### Paediatric Dosing
- **General Megaloblastic Anaemia:** 1–5 mg daily. **Note:** Vitamin B12 deficiency must be excluded before initiating this supra-physiological dose.
- **Pre-term infants (<2 kg body weight):** 100–200 micrograms daily (oral or IM).
- **Infants (5–12 months):** 50 micrograms daily (oral or IM).

### Administration Instructions
- **IM/SC:** May be administered undiluted.
- **IV:** Must be diluted before use. 
- **Dilution Protocol [Australian]:** Add 1 mL (5 mg) of the injection to 49 mL of normal saline or 5% glucose to achieve a concentration of 100 micrograms/mL.
- **Single Use:** The product contains no antimicrobial agents; discard any unused portion.

## 4. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to **folic acid** or any excipients (e.g., disodium edetate).
- Prescribing for megaloblastic anaemia secondary to Vitamin B12 deficiency.

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Vitamin B12 Masking:** Folic acid may correct haematological markers of Vitamin B12 deficiency while allowing progressive and irreversible neurological damage to continue. B12 deficiency must be ruled out prior to treatment.
- **Seizure Control:** Patients on diphenylhydantoin (phenytoin) require monitoring; high doses of folic acid may interfere with seizure control.
- **Folate-Dependent Tumours:** Use with caution in patients with a history of or active folate-dependent malignancies.
- **Dihydrofolate Reductase Inhibitors:** Folic acid will not correct deficiencies caused by these agents (e.g., methotrexate). Leucovorin (folinic acid) must be used instead.
- **B12 Depletion:** Large, continuous doses of folic acid may reduce serum Vitamin B12 concentrations.

## 6. Adverse Effects
Folic acid is generally well-tolerated. No side effects other than allergic reactions have been reported at doses up to 10 times the RDA for one month.

| Frequency | Adverse Event |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Rare (<0.1%)** | Allergic reactions: Bronchospasm (wheezing, dyspnoea), erythema, fever, general malaise, skin rash, or pruritus. |
| **Observed at 15mg/day** | Abdominal bloating, gas, anorexia, nausea, altered taste, excitement, irritability, confusion, depression, impaired judgment, and sleep disturbances. |

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- **Anticonvulsants (Hydantoins, Carbamazepine, Phenobarbital, Primidone):** Folic acid may decrease serum levels of these drugs by increasing their metabolism or antagonising CNS effects, potentially increasing seizure frequency. Conversely, these drugs may increase folate requirements.
- **Folate Antagonists:** Methotrexate, pyrimethamine, triamterene, and trimethoprim inhibit dihydrofolate reductase. Folic acid is ineffective against these; leucovorin calcium is required.

### Pharmacokinetic Interactions
- **Absorption Interference:** 
    - **Antacids (Al/Mg containing):** May lower small intestine pH and decrease folate absorption. Space doses by at least 2 hours.
    - **Cholestyramine:** May interfere with folate absorption. Folic acid should be taken 1 hour before or 4–6 hours after cholestyramine.
    - **Sulfasalazine:** Inhibits folate absorption; requirements may increase.
- **Zinc:** Folate may decrease zinc absorption, though data is inconsistent.
- **Alcohol:** Chronic alcohol consumption decreases folate absorption.
- **Laboratory Tests:** Antibiotics may cause falsely low results in microbiologic assays for serum/erythrocyte folate.

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** 
    - **Category A [Australian]:** Drugs taken by a large number of pregnant women without proven increase in malformations or harmful effects on the foetus. 
- **Breastfeeding:** Folic acid is actively excreted in breast milk. It is indicated for supplementation in lactating women if they are folate deficient.
- **Fertility:** No data available in source documents.

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Dosing is established for infants and children (see Section 3).
- **Elderly Use:** No specific data available; general adult dosing applies with clinical caution.
- **Renal/Hepatic Impairment:** Specific dose adjustments are not provided in the source documentation. Metabolism is primarily hepatic.

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Folic acid is a precursor to tetrahydrofolic acid, a coenzyme for one-carbon transfer reactions. It is essential for the synthesis of purine and pyrimidine bases, amino acid metabolism (e.g., methionine, histidine), and the maturation of rapidly proliferating tissues (bone marrow, GI tract).
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Distribution:** Extensively protein-bound in plasma. The primary storage site is the liver.
    - **Metabolism:** Converted in the liver and plasma to the active form (tetrahydrofolic acid) by dihydrofolate reductase; requires ascorbic acid for this conversion.
    - **Excretion:** Undergoes enterohepatic circulation. Approximately 4–5 micrograms are excreted in the urine daily; higher doses result in proportionately higher urinary excretion.

## 11. Clinical Trials
- **Summary:** No clinical trial data provided in the source documents. 
- **Overdose Study:** A double-blind trial using 15 mg/day for one month in healthy volunteers showed no signs of toxicity.

## 12. Storage Conditions
- **Temperature:** Store at 2°C to 8°C (Refrigerate).
- **Precautions:** Do not freeze. Protect from light. Keep out of reach of children.

## 13. Regulatory Information by Jurisdiction
- **Australian Classification:** Schedule 4 (S4) – Prescription Only Medicine.
