# Editorial Product Information: fluocinolone acetonide

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **fluocinolone acetonide**
- Chemical class: Synthetic corticosteroid (glucocorticoid)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravitreal (implant)

## 2. Regulatory History
- Initial U.S. approval: 1963 [US]
- Marketing start date for the intravitreal implant product described: 05/17/2023 [US]
- Most recent PI revision dates reported in source: Revised 1/2026 and Revised 2/2026 [US]
- Withdrawal/suspension/restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- Registrations/approvals in jurisdictions other than the United States: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of chronic non‑infectious uveitis affecting the posterior segment of the eye. (Indication as stated in available sources.) [US]

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Single intravitreal administration of an implant containing 0.18 mg of fluocinolone acetonide. The implant is designed for sustained release with an initial release rate of approximately 0.25 µg/day and duration of effect up to 36 months.

### Dosing by indication
- Chronic non‑infectious posterior segment uveitis: single intravitreal implant (0.18 mg). No alternative dosing schedules, repeated-dose regimens, or systemic dosing provided in available sources.

### Administration instructions
- The intravitreal injection procedure must be performed under aseptic conditions.
- Provide adequate topical and/or subconjunctival anesthesia at the injection site (inferotemporal quadrant is recommended).
- Use a broad‑spectrum antiseptic; instill 2–3 drops into the lower fornix and allow 30–60 seconds to dry prior to injection. Lids may be scrubbed with antiseptic‑soaked applicators and use a sterile eyelid speculum.
- Measure approximately 4 mm inferotemporal from the limbus for the scleral entry point (optimal implant placement is inferior to the optic disc and posterior to the equator).
- Maintain sterility of the implant applicator; handle the applicator per manufacturer technique (do not grasp the plunger; inspect needle tip; keep applicator tip above horizontal plane prior to insertion to prevent implant loss).
- Displace conjunctiva to create offset entry tract, insert needle through conjunctiva and sclera to the positive stop, depress plunger fully to deliver implant into the vitreous, withdraw needle, and dispose of sharps appropriately.
- After removal of applicator, perform indirect ophthalmoscopy to confirm central retinal artery perfusion, absence of immediate complications, and to verify implant placement. Scleral depression may assist visualization.

### Titration schedules
- Not applicable / Not specified in available sources.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not applicable (single‑administration implant); further management not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Active or suspected ocular or periocular infections, including but not limited to:
  - Active epithelial herpes simplex keratitis (dendritic keratitis)
  - Vaccinia
  - Varicella
  - Mycobacterial infections
  - Fungal ocular infections
- Known hypersensitivity to fluocinolone acetonide or any component of the implant/device
- Any other contraindication specific to other jurisdictions: Not specified in available sources

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / strong warnings
- Not reported as a separate boxed warning in the available sources. Clinically significant risks are summarized below. [US]

### General warnings and precautions
- Intravitreal injection‑related risks: endophthalmitis, ocular inflammation, increased or decreased intraocular pressure (IOP), choroidal or retinal detachments, hypotony (reported within 24 hours and resolved within 2 weeks in some cases).
- Corticosteroid‑related effects: development of posterior subcapsular cataracts, increased IOP potentially leading to glaucoma, and enhanced susceptibility to secondary ocular infections (bacterial, fungal, viral). Corticosteroids are not recommended in patients with a history of ocular herpes simplex because of potential for viral reactivation.
- Implant migration: patients with an absent or torn posterior lens capsule are at risk for implant migration into the anterior chamber. Migration may lead to corneal edema or other complications.
- Perforation risk: perforation of the globe can occur where there is thinning of cornea or sclera.
- Systemic exposure and systemic corticosteroid effects following intravitreal implant: not quantified; systemic effects are possible in theory but not specified.

### Monitoring requirements
- Immediately post‑injection: verify central retinal artery perfusion.
- Tonometry: may be measured within 30 minutes after injection at clinician discretion.
- Early follow‑up: biomicroscopy between 2 and 7 days after injection to assess for signs of infection or other complications.
- Ongoing: monitor intraocular pressure and for signs/symptoms of endophthalmitis or other ocular complications during follow‑up visits; monitor visual acuity.
- Instruct patients to report immediately any ocular redness, pain, photophobia, or changes in vision.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Use with caution in phakic patients because of high risk of cataract formation; counsel patients about potential need for cataract surgery.
- Be cautious in patients with pre‑existing glaucoma or ocular hypertension since steroid treatment may cause substantial IOP elevations requiring medical or surgical management.
- Do not administer in eyes with active infection.
- Evaluate integrity of posterior lens capsule prior to use; avoid in eyes with a deficient posterior capsule when migration to anterior chamber would risk corneal damage.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Pharmacokinetic interactions: Not specified in available sources for intravitreal fluocinolone acetonide implants.
- Pharmacodynamic interactions:
  - Concurrent use of other ocular steroids would be expected to have additive corticosteroid effects (IOP elevation, cataract risk), but formal interaction data are not specified.
  - Use of IOP‑lowering medications was common in clinical trials (43% of patients required IOP‑lowering agents); no formal interaction guidance beyond standard clinical management is provided.
- Clinically significant interactions with management notes: Not specified in available sources beyond monitoring for steroid‑related ocular effects.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Adequate and well‑controlled studies in pregnant women are not available.
- Animal reproduction studies with this intravitreal implant product were not conducted.
- Systemically administered corticosteroids have produced teratogenic effects in laboratory animals at relatively low systemic exposures; therefore, intravitreal fluocinolone acetonide should be administered in pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus.
- Pregnancy risk classification systems (e.g., Australian letter categories, US letter categories) and jurisdictional categories: Not specified in available sources beyond the above risk summary.

### Breastfeeding
- Systemically administered corticosteroids are present in human milk and can suppress growth and interfere with endogenous corticosteroid production in infants.
- It is not known whether intravitreal administration results in sufficient systemic absorption to produce detectable levels of fluocinolone acetonide in human milk or whether there are effects on a breastfed infant or milk production.
- Decisions should weigh the benefits of breastfeeding, the mother’s clinical need for therapy, and potential risks to the infant.

### Fertility
- Effect on human fertility: Not specified in available sources. Nonclinical fertility studies with the implant were not conducted.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **fluocinolone acetonide** is a corticosteroid that inhibits multiple components of the inflammatory response. Corticosteroids reduce edema, inhibit leukocyte migration and capillary dilation, limit fibrin deposition and fibroblast proliferation, and decrease formation of scar tissue. They exert effects in part by inducing inhibitory proteins (lipocortins) that inhibit phospholipase A2 and thereby reduce synthesis of inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes.

### Pharmacokinetics
- The intravitreal implant contains 0.18 mg fluocinolone acetonide and is designed to release drug at an initial rate of approximately 0.25 µg/day with sustained delivery up to 36 months.
- Physicochemical properties: fluocinolone acetonide is a white or almost white microcrystalline powder, practically insoluble in water, soluble in methanol, ethanol, chloroform and acetone.
- Systemic absorption after intravitreal implantation, plasma pharmacokinetics, distribution, metabolism and routes/timing of elimination are not specified in available sources.
- Whether intravitreal dosing yields measurable systemic levels or systemic corticosteroid effects is not specified.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Pharmacodynamic effects are consistent with glucocorticoid receptor‑mediated anti‑inflammatory actions described above (reduction in intraocular inflammation).

## 12. Clinical Trials
### Trial designs and key results
- Efficacy was demonstrated in randomized, double‑masked, multicenter trials enrolling patients with non‑infectious uveitis affecting the posterior segment.
- Studies 1 and 2: randomized 2:1 (implant:sham), primary endpoint was proportion of patients experiencing uveitis recurrence in the study eye within 6 months; recurrence also assessed at 12 months. Recurrence was defined by deterioration in visual acuity, vitreous haze attributable to uveitis, or need for rescue medications.
  - Study 1 (YUTIQ N=87; Sham N=42): recurrence within 6 months — 16/87 (18%) implant vs 33/42 (79%) sham. Difference ≈ 60% absolute reduction (95% CI reported in source). P < 0.01.
  - Study 2 (YUTIQ N=101; Sham N=52): recurrence within 6 months — 22/101 (22%) implant vs 28/52 (54%) sham. Difference ≈ 32% absolute reduction. P < 0.01.
  - Recurrence within 12 months was also substantially lower in implant treated eyes in both studies.
- Study 3: multicenter, randomized, masked trial where all patients received the implant with different applicators; safety data were collected.
- Clinical benefit: the implant substantially reduced the rate of uveitis recurrence in the study eye over 6 and 12 months compared with sham injection in pivotal trials.
- Safety observations in trials included increased incidence of cataract (in phakic eyes), IOP elevation often requiring topical IOP‑lowering medications, and small proportions requiring surgical intervention for elevated IOP.

### Nonclinical toxicology
- Carcinogenicity and long‑term fertility studies were not conducted with the implant product.
- Fluocinolone acetonide was not genotoxic in the Ames test, the mouse lymphoma TK assay, or in vivo mouse bone marrow micronucleus assay (per available source).
