# Editorial Product Information: Hydromorphone Hydrochloride

## Editorial Note
- **Jurisdictions Contributed:** Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial synthesis of the Product Information (PI) for hydromorphone hydrochloride, designed for use by healthcare professionals. 
- **Disclaimer:** This is an editorial summary and does not replace the official, legally approved Product Information required for prescribing in any specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should consult the current official PI for their region before prescribing.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Hydromorphone hydrochloride (a hydrogenated ketone of morphine).
- **Chemical Class:** Opioid analgesic; $\mu$-opioid agonist.
- **Brand Names:** Dilaudid [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** N02AA03.
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - Immediate-release tablets: 2 mg (orange, round), 4 mg (yellow, round), 8 mg (white, triangular/bisected) [Australian].
- **Route of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Mundipharma Pty Limited [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Severe Pain:** Short-term management of severe pain where alternative treatment options (e.g., non-opioid analgesics) are inadequate, not tolerated, or contraindicated [Australian-approved].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adult)
- **Non-opioid-tolerant patients:** The recommended initial oral dose is 2 mg to 4 mg every four hours [Australian].
- **Individualisation:** Dosing must be titrated based on pain severity, patient response, and prior opioid experience.

### Administration Instructions
- **Food Effects:** Absorption data is limited; however, for chronic use, patients should maintain consistency regarding food intake (either always with food or always on an empty stomach) [Australian].

### Titration and Conversion
- **Switching from other opioids:** Dosing should be based on the prior daily opioid dose using equianalgesic tables. When converting, it is conservative practice to start with 1/2 to 2/3 of the estimated equianalgesic hydromorphone dose [Australian].
- **Relative Potency:** Oral hydromorphone is approximately one-fifth as potent as intramuscular (IM) hydromorphone in single-dose studies, but this ratio may shift toward 1:2 with chronic dosing [Australian].

### Ceasing Therapy
- **Tapering:** Do not discontinue abruptly in physically dependent patients. Reductions of 10% to 25% every 2 to 4 weeks are generally recommended to avoid withdrawal [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to hydromorphone or excipients (e.g., lactose, sulfites).
- Severe or acute respiratory depression or obstructive airway disease.
- Status asthmaticus.
- Paralytic ileus.
- Concurrent use of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) or use within 14 days of stopping MAOIs.
- Pregnancy and labour for delivery of premature infants [Australian].
- Paediatric use (premature infants and children) [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed Warnings [Australian]
- **Hazardous and Harmful Use:** Risk of addiction, abuse, and misuse leading to overdose and death.
- **Life-threatening Respiratory Depression:** Serious or fatal depression may occur; monitoring is critical during initiation and dose increases.
- **CNS Depressants:** Concomitant use with benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or alcohol may result in profound sedation, coma, and death.

### General Warnings
- **Sleep-Related Breathing Disorders:** Opioids can cause central sleep apnoea (CSA) and sleep-related hypoxemia in a dose-dependent manner.
- **Adrenal Insufficiency:** Reported primarily with long-term use; symptoms include nausea, fatigue, and hypotension.
- **Hyperalgesia:** Increased pain sensitivity may occur at high doses; this should be distinguished from tolerance.
- **Head Injury/Increased Intracranial Pressure:** Hydromorphone may exaggerate the effects of CO2 retention and obscure the clinical course of intracranial lesions.
- **Hepatobiliary Disorders:** May cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi; use with caution in biliary tract disease or pancreatitis.

## 6. Adverse Effects
Adverse effects are typical of $\mu$-opioid agonists. Frequency data is as follows [Australian]:

| Frequency | Adverse Effects |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Very Common (>10%)** | Constipation, nausea, dizziness, somnolence. |
| **Common (1–10%)** | Hypotension, abdominal pain, dry mouth, vomiting, asthenia, headache, sedation, anxiety, insomnia, pruritus, sweating. |
| **Uncommon (0.1–1%)** | Bradycardia, palpitations, tachycardia, blurred vision, diplopia, cramps, diarrhoea, paralytic ileus, biliary colic, myoclonus, seizures, hallucinations, urinary retention. |
| **Rare (<0.1%)** | Lethargy. |
| **Not Known** | Adrenal insufficiency, androgen deficiency, Central Sleep Apnoea, spasm of the sphincter of Oddi, toxic leukoencephalopathy (overdose). |

## 7. Drug Interactions
- **CNS Depressants:** Alcohol, benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants, and antipsychotics increase the risk of respiratory depression and death.
- **Gabapentinoids (Gabapentin/Pregabalin):** Increased risk of fatal respiratory depression [Australian].
- **MAOIs:** Contraindicated; risk of severe hypertensive crisis or CNS excitation.
- **Neuromuscular Blockers:** Hydromorphone may enhance the respiratory depressant effects of these agents.

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** Australian Category C. Opioids may cause respiratory depression in the neonate. Long-term use may lead to Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome. Teratogenic effects were seen in hamsters at high doses (14 mg/kg) but not in rats or rabbits [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Hydromorphone is detected in human milk at low levels. Breastfeeding is generally not recommended during treatment [Australian].
- **Fertility:** No adverse effects on fertility were observed in rat studies [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric:** Safety and efficacy have not been established in children [Australian].
- **Elderly:** Patients may exhibit twice the sensitivity of younger adults. Lower initial doses and careful titration are required.
- **Renal Impairment:** Clearance is reduced. The metabolite **hydromorphone-3-glucuronide (H3G)** can accumulate (levels >4x higher than normal), potentially causing neuroexcitatory or psychomimetic reactions [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Initial doses should be reduced due to potential for decreased metabolism.

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Pure opioid agonist acting primarily on $\mu$-receptors in the CNS and smooth muscle.
- **Pharmacodynamics:** Approximately 8 times as potent as morphine by weight. It depresses the respiratory reflex by acting on the brain stem and causes constipation by reducing GI motility.
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Bioavailability:** ~25% (extensive first-pass metabolism).
    - **Metabolism:** Primarily glucuronidated in the liver to H3G.
    - **Distribution:** Extensive tissue uptake (Vd = 301 L); crosses the placenta.
    - **Elimination:** Plasma half-life ~2.3–2.6 hours. Excreted primarily in the urine.
