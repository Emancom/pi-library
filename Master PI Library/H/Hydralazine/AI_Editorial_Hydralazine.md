# Editorial Product Information: Hydralazine

## Editorial Note
- This editorial synthesis is based on the official Product Information (PI) provided by the following jurisdiction: **Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)**.
- The purpose of this document is to provide a harmonised editorial overview for healthcare professionals, consolidating regulatory information into a clinically actionable format.
- **Note:** This document is an editorial synthesis and does not substitute for the official PI required for prescribing in any specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should refer to local guidelines and the specific approved PI for their region.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Hydralazine hydrochloride (a peripheral vasodilator).
- **Brand Name(s):** **Alphapress** [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** C02DB02.
- **Dosage Form(s) and Strength(s):** 
  - 25 mg tablets: Cream, flat bevelled edged, scored [Australian].
  - 50 mg tablets: Pink, film-coated, convex [Australian].
- **Route(s) of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Alphapharm Pty Ltd (trading as Viatris) [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Hypertension:** Specifically indicated for moderate to severe, drug-resistant hypertension [Australian].
- **Combination Therapy:** Approved as supplementary medication for use alongside other antihypertensives, such as beta-blockers and diuretics, to enhance efficacy and mitigate compensatory side effects [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
- **General Dosing (Adult):** Dosage must be individualised. Treatment should commence at low doses and be titrated stepwise to achieve optimal effect while minimising adverse reactions.
- **Standard Regimen:** 
  - **Starting Dose:** 25 mg twice daily [Australian].
  - **Maintenance Range:** 50 mg to 200 mg daily [Australian].
- **Dose Limitation:** The daily dose should not exceed 100 mg without first determining the patient's acetylator phenotype, due to the increased risk of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)-like syndrome in slow acetylators [Australian].
- **Administration:** Administer in a twice-daily regimen [Australian].
- **Missed Dose:** Not specified in available sources.

## 4. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to hydralazine or dihydralazine [Australian].
- Idiopathic systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and related connective tissue diseases [Australian].
- Severe tachycardia and high-output heart failure (e.g., thyrotoxicosis) [Australian].
- Myocardial insufficiency due to mechanical obstruction (e.g., aortic or mitral stenosis, or constrictive pericarditis) [Australian].
- Isolated right ventricular heart failure resulting from pulmonary hypertension (cor pulmonale) [Australian].
- Dissecting aortic aneurysm [Australian].
- Porphyria [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Boxed Warnings:** None specified in available sources.
- **Cardiac Dysfunction:** Hydralazine induces a hyperdynamic circulatory state (reflex tachycardia and increased stroke volume) which may provoke or aggravate angina pectoris or myocardial infarction. Use with extreme caution in suspected coronary artery disease; patients should ideally be pre-treated with a beta-blocker for several days before starting hydralazine. Avoid use in heart failure or until post-infarction stabilisation is achieved [Australian].
- **SLE-like Syndrome:** Prolonged use (typically >6 months) can provoke an SLE-like syndrome, particularly at doses exceeding 100 mg/day and in slow acetylators or women. Mild forms present as rheumatoid-like symptoms (arthralgia, fever); severe forms include pleurisy, effusions, and pericarditis. The drug must be withdrawn immediately if clinical signs appear [Australian].
- **Systemic Vasculitis:** May induce ANCA-positive vasculitis, potentially leading to pulmonary renal syndrome (alveolar haemorrhage and glomerulonephritis). This condition can be fulminant and fatal [Australian].
- **Peripheral Neuritis:** Cases of paraesthesia have been reported; symptoms may respond to pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) or drug withdrawal [Australian].
- **Monitoring:** For long-term therapy, check antinuclear factors (ANF), full blood count, and urinalysis every 6 months [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
Adverse effects are often dose-dependent and commonly occur at the start of treatment or during rapid titration.

| Frequency | Adverse Effects |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Very Common (>10%)** | Tachycardia, palpitation, headache. |
| **Common (1–10%)** | Flushing, hypotension, angina pectoris, gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea), arthralgia, myalgia, positive ANF test. |
| **Uncommon (0.1–1%)** | Anaemia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, oedema, congestive heart failure, jaundice, hepatomegaly, SLE-like syndrome, rash, pruritus, dyspnoea, proteinuria, haematuria. |
| **Rare (<0.1%)** | Not specified in available sources. |
| **Very Rare (<0.01%)** | Haemolytic anaemia, agranulocytosis, exophthalmos, paralytic ileus, acute renal failure, depression, hallucinations, tremor. |

- **Serious Adverse Effects:** Systemic vasculitis, pulmonary renal syndrome, and severe SLE-like manifestations (pericarditis/pleurisy) [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
- **Pharmacodynamic Interactions:**
  - **Potentiation:** Antihypertensive effects are increased by other vasodilators, calcium antagonists, ACE inhibitors, diuretics, tricyclic antidepressants, anaesthetics, major tranquillisers, MAOIs, and alcohol [Australian].
  - **Diazoxide:** Concurrent use may lead to severe hypotension [Australian].
  - **Antagonism:** Hypotensive effects may be reduced by oestrogens or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) [Australian].
  - **Adrenaline (Epinephrine):** Adrenaline may enhance the cardiac accelerating effects of hydralazine. Do not use adrenaline to treat hydralazine-induced hypotension [Australian].
- **Pharmacokinetic Interactions:**
  - **Beta-blockers:** Hydralazine may increase the bioavailability of beta-blockers subject to high first-pass metabolism (e.g., propranolol, metoprolol), necessitating dose adjustments [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** 
  - **Category C [Australian]:** Hydralazine crosses the placenta. Intravenous use in the third trimester has been linked to fetal distress and cardiac arrhythmia. 
  - **Recommendation:** Avoid use before the third trimester. Use in the third trimester only if the maternal benefit outweighs the fetal risk [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Hydralazine is excreted into breast milk. Alternative therapies should be considered unless the benefits clearly outweigh the risks to the infant [Australian].
- **Fertility:** Human data are unavailable; however, animal studies show potential teratogenicity (cleft palate, skeletal malformations) at maternotoxic doses [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Safety and efficacy have not been established; use is not recommended in children [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Increased risk of impaired renal elimination; dose adjustments may be necessary [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Reduce dosage or increase dosing intervals in patients with significant impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min or serum creatinine >221 micromol/L) to avoid accumulation of the active substance [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Dose adjustments are required based on clinical response to avoid drug accumulation [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Direct relaxation of vascular smooth muscle, primarily in the arterioles. This reduces peripheral resistance and decreases blood pressure (diastolic more than systolic) [Australian].
- **Pharmacodynamics:** Preferential arteriolar dilation minimises postural hypotension. It triggers a significant reflex sympathetic response (increased heart rate and cardiac output) and may cause sodium/fluid retention [Australian].
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
  - **Absorption:** Rapid and complete; peak plasma levels reached within 1 hour [Australian].
  - **Metabolism:** Significant dose-dependent first-pass metabolism. Acetylation is the primary pathway; "slow acetylators" have higher plasma levels and a higher risk of toxicity [Australian].
  - **Distribution:** 88–90% protein bound (mainly albumin). High affinity for muscle tissue in arterial walls [Australian].
  - **Elimination:** Plasma half-life is 2–3 hours (45 minutes in rapid acetylators). 80% of an oral dose is recovered in urine within 24 hours as metabolites [Australian].
