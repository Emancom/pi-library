# Editorial Product Information: human albumin

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **human albumin**
- Chemical class: Plasma‑derived human serum albumin (human plasma protein)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous infusion

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Any withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Hypoproteinaemia in the acutely ill patient (administer when existing or anticipated clinical problems from reduced oncotic pressure are present and/or as an adjunct to diuretic therapy).
- Shock — as a plasma expander for resuscitation of patients in shock due to acute blood or plasma loss. (When available, lower‑concentration albumin preparations (4–5%) are preferred for initial resuscitation.)
- Burns — to maintain plasma volume and colloid oncotic pressure after initial crystalloid resuscitation; used beyond the first 24 hours to maintain colloid osmotic pressure.
- Adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) — adjunctive use together with diuretic therapy to control pulmonary interstitial oedema and maintain vital signs.
- Haemodialysis — to assist with rapid removal of excess extravascular fluid and to support perfusion pressure (typically administered at end of dialysis in fluid‑overloaded patients).
- Therapeutic plasma exchange — as a colloid replacement solution; concentrated albumin (20%) may be used if serum albumin is not maintained or when exchange is less frequent. Iso‑oncotic solutions are preferred; replacement is guided by clinical context and exchange frequency.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Strength referenced in available sources: 20% w/v (200 g/L) human albumin solution.
- Typical dosing must be individualized and determined by clinical response and monitoring.

### Dosing by indication (adult)
- Hypoproteinaemia in the acutely ill patient: Usual daily dose 50–75 g human albumin (equivalent to 250–375 mL of 20% solution). Do not exceed an infusion rate of 2 mL/min under routine conditions to avoid circulatory overload.
- Shock (blood volume expansion): Usual initial dose 20 g human albumin (100 mL of 20% solution). Administer at 2–4 mL/min; rate may be increased in emergencies. Repeat dose in 15–30 minutes if necessary. Total dose should not exceed approximately 2 g/kg body weight in the absence of active bleeding.
- Burns: Usual daily dose 20–80 g human albumin (100–400 mL of 20% solution), given at about 1 mL/min. Beyond 24 hours, use to maintain plasma albumin ≈25 g/L or colloid osmotic pressure ≈20 mmHg.
- ARDS: Commence with 50 g human albumin (250 mL of 20% solution) over the first 24 hours together with diuretics. Adjust subsequent dosing to maintain vital signs (central venous pressure, urine output, plasma albumin).
- Haemodialysis: 20–40 g human albumin (100–200 mL of 20% solution) may be given at the end of dialysis in patients with significant fluid overload.
- Plasma exchange: Replace albumin removed on a gram‑for‑gram basis (example provided: removal of 2.5 L plasma ≈ replacement with 125 g albumin = 625 mL of 20% solution), administered either prediluted or followed by 4–5 volumes of an appropriate crystalloid.

### Administration instructions
- Use intravenous infusion technique.
- This product contains no antimicrobial preservative. Use immediately after opening; discard any unused portion. Use for one patient on one occasion only.
- Inspect visually: normally clear or slightly opalescent; do not use if cloudy or turbid.
- Dilution: 20% albumin may be diluted to an iso‑oncotic concentration (4–5%) by mixing 1 mL of 20% albumin with 4 mL of a suitable crystalloid solution. Under no circumstances should water be used for dilution (risk of intravascular haemolysis from hypotonic solution).
- If refrigerated, allow to reach room temperature before administration. Do not use if frozen.
- Record product name and batch number each time the product is administered to maintain traceability.
- Monitoring during infusion (see Section 6): blood pressure, signs of circulatory overload, myocardial function parameters (central venous pressure/arterial pressure/pulse), and laboratory monitoring as appropriate.

### Titration schedules
- Titrate dose and infusion rate to clinical response and monitoring parameters (blood pressure, central venous pressure, urine output, signs of pulmonary oedema).
- In shock and emergencies, infusion rate may be increased temporarily; thereafter revert to monitored administration.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources

## 5. Contraindications
- History of allergy to human albumin or any component of the product.
- Cardiac failure.
- Pulmonary oedema.
- Severe anaemia.
- Infusion not justified (contraindicated in this clinical sense) for hypoproteinaemic states associated with:
  - Chronic cirrhosis
  - Malabsorption
  - Protein losing enteropathies
  - Pancreatic insufficiency
  - Undernutrition
- In chronic nephrosis, administration of concentrated albumin (20%) provides no relief of chronic oedema because the albumin is promptly excreted; infusion is therefore not indicated in this situation.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- Not specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Circulatory overload: The colloid osmotic effect of concentrated albumin is markedly greater than plasma; monitor rate and volume closely to avoid circulatory overload. Patients with cardiac failure, pulmonary oedema, renal insufficiency, severe or unstable chronic anaemia, or those on cardiopulmonary bypass are at higher risk and require careful dose and rate adjustment.
- If signs of circulatory overload develop (headache, dyspnoea, jugular venous distension, increased blood pressure, raised venous pressure associated with pulmonary oedema), stop the infusion immediately.
- In the presence of dehydration, concentrated albumin must be given with, or followed by, appropriate crystalloid solution to avoid tissue dehydration.
- Myocardial depression: Administration of albumin can aggravate myocardial depression in shock — monitor cardiac function.
- Traumatic brain injury: Use of albumin for fluid resuscitation in patients with traumatic brain injury is not recommended.
- Sodium content: Product sodium concentration ranges 48–100 mmol/L; consider this for patients requiring sodium restriction.
- Aluminium content: Product may contain trace aluminium (documented ≤200 µg/L). Accumulation in patients with chronic renal insufficiency can produce toxic effects (e.g., osteodystrophy, anaemia, encephalopathy). When large volumes are contemplated in such patients, weigh the risks and benefits carefully.
- Allergic/hypersensitivity reactions: Hypersensitivity reactions may occur. Preparedness to stop infusion and treat anaphylaxis (epinephrine, corticosteroids, antihistamines) is required.
- Infectious agent risk: Product manufactured from human plasma; despite donor screening and viral inactivation/removal steps (e.g., pasteurisation and low pH incubation noted in manufacturing), residual risk of transmission of infectious agents, including viruses and (theoretically) Creutzfeldt‑Jakob Disease agents, remains. Consider vaccination where indicated for patients receiving plasma‑derived medicinal products.
- Use in one patient only; sterility concerns require immediate use after opening.

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor blood pressure frequently during administration.
- Monitor for symptoms/signs of circulatory overload.
- Monitor myocardial function (central venous pressure, arterial pressure, pulse rate) as indicated.
- Laboratory monitoring: plasma electrolytes, prothrombin time, biochemistry, haematological indices.
- Maintain batch number traceability for every administration.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Avoid rapid infusion except in true emergencies; slower infusion reduces risk of pulmonary oedema and new haemorrhage in injured patients due to rises in blood pressure.
- Paradoxical refractory oliguria has been reported in burns patients receiving albumin, possibly because of insufficient accompanying crystalloids — ensure adequate crystalloid administration when indicated.
- Use with ACE inhibitors: hypotension has been reported in patients on ACE inhibitors (see Section 8).

- Post‑marketing surveillance (current generation 20% product, as reported in sources):
  - Most commonly reported reactions: chills and fever (low number of reports overall).
  - Other reported events in routine surveillance: hypotension, hypertension, tachycardia, decreased oxygen saturation, dyspnoea, flushing, dizziness, chills, pyrexia, muscle spasms.

- Serious adverse effects highlighted:
  - Anaphylactic reactions and anaphylactic shock (very rare).
  - Circulatory overload leading to pulmonary oedema (risk increased in susceptible patients).
  - Infectious disease transmission (theoretical residual risk despite manufacturing inactivation/removal steps).

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Pharmacodynamic interactions:
  - Hypotension has been reported in patients receiving albumin who are concomitantly taking angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors — monitor blood pressure; consider temporary withholding of ACE inhibitor if clinically indicated.
- Pharmacokinetic interactions:
  - Not specifically described in available sources; addition of other medicines to the albumin solution has not been evaluated (compatibility unknown).
- Incompatibilities:
  - Mixing with water for injection is contraindicated (dilution with water may cause intravascular haemolysis because of low tonicity).
  - Other admixtures: Not specified in available sources — do not add medications unless compatibility and stability are known.
- Management notes:
  - When giving concentrated albumin (>5%) ensure adequate crystalloid infusion to avoid dehydration of tissues.
  - Observe for additive effects on circulatory load when used with other volume‑expanding therapies.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- Pregnancy:
  - Reproductive toxicity studies in animals: Not conducted (impracticable due to antibody development to human albumin in animal models).
  - Clinical data in pregnancy: Use in human pregnancy not established in controlled clinical trials; should be given in pregnancy only if clearly needed.
  - Pregnancy category/systems (jurisdictional): Not specified in available sources.
- Breastfeeding:
  - Like endogenous albumin, human albumin may be excreted in breast milk; no safety information is available from studies.
- Fertility:
  - No studies examining fertility effects have been conducted; not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **human albumin** provides maintenance of plasma colloid (oncotic) pressure and functions as a carrier for endogenous and exogenous substances. The oncotic effect of concentrated albumin (20%) supplies the oncotic equivalence of approximately four times its volume of human plasma.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Distribution:
  - Total exchangeable albumin pool ≈4–5 g/kg body weight; approximately 40–45% intravascular, 55–60% extravascular under normal conditions.
  - Increased capillary permeability (e.g., severe burns, septic shock) alters albumin kinetics and distribution.
  - In healthy subjects, <10% of infused albumin leaves the intravascular compartment during the first 2 hours post‑infusion (considerable interindividual variability).
- Metabolism and elimination:
  - Metabolic half‑life of albumin in vivo ≈19 days; turnover in adult ≈15 g/day.
  - Elimination predominantly by intracellular catabolism (lysosomal proteases); balance of synthesis and breakdown maintained by feedback regulation.
- Absorption:
  - Not applicable for intravenous administration.
- Pharmacodynamics:
  - Rapid interchange between intra‑ and extravascular spaces; primary therapeutic effect in resuscitation and plasma expansion is via restoration/maintenance of oncotic pressure.

### Viral inactivation/removal (manufacturing)
- Manufacturing steps described in sources include virus inactivation procedures (e.g., pasteurisation — 60°C for 10 hours — and low pH incubation) and other dedicated steps intended to reduce risk of viral transmission, including efficacy against enveloped viruses (HIV, HBV, HCV) and contributions to reduction/removal of non‑enveloped viruses (e.g., hepatitis A virus and parvovirus B19). Despite these measures, residual risk cannot be entirely excluded.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- The SAFE study (Saline versus Albumin Fluid Evaluation):
  - Design: Large multicentre, double‑blind, prospective randomized controlled trial in heterogeneous ICU patients comparing 4% albumin versus normal saline for fluid resuscitation. Exclusions included burns, plasmapheresis, cardiac bypass and liver transplant patients.
  - Size: 6997 patients randomized (Albumin 4% n = 3497; Saline n = 3500).
  - Primary outcome: Death from any cause within 28 days after randomization.
  - Results: 28‑day mortality — albumin group 726/3473 (20.9%); saline group 729/3460 (21.1%). Relative risk 0.99 (95% CI 0.91–1.09), p = 0.87. No statistically significant differences in secondary outcomes (ICU days, hospital days, days of ventilation, days of renal replacement therapy).
  - Fluid volumes: Those randomized to albumin received less fluid volume compared with saline in the first days (ratio albumin:saline ~1:1.4 over first 4 days).
  - Subgroup analyses: Trends noted — increased mortality signal in trauma patients with associated brain injury receiving albumin; trend toward better outcome in severe sepsis with albumin. These subgroup findings are to be interpreted cautiously and require specifically designed studies for confirmation.
- Post‑hoc analysis in traumatic brain injury:
  - A post‑hoc SAFE subgroup analysis (published) reported higher mortality among patients with severe traumatic brain injury (Glasgow Coma Score 3–8) randomized to 4% albumin versus saline. The analysis was post‑hoc with some retrospective data collection; causality and mechanisms remain uncertain.
- Other observations:
  - In earlier generations of albumin used in plasma exchange, small incidences of clinically significant increases in prothrombin time and reductions in potassium, calcium, bicarbonate, total serum protein and platelet count were reported — findings consistent with plasma exchange physiology.
