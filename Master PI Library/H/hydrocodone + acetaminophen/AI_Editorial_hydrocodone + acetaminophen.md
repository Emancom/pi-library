# Editorial Product Information: hydrocodone + acetaminophen

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **hydrocodone + acetaminophen**
- Chemical class:
  - Hydrocodone: opioid analgesic (full μ-opioid receptor agonist)
  - Acetaminophen: non-opiate, non-salicylate analgesic/antipyretic
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 3. Approved Indications
- Management of pain severe enough to require an opioid analgesic and for which alternative treatments are inadequate.  
  - Limitation of use: Reserve for patients who have not tolerated, or are not expected to tolerate, non-opioid analgesics or for whom non-opioid options have not provided adequate analgesia.  
- Jurisdictional variations of specific indications: Not specified in available sources beyond the general indication above.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Available combination strengths described in sources: hydrocodone 5 mg, 7.5 mg, and 10 mg each combined with acetaminophen 325 mg.  
- Dosing must be individualized and titrated to clinical effect while minimizing adverse effects.

### Dosing by indication
- Specific dose schedules by indication: Not specified in available sources.

### Administration instructions
- Administer orally.  
- Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with individual patient treatment goals and risks of opioid therapy.  
- When converting from another opioid, overestimation of hydrocodone dose can result in fatal overdose; perform conversion carefully and consider opioid tolerance.

### Titration schedules

## 5. Contraindications
- Significant respiratory depression.
- Acute or severe bronchial asthma in an unmonitored setting or in the absence of resuscitative equipment.
- Known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction, including paralytic ileus.
- Hypersensitivity (e.g., anaphylaxis) to hydrocodone, acetaminophen, or any component of the product.
- Any additional jurisdiction-specific contraindications: Not specified in available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / Highlighted Warnings
- **Addiction, Abuse, and Misuse:** Contains an opioid; exposes patients to risk of opioid addiction, abuse, and misuse which may lead to overdose and death. Assess risk prior to prescribing and reassess regularly.
- **Life-Threatening Respiratory Depression:** Serious, life‑threatening, or fatal respiratory depression can occur, especially on initiation or after dose increases. Close monitoring and proper titration required.
- **Accidental Ingestion:** Accidental ingestion of even one dose, particularly by children, can be fatal.
- **Concomitant Use with Benzodiazepines or Other CNS Depressants:** Combined use may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death. Reserve concomitant prescribing for when alternatives are inadequate; use lowest effective dosages and minimum durations if co-prescribed.
- **Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS):** Prolonged maternal use during pregnancy can result in neonatal withdrawal that may be life‑threatening if not recognized and treated.
- **Opioid Analgesic REMS [US]:** Healthcare providers are strongly encouraged to complete REMS-compliant education and counsel patients/caregivers on serious risks and safe use. [US]
- **Cytochrome P450 3A4 Interactions:** Concomitant use with CYP3A4 inhibitors may increase hydrocodone plasma concentrations and risk of respiratory depression; discontinuation of CYP3A4 inducers may increase hydrocodone concentrations.
- **Hepatotoxicity (Acetaminophen component):** Acute liver failure, sometimes resulting in liver transplant or death, has been reported with acetaminophen, most often with doses exceeding 4,000 mg/day or with use of multiple acetaminophen-containing products.

### General warnings and precautions
- Monitor patients for signs of respiratory depression, sedation, and hypotension. Carbon dioxide retention from opioid-induced respiratory depression may exacerbate sedation.
- Opioids can cause sleep-related breathing disorders including central sleep apnea and sleep-related hypoxemia; risk is dose-dependent.
- Use caution in patients with head injury, increased intracranial pressure, seizures, biliary tract disease (sphincter of Oddi spasm), severe hepatic or renal impairment, and in those taking concomitant CNS depressants.
- Chronic opioid use may cause endocrine effects (suppression of ACTH, cortisol, LH; stimulation of prolactin and GH) and may influence hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis leading to androgen deficiency (low libido, impotence, amenorrhea, infertility).
- Opioids may have modest immunosuppressive effects; clinical significance unknown.
- Advise patients about the dangers of concomitant alcohol and acetaminophen-containing products to avoid hepatotoxicity.

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor respiratory status, sedation level, and signs of addiction, abuse, or misuse during treatment.  
- Monitor liver function if clinically indicated or in cases of suspected acetaminophen overdose or chronic high-dose use.  
- Monitor for signs of neonatal opioid withdrawal when used long term during pregnancy.  
- Monitor patients receiving interacting drugs (CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers) for signs of respiratory depression or increased opioid effect.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Consider prescribing naloxone for emergency treatment of opioid overdose for patients at increased risk (e.g., concomitant CNS depressant use, history of substance use disorder, prior overdose, household contacts at risk) and provide education on its use. [US]
- Educate patients and caregivers on safe storage, disposal, recognition of overdose, and not to drive or operate machinery until effects of drug (and any co-administered CNS depressant) are known.
- Avoid co-prescription with benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants whenever possible; if unavoidable, use lowest effective doses and monitor closely.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- CYP3A4 inhibitors (examples: macrolide antibiotics, azole antifungals, protease inhibitors) may increase hydrocodone plasma concentrations and prolong opioid adverse reactions, including potentially fatal respiratory depression. Monitor patients for sedation and respiratory depression when co-administered.
- CYP3A4 inducers may decrease hydrocodone concentrations; discontinuation of a CYP3A4 inducer may result in increased hydrocodone concentrations and risk of toxicity.
- CYP2D6 is involved in O-demethylation of hydrocodone to hydromorphone (minor pathway relative to CYP3A4). Inhibition of CYP2D6 may alter formation of hydromorphone; clinical significance may be limited but should be considered.
- Acetaminophen is primarily metabolized by hepatic conjugation (glucuronidation, sulfation) and minor CYP-mediated oxidation (CYP2E1, CYP1A2, CYP3A4). Concomitant use of other hepatically metabolized drugs or inducers/inhibitors of these enzymes may alter acetaminophen metabolism; risk of hepatotoxicity increases with excessive total daily acetaminophen intake or combination with other acetaminophen-containing products.

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Additive CNS and respiratory depression when used with benzodiazepines, other sedative-hypnotics, anxiolytics, muscle relaxants, antipsychotics, other opioids, general anesthetics, alcohol, and illicit drugs. Concomitant use increases risk of profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death.
  - Observational data indicate increased drug-related mortality with concomitant use of opioid analgesics and benzodiazepines compared to opioids alone.
- Co-administration with MAO inhibitors or within 14 days of MAO inhibitor therapy: Not specified in available sources.

### Clinically significant interactions with management notes
- When starting or stopping CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers, monitor for altered opioid effect and respiratory depression; adjust dose or discontinue if necessary.
- Avoid or minimize concomitant use of benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants; if co‑administration is unavoidable, use lowest effective doses and monitor closely; consider prescribing naloxone for overdose rescue.
- Avoid concomitant use of multiple acetaminophen-containing products to reduce hepatotoxicity risk; counsel patients accordingly.
- Region-specific interaction management: Not specified beyond the above; REMS-related counseling and naloxone considerations are highlighted [US].

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Hydrocodone: full opioid agonist with relative selectivity for the μ-opioid receptor; analgesia mediated through central opioid receptors. No ceiling effect for analgesia; dose limited by adverse effects (respiratory/CNS depression).
- Acetaminophen: precise analgesic mechanism not fully established; thought to involve central mechanisms (analgesic and antipyretic effects).

### Pharmacodynamics
- Hydrocodone produces respiratory depression by direct action on brainstem respiratory centers, reducing responsiveness to CO2 and other stimuli.
- Opioids cause miosis, decreased gastrointestinal motility, increased smooth muscle tone in stomach/duodenum, decreased peristalsis in colon (constipation), reduced biliary and pancreatic secretions, and potential spasm of the sphincter of Oddi.
- Opioids can cause peripheral vasodilation leading to orthostatic hypotension; histamine-mediated symptoms (pruritus, flushing) may occur.
- Endocrine effects include inhibition of ACTH, cortisol, LH and stimulation of prolactin and GH; prolonged use may affect gonadal hormones.
- Opioids may modestly suppress components of the immune system.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Hydrocodone:
  - Following a 10 mg oral dose: mean peak serum concentration ≈ 23.6 ± 5.2 ng/mL.
  - Time to maximum concentration (Tmax): ~1.3 ± 0.3 hours.
  - Elimination half‑life: ~3.8 ± 0.3 hours.
  - Metabolism: complex—primarily CYP3A4-mediated N‑demethylation to norhydrocodone; CYP2D6-mediated O‑demethylation to hydromorphone (minor contributor). Other pathways include 6-keto reduction to 6‑α/β‑hydroxymetabolites.
  - Elimination: primarily renal excretion of parent and metabolites.
- Acetaminophen:
  - Rapid gastrointestinal absorption; distributed widely in body tissues.
  - Plasma protein binding: ~10–25%.
  - Plasma half‑life: ~1.25 to 3 hours (can be increased by liver damage or overdose).
  - Metabolism: mainly hepatic conjugation (glucuronide and sulfate); minor CYP‑mediated oxidation (CYP2E1 principally; CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 as additional pathways) to a reactive intermediate detoxified by glutathione.
  - Excretion: ~85% of an oral dose recovered in urine within 24 hours (mostly glucuronide conjugate).
