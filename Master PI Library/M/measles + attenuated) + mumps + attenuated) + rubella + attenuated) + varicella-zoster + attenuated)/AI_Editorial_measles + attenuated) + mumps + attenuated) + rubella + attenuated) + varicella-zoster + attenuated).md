# Editorial Product Information: measles + attenuated) + mumps + attenuated) + rubella + attenuated) + varicella-zoster + attenuated)

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **measles + attenuated) + mumps + attenuated) + rubella + attenuated) + varicella-zoster + attenuated)**
- Chemical class: Live, attenuated viral combination vaccine (measles, mumps, rubella, varicella)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Subcutaneous (SC) or intramuscular (IM)
- Note: product is a live attenuated combination vaccine produced from specific vaccine strains grown in chick embryo cells (measles and mumps) and MRC‑5 human diploid cells (rubella and varicella). Each 0.5 mL reconstituted dose contains at least: measles (Schwarz strain) ≥ 10^3.0 CCID50; mumps (RIT 4385 derived from Jeryl Lynn) ≥ 10^4.4 CCID50; rubella (Wistar RA 27/3) ≥ 10^3.0 CCID50; varicella‑zoster (OKA) ≥ 10^3.3 PFU. Residual neomycin and the excipient phenylalanine are present.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Active immunisation against measles, mumps, rubella and varicella from 9 months of age.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing
- Infants and children aged 9 months to 12 years: two doses. Preferable interval between doses: 6 weeks to 3 months. Under no circumstances should the interval be less than 4 weeks.
- Alternative regimens where applicable:
  - A single dose may be given to children who have already received a single dose of another measles–mumps–rubella (MMR) vaccine with or without a single dose of another varicella vaccine.
  - A single dose may be given and followed by a single dose of another MMR vaccine with or without a single dose of another varicella vaccine.
- Adolescents and adults: clinical safety, immunogenicity and efficacy data are not available in the provided sources.

### Administration instructions
- Administer by SC or IM injection only. **Never administer intravascularly or intradermally.**
- For children with bleeding disorders (e.g., thrombocytopenia or coagulation disorders), administer subcutaneously.
- Injection site recommendations (per national immunisation guidance): anterolateral thigh for children under 12 months; deltoid region for children 12 months and older.
- Reconstitution: vaccine is supplied as lyophilised powder plus diluent. Reconstitute by adding the full volume of diluent to the vial containing powder, mix until dissolved, withdraw the entire contents and administer. Use a new sterile needle for administration. Reconstituted vaccine should be used promptly. Product is for single‑patient use. (Further specific syringe/needle assembly instructions provided in product materials — not reproduced here.)

## 5. Contraindications
List includes all contraindications reported in the available source:
- Pregnancy. Women should avoid pregnancy for one month following vaccination. **(Contraindicated in pregnant women.)**
- Known hypersensitivity to neomycin or to any component of the vaccine (residual neomycin present). A history of contact dermatitis to neomycin is not a contraindication.
- Previous clinically significant hypersensitivity reaction to any measles, mumps, rubella and/or varicella vaccine.
- Severe humoral or cellular (primary or acquired) immunodeficiency (e.g., those receiving high-dose systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants): contraindicated.
- Current or recent immunosuppressive therapy (includes high doses of corticosteroids; does not include topical or inhaled corticosteroids). Further clinical guidance should be followed when assessing immunosuppressed patients.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- Not specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Postpone vaccination in persons with acute severe febrile illness (temperature > 38.5°C). Minor illnesses (e.g., common cold) are not a contraindication.
- Avoid intravascular or intradermal administration.
- Syncope (fainting) can occur around the time of any vaccination; appropriate measures to prevent injury and observe the vaccinee should be in place.
- Facilities and personnel for recognition and treatment of anaphylaxis (e.g., adrenaline) must be available.
- Alcohol or other skin disinfectants must be allowed to evaporate before injection as they may inactivate the live viruses.
- Limited post‑exposure protection against measles and varicella may be achieved if vaccination is given within 72 hours of exposure. The antibody response to rubella and mumps is generally too slow for effective post‑exposure prophylaxis.
- Infants in the first year of life may have inadequate response to the measles component due to maternal antibodies; additional doses should be given according to official recommendations.
- Vaccine virus excretion and transmission:
  - Pharyngeal excretion of rubella vaccine virus can occur ~7–28 days post‑vaccination (peak ~11 days); transmission of measles, mumps and rubella vaccine virus to susceptible contacts has not been documented in routine use.
  - Transmission of the varicella Oka vaccine strain to seronegative contacts has been observed at a very low rate, typically associated with vaccinee rash; transmission from vaccinees without rash cannot be ruled out. Vaccinees should, where possible, avoid contact with susceptible immunocompromised individuals for up to 6 weeks after vaccination.
- Febrile convulsions: there is an increased risk of fever and febrile convulsions 5–12 days after vaccination when this combined vaccine is used as the first measles‑containing vaccination, compared with separate administration of MMR and varicella vaccines. No increased risk was observed when used as the second measles‑containing dose. Monitor for fever during the 5–12 day risk window after vaccination; consider alternative separate vaccination regimens for children with a personal or family history of febrile convulsions.
- Thrombocytopenia: cases of new or recurrent thrombocytopenia have been reported following live measles, mumps and rubella vaccines; vaccinating persons with a history of vaccine‑associated thrombocytopenia requires careful risk–benefit assessment.
- Immunocompromised patients:
  - Live vaccination is contraindicated in severe immunodeficiency. Data are limited in other immunocompromised states; vaccination should only be considered when benefits outweigh risks.
  - Immunocompromised recipients may have reduced vaccine response and may still acquire disease despite vaccination; monitor carefully for signs of measles, mumps, rubella or varicella.
  - Few reports exist of disseminated varicella with internal organ involvement after Oka‑strain vaccination; these have occurred mainly in immunocompromised patients.
- Encephalitis and fatal outcomes have been reported post‑marketing in association with live attenuated measles, mumps, rubella and varicella vaccines including this combination; fatalities mostly occurred in immunocompromised patients. Seek urgent medical attention for symptoms suggestive of encephalitis (e.g., reduced consciousness, convulsions, ataxia, fever, severe headache).
- Egg protein: measles and mumps components are produced in chick embryo cell culture and may contain trace egg proteins. Vaccination of persons with a history of anaphylaxis to egg ingestion should only be considered after careful risk–benefit assessment and in settings where anaphylaxis can be managed. Non‑anaphylactic egg allergies are not considered a contraindication.
- Timing with immunosuppressive therapy: consider potential effects of immunosuppression on vaccine efficacy and safety; plan vaccination relative to start or cessation of immunosuppressive therapy per clinical guidance.
- Tuberculin skin testing (Mantoux): combined measles–mumps–rubella vaccines may cause temporary depression of tuberculin reactivity (anergy) for up to 6 weeks; perform tuberculin testing before or concurrently with vaccination to avoid false negatives.
- Blood products/human normal immunoglobulin: in recipients of human gammaglobulin or blood transfusion, delay vaccination for at least 3 months because passively acquired antibodies may interfere with vaccine response.
- Salicylates (e.g., aspirin) should be avoided for 6 weeks after vaccination because of the risk of Reye’s syndrome following natural varicella infection.
- Do not mix this vaccine with other vaccines in the same syringe.

### Monitoring requirements
- Observe for immediate hypersensitivity reactions after vaccination.
- Monitor for fever, rash, neurological symptoms (encephalitis), and signs of varicella or disseminated infection, especially in immunocompromised contacts or recipients.
- Monitor children with history of febrile convulsions for fever in the 5–12 day post‑vaccination window.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Consider alternative strategy (separate MMR and varicella vaccination) for the first measles‑containing dose in children with personal or family history of convulsions.
- Counsel post‑pubertal women to avoid pregnancy for 1 month after vaccination.

### Serious adverse events
- Encephalitis and fatal outcomes have been reported post‑marketing with live measles, mumps, rubella and varicella vaccines including this combination; fatalities reported primarily in immunocompromised patients.
- Very few reports of disseminated varicella with internal organ involvement following Oka‑strain vaccination, mainly in immunocompromised persons.
- Cases of worsening or recurrence of thrombocytopenia have been reported following live measles–mumps–rubella vaccination.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Concomitant administration: Clinical studies demonstrated that this combination vaccine can be administered simultaneously (same visit) with many routine infant and childhood vaccines, including: hexavalent DTPa‑HBV‑IPV/Hib combination, diphtheria–tetanus–acellular pertussis (DTPa), Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV), hepatitis B vaccine (HBV), hepatitis A vaccine (HAV), meningococcal serogroup B vaccine (MenB), meningococcal serogroup C conjugate vaccine (MenC), meningococcal ACWY conjugate vaccine (MenACWY), and 10‑valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV). When given at the same visit as another injectable vaccine, administer at different injection sites.
- Data not available for concomitant administration with meningococcal CCV or PRP‑OMP Hib vaccines used in some schedules.
- If simultaneous administration with another live attenuated vaccine is not possible, an interval of at least 1 month should be left between the two live vaccinations.
- Tuberculin (Mantoux) testing: perform before or concurrently with vaccination; avoid tuberculin testing within 6 weeks after vaccination to prevent false negatives.
- Human gammaglobulin or recent blood transfusion: delay vaccination for at least 3 months because passively acquired antibodies may interfere with vaccine efficacy.
- Salicylates: avoid for 6 weeks after vaccination due to risk of Reye’s syndrome in the context of varicella.
- Do not mix with other vaccines in the same syringe.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy.** Women should be advised to avoid pregnancy for 1 month following vaccination.
- Pregnancy category/system: [Australian] Category B2 (as specified in available sources).
- Human data: a literature review of >3,500 women inadvertently vaccinated in early pregnancy reported no cases of congenital rubella syndrome. However, post‑marketing surveillance identified at least one occurrence of congenital rubella syndrome associated with rubella vaccine strain following inadvertent vaccination in pregnancy. Fetal damage has not been documented after inadvertent administration of measles, mumps or varicella vaccines in pregnancy.
- Clinical guidance: women of childbearing potential should be tested for rubella antibodies before pregnancy; seronegative women who are not pregnant should be offered rubella vaccination. Rubella vaccine infection in utero is not universally teratogenic and inadvertent vaccination is not by itself an indication for termination; decisions should be individualized by patient and physician.

### Breastfeeding
- Limited human data available. Vaccination may be given when benefits outweigh risks.

### Fertility

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Pooled pivotal safety data derived from controlled clinical studies in healthy children (second year of life) included >6,700 administered doses and active surveillance of solicited local/systemic reactions following each dose (follow‑up periods varied: local symptoms 4 days, fever 15 days, rash up to 43 days). Specific pooled safety data (see Section 7) show higher fever rates after the first dose compared with separate administration of MMR and varicella vaccines, and an increased risk of febrile convulsions 5–12 days after the first measles‑containing vaccination versus separate MMR and varicella injections. No increased risk for febrile convulsions was observed after the second measles‑containing dose.
- Trial designs: randomized controlled studies comparing two‑dose administration of the combination vaccine versus separate administration of measles–mumps–rubella vaccine plus varicella vaccine in the second year of life; active solicited adverse event monitoring was performed. Full trial design details, efficacy endpoints and immunogenicity outcomes are not fully specified in the available sources.
