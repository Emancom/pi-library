# Editorial Product Information: Methoxyflurane

## Editorial Note
- This editorial synthesis is based on the official Product Information (PI) provided by the following jurisdiction: **Australian (TGA)**. 
- Information regarding other jurisdictions (e.g., US, European, Canadian, Japanese) was not provided in the source materials for this specific synthesis.
- The purpose of this document is to provide a harmonised editorial overview for healthcare professionals. 
- **Note:** This is an editorial synthesis and not a substitute for the official, jurisdiction-specific PI required for prescribing or dispensing.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Methoxyflurane (a fluorinated hydrocarbon volatile agent).
- **Brand Names:** **PENTHROX** [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** N01AB03.
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - Volatile liquid for inhalation containing 99.9% methoxyflurane.
    - Available in 1.5 mL and 3 mL bottles [Australian].
- **Route of Administration:** Inhalation (self-administered via a handheld inhaler).
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Medical Developments International Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Emergency Analgesia:** For the rapid relief of pain in conscious, haemodynamically stable patients who have experienced trauma and associated pain [Australian].
- **Procedural Analgesia:** For the relief of pain in monitored conscious patients undergoing surgical procedures, such as wound dressing changes [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
- **General Dosing (Adult):** A single 3 mL bottle is vaporised in the specialized inhaler. If required, a second 3 mL bottle may be administered.
- **Maximum Dosing:** 
    - **Daily:** Do not exceed 6 mL per day [Australian].
    - **Weekly:** Do not exceed 15 mL per week [Australian].
- **Administration Instructions:**
    - Must be self-administered under the supervision of trained personnel.
    - The inhaler should be prepared by pouring the liquid into the base while rotating at a 45° angle.
    - An Activated Carbon (AC) chamber should be attached to the inhaler to adsorb exhaled vapour and reduce occupational exposure.
    - Patients should be instructed to inhale gently at first, then breathe normally through the mouthpiece.
    - For stronger analgesia, the patient may cover the dilutor hole on the inhaler during inhalation.
    - Administration on consecutive days is not recommended [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
- Use as a general anaesthetic agent (due to dose-related nephrotoxicity).
- Renal impairment or renal failure, including reduced GFR or reduced renal blood flow [Australian].
- Hypersensitivity to methoxyflurane or any fluorinated anaesthetic agents.
- Cardiovascular instability.
- Respiratory depression.
- Head injury or loss of consciousness.
- Personal or family history of malignant hyperthermia.
- History of previous adverse reactions to methoxyflurane.

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Renal Toxicity:** Methoxyflurane causes dose-related, often irreversible, renal failure. This is due to the release of inorganic fluoride ions during metabolism. Risk increases with total dose and frequency of exposure.
- **Hepatic Injury:** Rare but serious reports of hepatic dysfunction, jaundice, and fatal hepatic necrosis have occurred. Use is discouraged in patients with prior liver damage following halogenated anaesthetic exposure.
- **Occupational Exposure:** Healthcare workers regularly exposed to the agent should ensure the use of AC chambers. Chronic exposure may lead to elevated liver enzymes or uric acid.
- **Respiratory Depression:** While used at analgesic doses, monitoring for hypoxia and respiratory depression is required.
- **Paediatric Neurotoxicity:** Animal studies suggest potential adverse effects on brain development with prolonged exposure to anaesthetic/analgesic agents; clinical significance in humans is undetermined.

## 6. Adverse Effects
Adverse reactions are generally not dose-dependent at analgesic levels, though systemic risks increase with cumulative exposure.

| Frequency | Adverse Reaction |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Very Common (>10%)** | Dizziness (29.5%), Headache (21.5%) |
| **Common (1–10%)** | Somnolence, Nausea, Dry mouth, Feeling drunk, Cough, Rash |
| **Uncommon (0.1–1%)** | Dysgeusia, Paraesthesia, Fatigue, Diplopia, Inappropriate affect, Oral discomfort |
| **Rare (<0.1%)** | Non-specific hepatitis, Malignant hyperthermia |

- **Serious Adverse Effects:** Irreversible renal failure, hepatic failure, cardiac arrest (rare, historically associated with anaesthetic doses), and respiratory depression [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
- **Tetracyclines:** Concurrent use with methoxyflurane is reported to cause fatal renal toxicity; this combination must be avoided.
- **Nephrotoxic Agents:** Increased risk of renal injury when used with gentamicin, kanamycin, colistin, polymyxin B, cephaloridine, and amphotericin B [Australian].
- **CNS Depressants:** Additive effects occur with opioids and other sedatives; monitor patient closely.
- **Enzyme Inducers:** Barbiturates, alcohol, and rifampicin may increase the rate of metabolism, potentially increasing fluoride-related toxicity.
- **Adrenaline/Noradrenaline:** Use with caution as the myocardium may be minimally sensitised to arrhythmias.
- **Beta-blockers:** Increased risk of hypotension [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** **Category C** [Australian]. All general anaesthetics cross the placenta and may cause CNS/respiratory depression in the neonate. Avoid in patients with toxaemia due to potential renal impairment.
- **Breastfeeding:** Exercise caution; safety data is limited when administered to nursing mothers [Australian].
- **Fertility:** Data not available in provided sources.

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Limited data available. Use the minimum effective dose to achieve analgesia [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Caution is required due to the risk of reduced blood pressure or heart rate. Peak blood fluoride levels may be reached earlier in elderly patients.
- **Renal Impairment:** Strictly contraindicated due to the risk of nephrotoxicity [Australian].
- **Obese Patients:** Higher peak blood fluoride levels are observed in obese patients compared to non-obese patients; use the lowest effective dose [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Provides rapid analgesia at low concentrations via inhalation. The exact cellular mechanism is not fully detailed, but it functions as a volatile anaesthetic agent with analgesic properties at sub-anaesthetic doses.
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Distribution:** Highly lipophilic; diffuses into fatty tissues and is released slowly over several days.
    - **Metabolism:** 50–70% of the absorbed dose is metabolised, primarily to free fluoride, oxalic acid, and dichloroacetic acid.
    - **Elimination:** Approximately 20% is exhaled; 30% of the uptake is excreted in the urine as organic and inorganic fluorine.
- **Pharmacodynamics:** At analgesic concentrations, cardiac rhythm usually remains regular, though some decrease in blood pressure and cardiac output may occur.

## 11. Clinical Trials
- **Trauma Pain Study:** A placebo-controlled study (n=149 for methoxyflurane) demonstrated efficacy in trauma patients. The most frequent treatment-emergent adverse events were dizziness (29.5%) and headache (21.5%) compared to placebo (8.1% and 16.1%, respectively) [Australian].
- **Minor Surgical Procedures:** A separate study in 49 patients undergoing minor surgery reported dizziness (8.2%) and euphoria (4.1%) as common reactions within 45 minutes of the procedure.

## 12. Storage Conditions
- **Temperature:** Store below 30°C [Australian].
- **Handling:** Methoxyflurane extracts polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plastics; avoid contact with PVC materials. It is stable and does not decompose in contact with soda lime.
- **Shelf Life:** Noted on external packaging [Australian].

## 13. Regulatory Information by Jurisdiction
- **Poison Schedule:** **Schedule 4** (Prescription Only Medicine) [Australian].
