# Editorial Product Information: monobasic potassium phosphate

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **monobasic potassium phosphate**
- Chemical class: Inorganic phosphate salt (potassium dihydrogen phosphate; ionic source of potassium and phosphate)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous (parenteral) infusion

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first approval/registration: 28 January 1998 [Australian]
- Date(s) of last revision/update of source PI: 3 November 2021 [Australian]
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- Registration dates in jurisdictions other than those listed above: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of severe hypophosphataemia (serum phosphate < 0.3 mmol/L) and other degrees of hypophosphataemia when oral therapy is not possible.
  - The underlying cause of hypophosphataemia should be identified and treated where possible.
- Conversion to oral phosphate therapy is recommended as soon as clinically feasible.

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General dosing — adults
- For severe hypophosphataemia: up to 10 mmol phosphate given by slow intravenous infusion over 12 hours.
- The dose may be repeated every 12 hours until serum phosphate exceeds 0.3 mmol/L.

### Paediatric dosing
- Suggested dose: 0.15–0.33 mmol/kg administered by intravenous infusion over 6 hours.
- The dose may be repeated at 6-hour intervals until serum phosphate exceeds 0.6 mmol/L.
- Do not exceed the maximum recommended adult dose.
- Recommended maximum infusion rate: 0.2 mmol/kg/hour.

### Administration instructions
- Must be diluted prior to administration.
- Suitable diluents (as specified): 0.9% sodium chloride or 5% glucose solution.
- Administer by slow intravenous infusion to reduce risk of phosphate- and potassium-related toxicity.
- Monitor electrolytes and renal function frequently during therapy (see Monitoring).

### Titration / Dose adjustment
- Reduce dose in renal impairment.
- Use in severe renal impairment is contraindicated (see Section 5 and Section 10).
- Specific titration schedules beyond the dosing schemes above are not specified in available sources.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Severe renal function impairment (defined in source as less than 30% of normal) because of increased risk of hyperphosphataemia.
- Hyperphosphataemia.
- Hypocalcaemia.
- Hyperkalaemia.
- Addison’s disease (due to increased risk of hyperkalaemia).
- Urolithiasis of the infected magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite) type.
- Renal impairment with oliguria and azotaemia.
- Ventricular fibrillation.
- Hyperadrenalism associated with adrenogenital syndrome.
- Extensive tissue breakdown (for example, severe burns).
- Acute dehydration.
- Heat cramps.
- Increased sensitivity to potassium administration, for example adynamia episodica hereditaria or congenital paramyotonia.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed/Highlighted warnings
- Not specified in available sources as a distinct boxed warning. The product carries important risks of **hyperkalaemia** and **hyperphosphataemia/hypocalcaemia** that require monitoring and dosing precautions.

### General warnings and precautions
- **Potassium-related risks**:
  - Use with caution in patients with conditions that impair potassium excretion (chronic renal disease, adrenal insufficiency) — careful serum potassium monitoring and dose adjustment required.
  - Use with caution in patients with conduction abnormalities (heart block) because increases in serum potassium may worsen block.
  - Avoid monobasic potassium phosphate in patients with pre-existing hyperkalaemia; consider an alternative phosphate salt without potassium (e.g., sodium phosphate) if appropriate.
  - IV potassium can produce rapid, potentially fatal hyperkalaemia if excretory mechanisms are impaired or infusion is too rapid. Monitor ECG and electrolytes in patients receiving IV potassium.

- **Phosphate-related risks**:
  - Administer phosphate cautiously where high phosphate levels may be encountered or of concern (hypoparathyroidism, chronic renal disease, acute dehydration, pancreatitis, rhabdomyolysis, severe renal insufficiency, extensive tissue damage).
  - Phosphate administration may precipitate or worsen hypocalcaemia; exercise caution when conditions with low calcium are possible (hypoparathyroidism, osteomalacia, chronic renal disease, acute pancreatitis, rickets, myotonia congenita, and certain cardiac conditions — particularly patients receiving digitalis).
  - Phosphate can bind trivalent and divalent cations; coadministration of aluminium-, calcium- or magnesium-containing salts with oral phosphate formulations impairs phosphate absorption — avoid concomitant administration where clinically relevant.

- **Other cautions**:
  - Use caution in patients receiving ACE inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, or other agents that increase potassium — these combinations increase risk of hyperkalaemia (see Section 8).
  - Monitor for signs of extraskeletal calcification, particularly in children receiving chronic phosphate supplementation (nephrocalcinosis reported in children with hypophosphataemic rickets receiving phosphate therapy).
  - Administration may affect bone scintigraphy: high phosphate may reduce skeletal uptake of technetium Tc‑99m labelled bone imaging agents.

### Monitoring requirements
- Serum concentrations of sodium, potassium, phosphate and calcium, and renal function should be monitored every 12–24 hours during therapy.
- ECG monitoring is recommended when there is a risk of hyperkalaemia or if cardiac conduction abnormalities are present or suspected.
- Monitor clinical status for signs of electrolyte disturbances (see Adverse Effects).

### Specific clinical precautions
- Convert to oral phosphate supplementation as soon as clinically appropriate.
- Reduce dose in renal impairment; contraindicated in severe renal impairment or in patients with oliguria/azotaemia.
- Use caution in elderly and paediatric patients — limited specific data available (see Section 10).

Serious adverse events and clinical presentations:
- Life‑threatening hyperkalaemia (which may be asymptomatic until severe) can progress to muscle paralysis, severe arrhythmias, and cardiac arrest. ECG changes may be the first detectable sign.
- Severe hyperphosphataemia can cause hypocalcaemia, tetany/convulsions, ectopic calcification (including vascular and soft tissue), respiratory compromise, and may be fatal in extreme cases.
- Crystal deposition in critical structures (eye, lung, heart, kidney) with severe phosphate excess has been reported; fatal alveolar diffusion block has occurred, with higher risk in alkalotic patients.

Management of adverse effects:
- Immediate cessation of phosphate administration and supportive measures.
- Correct serum electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypocalcaemia or hyperkalaemia, as clinically indicated (see Overdose).
- Continuous ECG monitoring where cardiac involvement or hyperkalaemia is suspected.

## 8. Drug Interactions

### Pharmacodynamic interactions / clinically significant combinations
- ACE inhibitors: may exacerbate hyperkalaemia when given concomitantly — monitor potassium especially in patients with renal impairment.
- Potassium‑sparing diuretics: concomitant use increases risk of hyperkalaemia, particularly in renal impairment.
- Non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory agents (NSAIDs): may increase risk of hyperkalaemia, particularly in renal impairment.
- Potassium‑containing medicines: additive risk of hyperkalaemia with concomitant potassium sources.
- Phosphate‑containing medicines: concurrent phosphate products increase risk of hyperphosphataemia, especially with impaired renal function.
- Digitalis glycosides: hyperkalaemia or rapid changes in serum potassium can precipitate digitalis toxicity; use caution in digitalised patients.
- Calcium-containing medicines: concurrent administration may increase risk of soft tissue/calcium deposition; monitor calcium and phosphate balance.
- Salicylates: administration of phosphate may acidify urine and reduce salicylate excretion, potentially increasing serum salicylate concentrations in patients stabilized on salicylates — monitor for salicylate toxicity.

### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Not specifically described in available sources beyond ionic interactions (binding/precipitation) with divalent/trivalent cations described below.

### Physical incompatibilities
- Phosphate solutions are incompatible with calcium- or magnesium-containing solutions; admixture will precipitate.
- Solutions containing other cations such as iron and aluminium may form precipitates when mixed with phosphate preparations.

### Management notes
- Avoid or closely monitor combinations that increase serum potassium or phosphate.
- Avoid mixing with calcium- or magnesium-containing intravenous solutions; if coadministration is required, use separate lines or flush adequately.
- For patients on digitalis, avoid too rapid correction of hyperkalaemia to prevent precipitating digitalis toxicity.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- Effects on fertility: Not specified in available sources.
- Pregnancy:
  - Animal reproductive studies have not been conducted with this product.
  - It is not known whether monobasic potassium phosphate adversely affects the fetus when administered to a pregnant woman.
  - The product is **not recommended for use during pregnancy** (per available source).
- Breastfeeding:
  - It is not known whether phosphates are excreted into human breast milk.
  - The product is **not recommended for use during lactation** (per available source).

## 10. Use in Special Populations

### Paediatric use
- Suggested therapeutic dosing is provided (0.15–0.33 mmol/kg over 6 hours; repeat every 6 hours until serum phosphate > 0.6 mmol/L; maximum infusion rate 0.2 mmol/kg/h).
- Other paediatric safety data: source states "No data available" in some sections; chronic phosphate supplementation in children (e.g., for hypophosphataemic rickets) has been associated with nephrocalcinosis.

### Elderly
- No specific data available; exercise clinical caution and monitor renal function and electrolytes.

### Renal impairment
- Dose should be reduced in renal impairment.
- Use in severe renal impairment (less than ~30% of normal renal function) is contraindicated due to high risk of hyperphosphataemia.
- Contraindicated in renal impairment with oliguria and azotaemia.

### Hepatic impairment
- Not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action
- Provides inorganic phosphate and potassium ions. Phosphate is a major component of bone as calcium phosphate and is essential in intracellular anion balance, energy metabolism (ATP), enzymatic processes (B‑vitamin utilization), buffering of body fluids, and renal acid excretion.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Normal plasma phosphate range stated in source: 0.8 to 1.5 mmol/L.
- Serum phosphate exists primarily as hydrogen phosphate (HPO4 2–) and dihydrogen phosphate (H2PO4 –) in body fluids; changes in phosphate affect calcium homeostasis and vitamin D renal metabolism.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Excretion: Phosphate is primarily eliminated by urinary excretion. Over 90% of plasma phosphate is filtered at the glomerulus; most is reabsorbed in the renal proximal tubule.
- Regulation: Parathyroid hormone reduces proximal tubular reabsorption of phosphate, increasing urinary excretion. Serum phosphate is inversely related to serum calcium and to renal vitamin D metabolism.
- Absorption, distribution and metabolism specifics of the IV formulation beyond the above are not specified in available sources.

### Preclinical safety data

- Carcinogenicity: Not specified in available sources.

## Overdose and Management (clinical guidance)
- Excessive phosphate administration (particularly IV) may cause hyperphosphataemia; this is most likely when renal function is impaired but can also occur with acidosis, acromegaly, haemolysis, hypoparathyroidism, extensive tissue destruction, or vitamin D toxicity.
- Clinical consequences of hyperphosphataemia: muscle weakness, paresthesias, convulsions, cardiomyopathy, respiratory failure, haematological abnormalities; hypocalcaemia and ectopic calcification may follow severe phosphate excess.
- Crystal deposition in critical organs and fatal alveolar diffusion block have been reported; risk may be greater in alkalotic patients.

Treatment principles for overdose or severe electrolyte disturbances:
- Immediately stop phosphate administration.
- Correct serum electrolyte abnormalities, particularly hypocalcaemia; provide supportive care as required.
- Management of hyperkalaemia (if present):
  - Eliminate exogenous potassium sources and discontinue potassium‑sparing agents.
  - Consider IV glucose with insulin to drive potassium intracellularly (source guidance: e.g., administer glucose infusion with insulin; specific regimens in source: 10–20 units insulin per 1000 mL of 10% glucose, with infusion rates and exact dosing tailored to clinical situation).
  - Correct acidosis if present (e.g., IV sodium bicarbonate).
  - Use exchange resins, haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis if indicated.
  - For life‑threatening cardiac arrhythmias, consider IV calcium (e.g., calcium gluconate 10% in doses described in source: 10–50 mL IV over 5 minutes), with continuous ECG monitoring.
- For severe phosphate excess with ectopic calcification or renal failure, dialysis may be required.
- Contact local poison information centre or toxicology service for additional guidance (source lists an Australian poison centre number; clinicians should use their appropriate local emergency/toxicology resources).
