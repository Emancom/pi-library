# Editorial Product Information: Suxamethonium

## Editorial Note
- **Contributing Jurisdictions:** Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview of suxamethonium (succinylcholine) for healthcare professionals, synthesising clinical data from the provided regulatory source.
- **Disclaimer:** This is an editorial synthesis and should not be used as a substitute for the official Product Information (PI) when prescribing within a specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should refer to the local approved labeling for definitive guidance.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Suxamethonium chloride dihydrate (a depolarising neuromuscular blocking agent).
- **Brand Names:** Suxamethonium Accord [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** M03AB01 (not explicitly stated in source, but standard for suxamethonium).
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 100 mg/2 mL solution for injection in a pre-filled glass syringe [Australian].
- **Routes of Administration:** Intravenous (IV) injection or infusion; Intramuscular (IM) injection (when IV access is unavailable).
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Accord Healthcare Pty Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
Suxamethonium is indicated for producing brief skeletal muscle relaxation during anaesthesia. Specific procedures include:
- Endotracheal intubation.
- Endoscopic examinations.
- Orthopaedic manipulations.
- Short surgical procedures.
- Electro-convulsive therapy (ECT).

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Principles
Dosage must be individualised based on body weight, required degree of relaxation, and patient response. Respiration must be controlled; the drug must never be administered to a conscious patient.

### Adult Dosing
- **Short Procedures (e.g., Intubation):** 0.6 mg/kg IV (range 0.3–1.1 mg/kg) administered over 10–30 seconds. Muscle relaxation typically occurs within 60 seconds and lasts 4–6 minutes.
- **Prolonged Procedures:** IV infusion at 2.5–4.3 mg/minute. Solutions should be diluted to 0.1% to 0.2% (1–2 mg/mL) in 5% glucose or sterile isotonic saline.
- **Test Dose:** An initial dose of 0.1 mg/kg may be used to assess patient sensitivity.

### Paediatric Dosing
- **IV Dose:** 1 to 2 mg/kg.
- **IM Dose:** Up to 2.5 mg/kg if IV access is unavailable; maximum total dose of 150 mg.
- **Note:** Neonates and premature infants may exhibit relative resistance to the drug. Continuous IV infusions are considered unsafe in children due to the risk of malignant hyperthermia [Australian].

### Administration Instructions
- Do not mix with other neuromuscular blockers, general anaesthetics (e.g., barbiturates), or other agents in the same syringe.
- Use as soon as practicable after dilution; if stored, hold at 2°C–8°C for no more than 24 hours.

## 4. Contraindications
- Personal or familial history of **malignant hyperthermia**.
- Genetically determined disorders of plasma pseudocholinesterase.
- Myopathies associated with elevated creatinine phosphokinase (CPK).
- Duchenne’s muscular dystrophy.
- Known hypersensitivity to suxamethonium.
- **Severe hyperkalaemia** or renal impairment with elevated potassium levels.
- Acute narrow-angle glaucoma and penetrating eye injuries (due to potential increase in intraocular pressure).
- Post-acute phase of major burns or multiple trauma (risk of hyperkalaemic arrest).
- Extensive muscle degeneration (e.g., recent paraplegia, severe long-lasting sepsis).

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Expert Supervision:** Use only under the strict supervision of an anaesthetist with immediate access to intubation equipment and positive pressure oxygen ventilation.
- **Malignant Hyperthermia:** A rare, potentially fatal hypermetabolic state. Early signs include muscle rigidity, tachycardia, and rising temperature. Treatment requires immediate discontinuation, oxygen, and IV dantrolene.
- **Hyperkalaemia and Cardiac Arrest:** Administration causes an immediate rise in serum potassium. This can be fatal in patients with denervation, burns, or trauma (greatest risk 10–90 days post-injury) [Australian].
- **Low Pseudocholinesterase Levels:** Recovery may be significantly delayed in patients with low enzyme levels (due to liver disease, pregnancy, malnutrition, or genetic factors). 
- **Bradycardia:** Profound bradycardia or asystole may occur, especially in children or after a second dose. Pre-treatment with atropine can mitigate this risk.
- **Bone Fractures:** Use with caution as initial fasciculations (muscle contractions) may exacerbate injuries.
- **Delayed Recovery:** If apnoea is prolonged, neostigmine should NOT be used to reverse a single dose, as it may intensify the depolarisation block.

## 6. Adverse Effects
Frequencies are not specifically categorised by the source document, but the following reactions are reported:
- **Neuromuscular:** Muscle pain (post-operative), fasciculation, rhabdomyolysis (especially in males with muscular dystrophy), myoglobinuria, and trismus.
- **Cardiovascular:** Bradycardia, tachycardia, arrhythmias, hypertension, hypotension, and cardiac arrest secondary to hyperkalaemia.
- **Respiratory:** Apnoea, prolonged respiratory failure, bronchospasm, and pulmonary oedema (in infants).
- **Metabolic:** Malignant hyperthermia, hyperkalaemia, porphyria, and excessive salivation.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Increased intragastric pressure (potential for aspiration).
- **Special Senses:** Increased intraocular pressure.
- **Other:** Increased intracranial pressure, renal failure, and severe hypersensitivity reactions (including anaphylactic shock).

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- **Inhaled Anaesthetics:** Halothane, cyclopropane, and nitrous oxide may increase the risk of dysrhythmias and malignant hyperthermia.
- **Propofol and Fentanyl:** Increased risk of severe bradycardia and asystole.
- **Non-depolarising Relaxants:** Administration of agents like pancuronium or mivacurium prior to suxamethonium can alter the intensity and duration of the block.
- **Digoxin/Verapamil:** Concomitant use may result in cardiac arrhythmias.

### Prolonged Blockage (Potentiation)
- **Plasma Cholinesterase Inhibitors:** Neostigmine, pyridostigmine, metoclopramide, and ecothiopate eye drops significantly prolong the action of suxamethonium.
- **Antibiotics:** Non-penicillin antibiotics (aminoglycosides, polymyxins, etc.) may enhance the block.
- **Other:** Lignocaine, procaine, lithium, phenytoin, carbamazepine, magnesium salts, and oral contraceptives.

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy Category:** Category A [Australian].
- **Clinical Considerations:** Plasma pseudocholinesterase levels decrease by approximately 25% during pregnancy and postpartum, potentially leading to prolonged apnoea. Suxamethonium crosses the placenta in small amounts; high doses to the mother may cause residual blockade in the neonate during caesarean section.
- **Breastfeeding:** Safety data not specified in available sources.
- **Fertility:** No data available.

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Neonates/infants may be relatively resistant. Bolus doses in children carry a higher risk of profound bradycardia. Continuous infusions are considered unsafe in this group [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** No specific data available in the source.
- **Renal Impairment:** Use with extreme caution. Avoid in patients with high serum potassium. Potential for prolonged apnoea due to accumulation of the metabolite succinylmonocholine.
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Caution required; severe liver disease may lower pseudocholinesterase levels, prolonging the drug’s effect.

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Suxamethonium mimics acetylcholine at the motor end plate, causing depolarisation. Because it is not immediately degraded by acetylcholinesterase, the end plate remains depolarised, causing transient fasciculation followed by skeletal muscle paralysis.
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Onset:** IV (30–60 seconds); IM (2–3 minutes).
    - **Duration:** IV (approx. 2–10 minutes); IM (10–30 minutes).
    - **Metabolism:** Rapidly hydrolysed by plasma pseudocholinesterase to succinylmonocholine (minimally active) and then to succinic acid and choline.
    - **Excretion:** Approximately 10% is excreted unchanged in the urine.
