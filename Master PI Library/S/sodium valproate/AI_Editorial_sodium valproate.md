## 3. Approved Indications
- Epilepsy:
  - Primary generalised epilepsy (including absence [petit mal] seizures, myoclonic epilepsy, tonic‑clonic [grand mal] seizures).
  - Partial (focal) epilepsy, as monotherapy or as adjunctive therapy.
- Mania:
  - Treatment of mania where other therapy has proved inadequate or is inappropriate.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adults)
- Usual initial adult dose: start 600 mg daily, increase by 200 mg/day every three days until control is achieved.
- Typical maintenance range: 1,000–2,000 mg/day (approx. 20–30 mg/kg/day).
- If required and not controlled in this range, dose may be increased up to 2,500 mg/day in some cases.
- Plasma levels are not routinely required for dose optimisation but may be useful when control is inadequate or adverse effects suspected.

### Dosing by indication
- Epilepsy — Monotherapy (adults): as above (start 600 mg/day, titrate by 200 mg every 3 days; usual 1,000–2,000 mg/day).
- Epilepsy — Children:
  - Children >20 kg: initial 400 mg/day (irrespective of weight) with spaced increases until control; usually 20–30 mg/kg/day.
  - Children <20 kg: initial 20 mg/kg/day; in severe cases may be increased only where plasma valproic acid levels can be monitored.
  - Doses above 40 mg/kg/day require clinical chemistry and haematological monitoring.
  - Tablets should only be used in children once control is achieved and the correct dosage can be given using available tablet strengths; dosing titration may require liquid preparations.
- Mania (adults): same initiation and usual dose range as for epilepsy (start 600 mg/day, increase by 200 mg every 3 days; usual 1,000–2,000 mg/day; may increase to 2,500 mg/day if needed).
  - Clinical data support greater efficacy with serum levels >45 µg/mL for mania; increased adverse events noted at levels >125 µg/mL.

### Administration instructions
- Preferably give with or after food.
- Oral doses should be swallowed whole with some water.
- For non-prolonged release formulations during pregnancy, divide the daily dose into at least two single doses (when valproate is used in pregnancy under exceptional circumstances).

### Titration schedules
- Adult titration example: start 600 mg/day → increase by 200 mg/day every 3 days until control is achieved.
- Pediatric titration: see dosing by weight above; use liquid formulations for accurate titration where required.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Pregnancy for the treatment of epilepsy, unless there is no suitable alternative treatment.
- Pregnancy for the treatment of mania.
- Women of childbearing potential: contraindicated unless the prescriber has provided information regarding the risks during pregnancy and recommendations on use.
- Pre‑existing acute or chronic hepatic dysfunction or family history of severe hepatitis (particularly drug‑related).
- Known hypersensitivity to sodium valproate.
- Known urea cycle disorders.
- Known systemic primary carnitine deficiency with uncorrected hypocarnitinaemia.
- Known hepatic porphyria.
- Patients known to have mitochondrial disorders caused by mutations in the nuclear gene encoding mitochondrial DNA polymerase gamma (POLG) (e.g., Alpers–Huttenlocher syndrome) and in children under two years of age who are suspected of having a POLG‑related disorder.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed/critical warnings (emphasised)
- **Teratogenicity and neurodevelopmental risk**: **sodium valproate** has a high teratogenic potential and in utero exposure is associated with increased risk of major congenital malformations and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in the child. Do not use in female children or women of childbearing potential unless other treatments are ineffective or not tolerated. If there is no suitable alternative during pregnancy, use only after specialist review, as monotherapy at the lowest effective dose, with informed counselling, and specialist prenatal referral.
- **Hepatic failure**: liver dysfunction, including hepatic failure and fatalities, has occurred with valproate. Avoid in patients with pre‑existing hepatic disease or family history of severe hepatitis.
- **Urea cycle disorders/hyperammonaemia**: valproate can precipitate hyperammonaemia and hyperammonaemic encephalopathy, including in patients without abnormal liver tests; evaluate for UCD where indicated and discontinue valproate if symptomatic hyperammonaemia develops.

### General warnings and precautions
- Initiation and ongoing treatment should be supervised by a specialist experienced in management of epilepsy or bipolar disorder.
- Women of childbearing potential must receive comprehensive counselling on risks, pregnancy prevention, and must not be started on valproate unless pregnancy is excluded by a confirmed negative pregnancy test; effective contraception must be used without interruption during treatment.
- Female children: prescribers must ensure parents/caregivers are informed and to contact specialist at menarche; reassess need for therapy annually and attempt to switch to alternatives prior to adulthood where possible.
- Male reproductive potential: a retrospective observational study suggests increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in children of men treated with valproate in the 3 months prior to conception; inform male patients of potential risk, advise against sperm donation during treatment and for 3 months after stopping, and discuss contraception for partner and family planning.
- Pancreatitis: life‑threatening and haemorrhagic pancreatitis has been reported; can occur shortly after initiation or after years of use; young children at particular risk. Discontinue valproate if pancreatitis is diagnosed.
- Hypocarnitinaemia: valproate may decrease carnitine levels and precipitate or worsen hypocarnitinaemia, which can cause hyperammonaemia, hypoketotic hypoglycaemia, myopathy including cardiomyopathy, rhabdomyolysis, Fanconi syndrome. Consider carnitine supplementation when symptomatic hypocarnitinaemia is suspected or detected.
- Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency: female heterozygotes may present variably; valproate may precipitate hyperammonaemia in OTC deficiency—evaluate appropriately and discontinue valproate if symptomatic.
- Haematological and bleeding risk: valproate can prolong bleeding time and cause thrombocytopenia; monitor platelet count and coagulation parameters before surgery and with unexplained bruising/bleeding.
- Suicidal ideation and behaviour: antiepileptic drugs, including valproate, are associated with an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviours; monitor for emergence or worsening of depression, suicidal ideation/behaviour, or unusual mood/behaviour changes.
- Other immune disorders (e.g., lupus erythematosus) have been described rarely; weigh risks vs benefits in such patients.
- Use caution in renal impairment; lower doses may be required because free drug levels may be elevated due to reduced albumin and altered excretion of metabolites.
- Use in the elderly: altered pharmacokinetics (increased volume of distribution and increased free fraction due to decreased serum albumin) may affect plasma level interpretation; dose by clinical response.
- Concomitant enzyme inducers (e.g., phenytoin, phenobarbitone, carbamazepine) may necessitate higher valproate doses; conversely, withdrawal of enzyme inducers may permit dose reduction.
- Estrogen‑containing products (including contraceptives): may increase clearance of valproate and reduce serum concentrations; monitor clinical response and consider serum level monitoring when starting or stopping estrogen therapy.

### Monitoring requirements
- Baseline and periodic liver function tests: monitor for signs of hepatic dysfunction; discontinue if clinically significant deterioration occurs.
- Baseline and periodic blood counts including platelet count; bleeding time and coagulation tests where clinically indicated (prior to surgery or if spontaneous bruising/bleeding).
- Consider ammonia measurement and metabolic evaluation if unexplained lethargy, vomiting, ataxia, or changes in consciousness occur.
- Consider carnitine level monitoring in patients at risk of hypocarnitinaemia; consider supplementation if symptomatic.
- Specialist review at least annually for women and men of reproductive potential to reassess ongoing suitability of therapy.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Enzyme inducers (e.g., phenytoin, phenobarbitone, carbamazepine): these may increase clearance of valproate or alter requirements; may require dose increases of valproate by approximately 5–10 mg/kg/day when given concomitantly. When enzyme inducers are withdrawn, valproate dose may be reducible to maintain control.
- Estrogen‑containing products (including hormonal contraceptives): may increase clearance of valproate and reduce serum concentrations; monitor clinical response (seizure or mood control) when initiating or discontinuing estrogen therapy and consider serum valproate monitoring.
- Pivalate‑conjugated medicines and some other antiepileptics: concomitant use may increase risk of hypocarnitinaemia by decreasing carnitine stores.
- Note: sodium valproate itself has a substantially lower enzyme‑inducing effect than certain other anticonvulsants.

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Concomitant barbiturate therapy: when used with barbiturates, observe for increased sedation and consider reducing the barbiturate dose if sedation occurs.
- Bleeding risk: concomitant use of other agents affecting haemostasis may increase bleeding risk because valproate can prolong bleeding time and cause thrombocytopenia; monitor accordingly.

### Clinically significant interactions — management notes
- Monitor clinical response and serum levels when adding or stopping estrogen‑containing products or enzyme inducers.
- Monitor platelets and coagulation when combining with anticoagulants or other drugs that impair haemostasis.
- Consider carnitine status and supplement where appropriate if combined with medications that deplete carnitine or in at‑risk patients.

(Jurisdiction‑specific interaction differences were not specified in the available sources.)

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Teratogenicity and neurodevelopmental risk: **sodium valproate** is associated with a high risk of major congenital malformations and neurodevelopmental disorders in children exposed in utero. Female children and women of childbearing potential should not be prescribed valproate unless other treatments are ineffective or not tolerated.
- If valproate is the only treatment option during pregnancy:
  - Prefer monotherapy at the lowest effective dose.
  - For non‑prolonged release formulations, divide the daily dose into at least two doses during pregnancy.
  - Patients should be referred to a prenatal specialist experienced in pre‑natal medicine for evaluation and counselling regarding pregnancy exposed to valproate.
- Mandatory counselling requirements (as stated in the source):
  - Exclude pregnancy before initiating treatment (negative pregnancy test required, confirmed by a healthcare provider).
  - Provide comprehensive information on risks to unborn child and on contraception and pregnancy planning.
  - Regular review (≥ annual) by a specialist; immediate specialist consultation if pregnancy is planned or suspected.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Children >20 kg: start 400 mg/day and titrate; usual target 20–30 mg/kg/day.
- Children <20 kg: 20 mg/kg/day initial dose; increases only with plasma level monitoring in severe cases.
- Doses >40 mg/kg/day require clinical chemistry and haematological monitoring.
- Liquids may be required for accurate titration in children; use tablets only when the correct dosage can be given with available tablet strengths.
- Contraindicated in children under two years suspected of POLG‑related disorder; valproate is contraindicated in patients with known POLG mutations.

### Elderly
- Pharmacokinetics are altered (increased volume of distribution; decreased albumin binding increases free drug fraction); adjust interpretation of plasma levels accordingly. Dose selection should be based on clinical response.

### Renal impairment
- Lower doses may be required because free drug levels may be increased secondary to lowered serum albumin and reduced urinary excretion of metabolites. Specific eGFR‑based adjustments: Not specified in available sources.

### Hepatic impairment
- Pre‑existing hepatic dysfunction is a contraindication. Use is not recommended in patients with significant hepatic disease. Monitor LFTs and discontinue for clinically significant hepatic deterioration.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Mania: The Bowden et al. study (cited in the source PI) supported greater efficacy at serum levels above 45 µg/mL (these levels achieved ≥20% improvement on both subscales of the Mania Rating Scale). Levels >125 µg/mL were associated with increased drug‑related adverse events. Exact trial design details, patient numbers, and full outcomes beyond these points: Not specified in available sources.
- Pancreatitis incidence in clinical trials: two cases of pancreatitis without alternative aetiology were reported among 2,416 patients (representing 1,044 patient‑years) in the trials cited.
- Suicidality: Pooled analyses of 199 placebo‑controlled trials of 11 different antiepileptic drugs (including valproate) demonstrated an increased risk of suicidal thinking or behaviour for AEDs overall (adjusted RR 1.8, 95% CI 1.2–2.7). In those pooled trials (median duration 12 weeks), estimated incidence was 0.43% for AED‑treated vs 0.24% for placebo; applicability specifically to valproate: Not specified in available sources.
- Other pivotal trial details and comparative efficacy/safety data: Not specified in available sources.
