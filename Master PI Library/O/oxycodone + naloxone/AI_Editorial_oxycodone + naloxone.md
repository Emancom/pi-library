# Editorial Product Information: oxycodone + naloxone

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **oxycodone + naloxone**
- Chemical class:
  - Oxycodone: opioid analgesic (opioid)
  - Naloxone: opioid receptor antagonist
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral (modified‑release oral product described in source documents)
- DO NOT include brand or trade names, dosage forms, tablet physical descriptions, or PBS/schedule listings

- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Any withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Management of severe pain that:
  - is opioid‑responsive, and
  - requires daily, continuous, long‑term treatment, and
  - other treatment options (including non‑opioid analgesics) are ineffective, not tolerated or otherwise inadequate.
- Therapy and/or prophylaxis of opioid‑induced constipation (effect attributable to the naloxone component in fixed combination).
- Second‑line symptomatic treatment of severe to very severe idiopathic restless legs syndrome (RLS) after failure of dopaminergic therapy; use only when prior dopaminergic treatment has failed (see Section 4).  
  - Notes for RLS:
    - RLS indication applies to patients with symptoms of at least 6 months, severe and present daily (≥ 4 days/week).
    - Treat under specialist supervision and reassess frequently (see Section 4).

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Usual starting dose for opioid‑naïve patients: one 10/5 mg (oxycodone/naloxone) modified‑release tablet every 12 hours.
- Lower starting strengths available to facilitate titration: 2.5/1.25 mg and 5/2.5 mg (used for initiation, mild hepatic impairment or renal impairment).
- Patients already on opioids may be started at higher doses according to prior opioid exposure (see conversion guidance below).
- Maximum recommended daily dose (from available sources): 160/80 mg (i.e., 80/40 mg every 12 hours). Patients needing higher total oxycodone doses should be switched to supplemental single‑entity controlled‑release oxycodone; note that using supplemental oxycodone may impair the bowel‑function benefit of the naloxone component.

### Dosing by indication
- Analgesia (adults and elderly):
  - Start at 10/5 mg every 12 hours for opioid‑naïve patients; titrate to clinical effect.
  - For non‑cancer pain, daily doses up to 40/20 mg are usually sufficient, though higher doses may be required in some patients.
- Restless legs syndrome (adults and elderly):
  - Usual starting dose for opioid‑naïve patients: 5/2.5 mg every 12 hours.
  - Titrate weekly if needed.
  - Reassess at least every 3 months; consider a brief taper and trial off before continuing beyond 1 year.
- Paediatric use: Not recommended for use in children (no paediatric dosing specified).
- Elderly: Use the same starting doses described for adults but exercise caution; monitor closely (see Section 10).

### Conversion from other opioids
- Determine total daily dose of prior opioid and calculate approximate oral oxycodone equivalent using the following multiplication factors (prior opioid mg/day × factor = mg/day oral oxycodone):
  - Oxycodone: 1
  - Codeine: 0.15
  - Hydromorphone (oral): 4
  - Pethidine (oral): 0.1
  - Methadone (oral): 1.5
  - Morphine (oral): 0.5
  - Parenteral conversion factors for selected agents (where provided):
    - Hydromorphone (parenteral): factor 20
    - Morphine (parenteral): factor 3 (use 1.5 for high‑dose parenteral morphine for conservative conversion)
    - Pethidine (parenteral): 0.4
    - Methadone (parenteral): 3
  - Transdermal fentanyl: conservative guidance—initiate oxycodone ~10 mg every 12 hours (i.e., 20 mg/day) for each 25 µg/hr fentanyl patch after removing patch and waiting ~18 hours; follow patient closely.

- After calculating total daily oral oxycodone requirement, divide equally into two 12‑hourly doses of the oxycodone/naloxone product.

### Titration schedules
- Titrate cautiously to the minimum effective dose that controls pain and provides functional improvement.
- Dose adjustment may be made as frequently as every 1–2 days if clinically required.
- For breakthrough pain, consider rescue medication equivalent to approximately 1/6 to 1/12 of the daily oxycodone dose; needing more than two rescue doses/day usually indicates need for reassessment and possible dose adjustment.

### Administration instructions
- Swallow tablets whole; do not break, chew, crush or dissolve. Breaking/chewing/crushing may lead to rapid release and absorption of a potentially fatal oxycodone dose.
- The 60/30 mg and 80/40 mg strengths should be used only in opioid‑tolerant patients; these strengths may cause fatal respiratory depression in opioid‑naïve patients.
- Before starting therapy, establish treatment strategy including duration, goals and plan for discontinuation; frequent patient contact is recommended during therapy.
- When discontinuing long‑term therapy, consider gradual tapering to reduce withdrawal risk.

### Missed dose
- If a scheduled dose is missed, do not take the missed dose unless it is more than 8 hours before the next scheduled dose. Do not take extra tablets or double doses to make up for a missed dose.

## 5. Contraindications
(Complete list as stated in available sources)
- Hypersensitivity to oxycodone, naloxone or any excipient.
- Any condition in which opioids are contraindicated.
- Moderate to severe hepatic impairment (contraindicated).
- Elevated carbon dioxide levels in blood (hypercapnia).
- Severe respiratory disease, acute respiratory disease and respiratory depression.
- Cor pulmonale.
- Cardiac arrhythmias.
- Uncontrolled bronchial asthma.
- Severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
- Non‑opioid induced paralytic ileus.
- Pregnancy.
- Lactation (breastfeeding).
- Severe central nervous system depression.
- Increased cerebrospinal or intracranial pressure.
- Brain tumour or head injury (due to risk of increased intracranial pressure).
- Uncontrolled convulsive disorders.
- Suspected surgical abdomen.
- Delayed gastric emptying.
- Alcoholism.
- Delirium tremens.
- Concurrent administration of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), and for 2 weeks after their cessation.
- History of opioid abuse for restless legs syndrome (RLS).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed/Prominent warnings
- **Risk of life‑threatening or fatal respiratory depression** — greatest on initiation or following dose increases. Monitor closely.
- **Abuse, misuse, addiction and diversion risk** — opioid component is a potential drug of abuse; assess patient risk prior to prescribing and monitor throughout therapy.
- **Concomitant use with benzodiazepines and other CNS depressants (including alcohol) may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death.**

### General warnings and precautions
- Assess risk of hazardous and harmful use before prescribing and monitor regularly for signs of misuse, abuse and addiction.
- Tolerance and physical and/or psychological dependence may develop. Prolonged use increases risk of Opioid Use Disorder (OUD).
- In patients with signs or symptoms of OUD, consider consultation with an addiction specialist.
- If tablets are abused parenterally or intranasally by opioid‑dependent individuals, naloxone may precipitate marked withdrawal symptoms or worsen existing withdrawal.
- Parenteral injection of tablet constituents (e.g., talc) can cause local tissue necrosis, pulmonary granulomas and other serious, potentially fatal complications.
- Respiratory depression risk is increased in elderly, frail, debilitated patients and in patients with impaired respiratory function, renal or hepatic impairment.
- Sleep‑related breathing disorders: opioids can cause central sleep apnoea (dose‑dependent) and sleep‑related hypoxaemia; may worsen pre‑existing sleep apnoea.
- Severe pain antagonises respiratory depression from opioids — if pain suddenly subsides, opioid respiratory depressant effects can rapidly emerge.
- Use with caution in patients with reduced respiratory reserve.
- Use with caution in mild hepatic impairment and renal impairment; contraindicated in moderate and severe hepatic impairment.
- Concurrent use with supplemental single‑entity oxycodone may reduce the beneficial effect of naloxone on bowel function.
- Not indicated for use as-needed (PRN) analgesia; designed for fixed 12‑hourly dosing.
- Use in chronic non‑cancer pain: product is not indicated except in exceptional circumstances (per available sources).
- Restless legs syndrome: use only after dopaminergic therapy failure; prior dopaminergic therapy should generally have lasted ~4 weeks (shorter if intolerance); combination with dopaminergic medications was not assessed in a named trial (see Section 12).

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor for respiratory depression, sedation, signs of OUD, misuse, abuse and diversion.
- Monitor bowel function; anticipate improvement vs. single‑entity oxycodone but watch for changes if product discontinued or if supplemental oxycodone is given.
- Monitor hepatic and renal function as clinically indicated; avoid use in moderate/severe hepatic impairment.
- Monitor for signs of serotoninergic effects only if combined with other serotonergic drugs — (Not specified in available sources whether serotonergic interactions are clinically documented for this product).
- Reassess pain control, functional improvement and adverse effects frequently; for RLS reassess at least every 3 months.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Do not crush, break or chew tablets — risk of fatal oxycodone overdose from rapid release.
- Avoid concomitant use with alcohol.
- Use extreme caution when co‑prescribing other CNS depressants (benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sedative antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, cannabis, and others); limit doses and durations and monitor closely.
- Avoid concurrent MAOI therapy and for 2 weeks after stopping MAOIs.
- In opioid‑dependent individuals, naloxone may precipitate withdrawal if administered parenterally.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Concomitant use with benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sedative antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, cannabis or other CNS depressants (including alcohol) may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death.
  - Management: avoid combined use where possible; if necessary, limit doses and durations, and monitor closely for respiratory depression and sedation.
- Concomitant use with other opioids increases the risk of respiratory depression and additive opioid effects; careful conversion and monitoring required.
- Parenteral or intranasal abuse in opioid‑dependent individuals: naloxone component may precipitate or intensify acute withdrawal due to opioid receptor antagonism.
- Concomitant administration of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) is contraindicated; avoid for 2 weeks after MAOI cessation.

### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Specific pharmacokinetic interaction data (e.g., CYP interactions, effects on metabolism) are not specified in the available sources.

### Clinically significant interaction notes and management
- When switching from transdermal fentanyl, begin oxycodone/naloxone approximately 18 hours after removal of the patch with a conservative initial oxycodone dose (~10 mg every 12 hours per 25 µg/hr fentanyl patch) and monitor closely.
- Use of supplemental single‑entity oxycodone with oxycodone/naloxone can reduce the bowel‑function benefit attributable to naloxone.
- For patients receiving high‑dose parenteral opioids, conservative opioid dose conversion is advised (e.g., use reduced conversion factors).
- Where concomitant use of CNS depressants is unavoidable, employ the lowest effective doses and observe patients closely for respiratory depression and sedation.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

  - Pregnancy category systems (e.g., US lettered categories or Australian categories) are not specified in the available sources.
- Breastfeeding (lactation): Listed as a contraindication in the available sources — breastfeeding is not recommended during treatment with **oxycodone + naloxone**.
- Fertility: Not specified in available sources

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Not recommended for use in children. No paediatric dosing or safety data specified in available sources.

### Elderly use
- Use caution in elderly patients; monitor closely for respiratory depression and other opioid‑related adverse effects. Initial dosing guidance for adults applies to elderly patients but the dose should be titrated with caution.

### Renal impairment
- Caution advised in patients with renal impairment.
- Lower strengths (2.5/1.25 mg or 5/2.5 mg every 12 hours) may be suitable for patients with renal impairment for initiation; dose should be titrated cautiously.
- Specific eGFR‑based dosing adjustments are not provided in available sources.

### Hepatic impairment
- Mild hepatic impairment: exercise caution; consider lower starting doses (2.5/1.25 mg or 5/2.5 mg every 12 hours) and careful titration.
- Moderate to severe hepatic impairment: contraindicated.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Oxycodone: opioid analgesic (opioid agonist) used for relief of moderate to severe pain.
- Naloxone: opioid receptor antagonist; in this fixed oral combination the naloxone component is indicated to treat and/or prevent opioid‑induced constipation. Naloxone’s opioid receptor antagonist properties are also associated with potential to precipitate withdrawal if systemically available in opioid‑dependent patients.
