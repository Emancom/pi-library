# Editorial Product Information: retinol + colecalciferol + dl-alpha-tocopherol + ascorbic + cocarboxylase + riboflavin + pyridoxine + cyanocobalamin + folic + dexpanthenol + biotin + nicotinamide

- Chemical class: Combination of fat‑soluble vitamins (retinol/palmitate [vitamin A], colecalciferol [vitamin D3], dl‑alpha‑tocopherol [vitamin E]) and water‑soluble vitamins (ascorbic acid [vitamin C], cocarboxylase [thiamine/Vit B1 derivative], riboflavin [Vit B2], pyridoxine [Vit B6], cyanocobalamin [Vit B12], folic acid [Vit B9], dexpanthenol [Vit B5 derivative], biotin [Vit B7], nicotinamide [niacin/Vit B3]).

## 3. Approved Indications
- Indicated for parenteral multi‑vitamin supplementation to correct or prevent vitamin deficiencies when oral administration is contraindicated, impossible, or insufficient:
  - Adults and children over 11 years of age (indication stated for this age group in available sources).
- Note: Product does not contain vitamin K; vitamin K should be given separately if clinically required.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult and paediatric >11 years)
- Typical recommended dose: one single dose vial per day (reconstituted prior to administration). Specific mg/IU content per vial not repeated here; use product label for exact strengths.

### Dosing by indication
- No separate dosing regimens by specific indications provided in available sources beyond the daily single‑vial recommendation for adults and children >11 years.

### Administration instructions
- Reconstitution:
  - Reconstitute the lyophilised vial with 5 mL sterile Water for Injections or other compatible parenteral fluids (e.g., 0.9% sodium chloride or 5% glucose) using aseptic technique.
  - Add 5 mL of diluent with a sterile syringe and gently mix until fully dissolved. Do not use unless the reconstituted solution is clear.
  - Discard any unused portion; do not store reconstituted product for later use. (Storage temperature/time details are not included here.)
- Intravenous injection:
  - Administer slowly. If injected intravenously as a bolus, infusion should be slow — injection should be given over at least 10 minutes.
- Intravenous infusion:
  - May be further diluted for intravenous infusion and should be administered slowly.
- Parenteral nutrition admixture:
  - When mixed into parenteral nutrition admixtures, mix thoroughly and consider compatibility/stability issues (see Incompatibilities and Interactions).
  - Use of a final filter is recommended during administration of all parenteral nutrition solutions.
- Immediately inspect reconstituted solution visually for particulate matter and abnormal discoloration prior to administration; do not use if abnormal.
- Missed dose instructions: Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
List of all contraindications stated in available sources:
- Known hypersensitivity to any of the active ingredients of the product.
- Known hypersensitivity to soy or peanut protein products (product contains soy‑derived lecithin).
- Known hypersensitivity or pre‑existing intolerance to thiamine (Vitamin B1) — do not administer to patients with thiamine intolerance.
- Pre‑existing hypervitaminosis (e.g., hypervitaminosis A/D/E) or known vitamin toxicity.
- Impaired hepatic function — contraindicated in patients with impaired hepatic function.
- Hyperparathyroidism with hypercalcaemic complications — contraindicated in such patients.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- None specified in available sources. (If regionally applicable boxed warnings exist in other jurisdictions, they are not specified in available sources.)

### General warnings and precautions
- Hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis:
  - Anaphylactic and severe systemic hypersensitivity reactions have been reported; thiamine and nicotinamide components can provoke anaphylaxis in susceptible individuals.
  - Mild allergic signs such as sneezing or asthma may precede severe reactions. Infusion or injection may be rate‑related; stop administration immediately if signs of hypersensitivity occur.
  - Cardiac arrest and fatal outcomes have been reported with parenteral multivitamin administration in post‑marketing experience.
- Vitamin toxicity:
  - Monitor for hypervitaminosis, particularly vitamins A, D and E. Fat‑soluble vitamins accumulate and pose a risk with additional supplementation from other sources or long‑term use.
  - Exercise caution in patients at increased risk for vitamin A toxicity (e.g., protein malnutrition, renal impairment, small body size, chronic therapy).
  - Pregnancy: Vitamin A content in this product exceeds some recommended intakes for pregnant/lactating women; use in pregnancy and lactation is not recommended. See Section 9.
- Refeeding syndrome:
  - Refeeding severely undernourished patients may precipitate refeeding syndrome (electrolyte shifts, thiamine deficiency, fluid retention). Monitor electrolytes and clinical status and advance feeding slowly to avoid overfeeding.
- Precipitates and catheter/infusion risks:
  - Precipitation (including calcium phosphate precipitates) has been reported in parenteral nutrition admixtures; precipitates may cause pulmonary vascular events including fatal outcomes. Inspect infusion set and catheter for precipitates. Stop infusion and evaluate if pulmonary distress occurs.
- Hepatic effects:
  - Increases in liver enzymes (transaminases) and bile acids have been reported; monitor liver function tests in patients receiving the product, particularly in those with cholestasis or hepatic jaundice.
  - Cases of hepatobiliary disorders can occur in patients on parenteral nutrition; assess and manage hepatobiliary abnormalities early.
- Vitamin B12/folate considerations:
  - Evaluate vitamin B12 status before initiating supplementation in patients at risk for deficiency or if prolonged administration planned. Folic acid alone may correct anaemia but not prevent or may mask neurologic progression in B12 deficiency.
- Laboratory interferences:
  - Ascorbic acid may interfere with some blood and urine glucose testing systems (can produce falsely high or low glucose readings depending on assay).
  - Biotin can interfere with assays using biotin‑streptavidin methodology (may cause falsely decreased or increased results); risk is higher with renal impairment, children, and with higher doses.
- Light and oxygen sensitivity:
  - Some vitamins (A, D, pyridoxine, riboflavin) are light sensitive; protect admixtures from light. Higher oxygen levels in solution may increase loss of vitamins A, B1, C and E.
- Absorption onto plastics:
  - Fat‑soluble vitamins, particularly vitamin A, may adsorb to PVC bag/plastic tubing; the product uses mixed micelles to reduce but not eliminate this phenomenon. Use of non‑PVC bags may prevent adsorption.
- Monitoring:
  - Monitor clinical status and vitamin levels, particularly when used as sole source of vitamins for prolonged periods. Monitor specific vitamins where indicated (e.g., Vitamin A in wound/burn/short bowel disease; B1 in dialysis patients; B2 in cancer patients; B6 in renal impairment). Monitor liver function tests as above.
- Vitamin K:
  - Product does not contain vitamin K; administer separately if required.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Administration rate:
  - Some hypersensitivity reactions are rate‑related; administer slowly and cease administration immediately on signs of hypersensitivity.
- Use in renal impairment:
  - Patients with renal impairment may require individualized vitamin dosing; particular attention to vitamin D status and prevention of vitamin A toxicity is recommended. Pyridoxine toxicity (neuropathy, involuntary movements) has been reported in dialysis patients receiving repeated parenteral multivitamins containing pyridoxine.
- Use in hepatic impairment:
  - Contraindicated in patients with impaired hepatic function (see Section 5).
- Use in the elderly:
  - No data available in available sources.
- Paediatric use:
  - Indication includes children over 11 years but otherwise data are limited; see Section 10.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Glycocholic acid (component of mixed micelles) increased the unbound fraction in vitro of drugs that bind to alpha‑1‑acid glycoprotein by 50–80%. Clinical relevance at the dose in this product is unknown. Patients receiving drugs highly bound to alpha‑1‑acid glycoprotein (AAG) should be closely monitored for increased pharmacologic response; examples include propranolol, prazosin, quinidine.
- Fat‑soluble vitamins (notably vitamin A) may adsorb to PVC bags and tubing; this may reduce delivered dose. Mixed micelles reduce but do not eliminate adsorption. Use of non‑PVC bags may reduce this interaction.

### Pharmacodynamic and other clinically significant interactions
- Folic acid:
  - May influence the serum concentrations or effects of anticonvulsants (e.g., phenytoin) and may obscure pernicious anaemia.
  - May decrease the antimetabolite effects of folate antagonists/antimetabolites (e.g., methotrexate, raltitrexed) and can interact with fluoropyrimidines (increasing cytotoxicity when combined with 5‑fluorouracil, capecitabine, tegafur).
  - May increase metabolism of some antiepileptics (phenobarbital, phenytoin, primidone).
  - May be reduced by high‑dose aspirin (increased urinary loss).
- Pyridoxine (vitamin B6):
  - Can reduce the effect of levodopa.
  - Antagonists or agents causing pyridoxine deficiency include cycloserine, hydralazine, isoniazid, penicillamine, phenelzine.
  - Theophylline can cause pyridoxine deficiency.
  - Ethionamide can cause pyridoxine deficiency.
  - Pyridoxine hypervitaminosis and toxicity (peripheral neuropathy) reported in dialysis patients with repeated dosing.
- Ascorbic acid and riboflavin:
  - Can inactivate bleomycin (in vitro / reported interaction).
- Several vitamins can decrease effectiveness of certain antibiotics (e.g., tetracyclines).
- Vitamin E:
  - May enhance anticoagulant effect of vitamin K antagonists (e.g., warfarin).
  - May add to platelet inhibition with antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin).
- Vitamin A:
  - Concomitant use with agents that can cause pseudotumor cerebri (including certain tetracyclines) may increase risk of pseudotumor cerebri.
  - Retinoids (including bexarotene) may increase risk of vitamin A toxicity when used concomitantly.
- Antiretroviral agents:
  - Some antiretroviral agents (e.g., efavirenz, zidovudine) associated with decreased vitamin D levels; protease inhibitors associated with decreased formation of active vitamin D metabolite.
- Chloramphenicol:
  - Can inhibit haematological response to vitamin B12 therapy.
- Deferoxamine:
  - Combined with supraphysiologic vitamin C supplementation may increase risk of iron mobilization and iron‑related cardiac failure (refer to deferoxamine product information for specifics).
- Tipranavir oral solution:
  - Contains high vitamin E content (example given): may exceed recommended intake when co‑administered.
- Folate antagonists (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, pyrimethamine, triamterene, trimethoprim) and high doses of tea catechins:
  - Can block conversion of folate to active metabolites and reduce effectiveness of folate supplementation.

### Management notes
- Evaluate need for dose adjustments of concomitant medications where interactions are suspected.
- When co‑administration of incompatible drugs at a Y‑site is unavoidable, use separate IV lines.
- Confirm compatibility and stability before adding other medicinal products to parenteral nutrition admixtures containing this product.
- Protect admixtures from light and minimize oxygen exposure to preserve labile vitamins.
- Consider using non‑PVC containers or tallies when fat‑soluble vitamin adsorption is a concern.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- Pregnancy:
  - Pregnancy category/system: **Category D [Australian]** (product contains vitamin A in amounts that exceed some pregnancy recommendations).
  - The product contains vitamin A in an amount that is below the RDA adopted by the FDA/USA but above the NHMRC recommendation for pregnant and lactating women; therefore use in pregnancy is not recommended.
  - Teratogenic effects have been observed in isolated cases with vitamin A doses >10,000 IU/day.
- Breastfeeding:
  - It is not known whether the product is excreted in human milk; because many vitamins are excreted and the product contains vitamin A above some recommended intakes, use in lactation is not recommended.
- Fertility:
  - No fertility studies have been performed with this multivitamin combination. Not specified in available sources otherwise.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
- Paediatric use:
  - Indicated for adults and children over 11 years. However, detailed paediatric safety and efficacy data are limited. The source states "Paediatric use: No data available" beyond the age >11 indication.
- Elderly:
  - No specific data available in available sources; exercise clinical judgment and monitor for vitamin toxicity and hepatic/renal issues.
- Renal impairment:
  - Patients with renal impairment may require individualized vitamin supplementation depending on degree of impairment and comorbidities.
  - Particular attention required to maintain adequate vitamin D and to avoid vitamin A toxicity, which can occur in patients with reduced renal function even at low doses.
  - Pyridoxine hypervitaminosis has been reported in chronic haemodialysis patients receiving repeated parenteral multivitamin regimens.
- Hepatic impairment:
  - Contraindicated in patients with impaired hepatic function.
  - Monitor liver enzymes in patients receiving the product; avoid use in patients with hepatic impairment.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Combination product providing supplementation of multiple water‑ and fat‑soluble vitamins to correct or prevent deficiencies when enteral administration is not possible or inadequate.
- Fat‑soluble vitamins are solubilized in mixed micelles (glycocholic acid and lecithin) to permit parenteral administration of both lipophilic and hydrophilic vitamins together.
- Glycine is present as an excipient to facilitate reconstitution.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Detailed pharmacokinetic parameters (absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination) for the multivitamin combination are not specified in available sources.
- In vitro data: glycocholic acid increased the unbound fraction of AAG‑binding drugs in human serum; clinical relevance at administered doses is unknown.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Individual vitamin pharmacodynamic effects are those expected of the constituent vitamins (not exhaustively detailed in available sources). Specific pharmacodynamic descriptions beyond the above are not provided in available sources.
