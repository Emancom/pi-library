# Editorial Product Information: Quinine Dihydrochloride

## Editorial Note
- This editorial synthesis is based on the official Product Information (PI) provided by the **Australian (TGA)** regulatory authority. 
- The purpose of this document is to provide a harmonised editorial overview for healthcare professionals. 
- **Note:** This document is an editorial synthesis and does not serve as a substitute for the official, jurisdiction-specific Product Information required for prescribing. At the time of writing, only the Australian PI was provided for review; therefore, all specific regulatory classifications and clinical nuances are attributed to that jurisdiction [Australian].

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Quinine dihydrochloride (Chemical class: Cinchona alkaloid)
- **Brand Name(s):** Quinine Dihydrochloride 6% (Phebra) [Australian]
- **ATC Code:** P01BC01
- **Dosage Form(s) and Strength(s):** Sterile concentrated solution for injection; 600 mg quinine dihydrochloride in 10 mL (6% w/v) [Australian].
- **Route(s) of Administration:** Intravenous (IV) infusion (preferred); Intramuscular (IM) injection (last resort).
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Phebra Pty Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Acute Treatment of Malaria:** Indicated for the management of acute malarial infections [Australian].
- **Babesiosis:** Used in conjunction with clindamycin for the treatment of Babesiosis [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adults)
Quinine must be diluted before administration and infused slowly. Patients should be transitioned to oral therapy as soon as clinically feasible [Australian].

- **Loading Dose:** 20 mg/kg (maximum 1400 mg) administered via slow IV infusion over 4 hours. A loading dose is omitted if the patient received other anti-malarials within the preceding 24 hours [Australian].
- **Maintenance Dose:** 10 mg/kg (maximum 700 mg) administered over 4 hours via slow IV infusion. This should be repeated every 8–12 hours as required, starting 8–12 hours after the loading dose [Australian].
- **Prolonged Therapy:** If parenteral therapy exceeds 48 hours, the maintenance dose must be reduced by one-third to one-half (to approximately 5 mg/kg) to prevent drug accumulation. Plasma level monitoring is strongly recommended in these cases [Australian].
- **Intensive Care Setting:** In ICU settings only, a loading dose of 7 mg/kg may be administered over 30 minutes, followed immediately by maintenance infusions [Australian].

### Administration Instructions
- **Dilution:** Must be diluted in 500 mL of Glucose 5% (preferred) or Sodium Chloride 0.9% [Australian].
- **IM Injection:** Should only be used as a "last resort" if IV access is impossible. It is associated with pain, focal necrosis, abscess formation, and concerns regarding efficacy and the risk of tetanus [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
- Hypersensitivity to quinine or quinidine [Australian].
- Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency [Australian].
- History of Blackwater Fever [Australian].
- Presence of tinnitus or optic neuritis [Australian].
- Presence of active haemolysis [Australian].
- Concurrent anticoagulant therapy [Australian].
- Patients with diabetes [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Cardiotoxicity:** Quinine may cause severe cardiotoxicity if administered too rapidly. Close monitoring of pulse and blood pressure is mandatory during IV infusion. Infusion rates should be slowed if dysrhythmias occur [Australian].
- **Hypoglycaemia:** Quinine can stimulate insulin release and aggravate hypoglycaemia; blood glucose monitoring is required [Australian].
- **Haemolysis:** Therapy must be discontinued immediately if signs of haemolysis appear, particularly in G6PD-deficient patients where it may lead to haemolytic anaemia [Australian].
- **Hypoprothrombinaemia:** Quinine may lower prothrombin levels, potentially enhancing the effects of anticoagulants [Australian].
- **Atrial Fibrillation:** Patients with AF should be digitalised prior to quinine therapy to prevent an excessive increase in ventricular rate [Australian].
- **Hypersensitivity:** Most commonly manifests as extreme cutaneous flushing and intense pruritus. Therapy must be stopped if hypersensitivity occurs [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
The primary syndrome associated with repeated quinine use is **Cinchonism**.

| System | Adverse Effects |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Cinchonism** | Tinnitus, impaired hearing, headache, nausea, visual disturbances, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, vertigo. |
| **Haematological** | Acute haemolysis, thrombocytopenic purpura (potentially fatal), agranulocytosis, hypoprothrombinaemia. |
| **Cardiovascular** | Arrhythmias, QRS widening, hypotension, ventricular tachycardia, angioedema, angina symptoms. Fatal toxicity can occur with rapid IV injection. |
| **Central Nervous System** | Visual: Blurred vision, scotomata, photophobia, diplopia, night blindness, optic atrophy (blindness). Severe: Confusion, syncope, deafness. |
| **Dermatological** | Rashes, urticaria, pruritus, facial oedema, photosensitivity. |
| **Other** | Hepatotoxicity, asthma precipitation, renal failure (anuria, uraemia), aggravation of myasthenia gravis. |

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic Interactions
- **Urinary Alkalinisers (e.g., Acetazolamide, Sodium Bicarbonate):** Increase blood quinine levels by reducing renal clearance [Australian].
- **Urinary Acidifiers (e.g., Ammonium Chloride):** Decrease blood quinine levels by increasing renal excretion [Australian].
- **Cimetidine:** May reduce quinine clearance, increasing plasma concentrations [Australian].
- **Digoxin:** Quinine may increase serum digoxin concentrations; monitoring and dose adjustment of digoxin may be necessary [Australian].

### Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- **Neuromuscular Blocking Agents:** Quinine may significantly potentiate the effects of both depolarising and non-depolarising blockers (e.g., pancuronium, suxamethonium, vecuronium), potentially leading to respiratory difficulties [Australian].
- **Anticoagulants:** Increased risk of bleeding/hypoprothrombinaemic effects with warfarin or coumarin derivatives [Australian].
- **Antimalarials:** Pyrimethamine may displace quinine from protein binding sites; mefloquine may increase toxicity [Australian].
- **Inhalation Anaesthetics:** Potentiate neuromuscular blockade in a dose-dependent manner (especially isoflurane, enflurane, sevoflurane) [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** [Australian Category: Not specifically letter-coded in source, but stated as "acceptable" for life-threatening malaria]. While benefits outweigh risks in severe malaria, high doses are associated with fetal injury, including deafness, cranial/extremity malformations, and uterine contractions (risk of abortion) [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Quinine is excreted in small concentrations in breast milk. Exercise caution during breastfeeding [Australian].
- **Fertility:** No data available in source [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** IV dose is 10 mg/kg, diluted and infused slowly at a rate not exceeding 0.5 mg/minute, preferably with ECG monitoring [Australian].
- **Elderly:** No specific data available in source [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Half-life is prolonged in patients with hepatitis or moderate chronic liver disease; dose adjustments or monitoring may be required [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Not specified in source, though reduction of maintenance doses is advised for prolonged therapy to avoid accumulation [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of Action
The exact mechanism is not fully elucidated but appears to involve interference with plasmodial DNA function (inhibiting protein synthesis and strand separation). It is primarily **schizonticidal** and gametocytocidal for *P. vivax* and *P. malariae*. It has no effect on sporozoites or pre-erythrocytic forms [Australian].

### Pharmacokinetics
- **Distribution:** Apparent volume of distribution is reduced during active malarial infection. Protein binding is ~70% in healthy individuals but rises to ≥90% during malaria [Australian].
- **Metabolism:** Metabolised in the liver. Half-life is ~11 hours in healthy subjects but may be prolonged during infection [Australian].
- **Excretion:** Primarily renal. Excretion is pH-dependent (increased in acidic urine) [Australian].

## 12. Storage Conditions
- **Storage:** Store below 25°C and protect from light [Australian].
- **Shelf Life:** Refer to the expiry date on the packaging [Australian].
- **Handling:** Sterile concentrate; contains no antimicrobial preservative. For single-use in one patient only; discard any unused portion [Australian].
