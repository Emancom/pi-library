# Editorial Product Information: Insulin Glargine

**Editorial Note:** This unified Product Information (PI) document has been compiled by synthesizing official regulatory data from the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA). It combines clinical guidance, safety specifications, and pharmacological data to provide a comprehensive reference for healthcare professionals. Where regional guidelines differ, the most conservative or detailed information is provided and cited accordingly.

---

# 1. Product Name and Active Ingredient
**Active Ingredient:** Insulin glargine (rDNA origin).

Insulin glargine is a long-acting human insulin analogue produced by recombinant DNA technology. It differs from naturally occurring human insulin through the replacement of asparagine with glycine at position A21 and the addition of two arginines at the C-terminus of the B-chain.

# 2. Approved Indications
Insulin glargine is indicated for the improvement of glycemic control in:
*   **Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus:** Adult and pediatric patients. (TGA specifically notes safety in children aged 6 years and older; FDA provides a broader pediatric indication).
*   **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus:** Adult patients who require insulin for the management of hyperglycemia.

**Limitations of Use:** This product is not recommended for the treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Intravenous short-acting insulin is the preferred treatment in such cases [FDA, TGA].

# 3. Contraindications
Insulin glargine is contraindicated in the following circumstances:
*   During episodes of hypoglycemia [FDA].
*   In patients with known hypersensitivity to insulin glargine or any of the inactive excipients (such as metacresol, zinc chloride, or glycerol).

# 4. Warnings and Precautions
### General Management
*   **Medication Errors:** Patients must be instructed to verify the insulin label before every injection to prevent accidental mix-ups between insulin glargine and other insulin products, particularly short-acting varieties.
*   **Never Share Pens:** Prefilled pens must never be shared between patients, even if the needle is changed, due to the risk of transmitting blood-borne pathogens [FDA].

### Glycemic Variations
*   **Hypoglycemia:** This is the most frequent adverse reaction. Severe episodes can lead to seizures, neurological damage, or death. Recovery may be delayed due to the long-acting nature of the drug.
*   **Regimen Changes:** Any change in insulin strength, manufacturer, type, or method of administration should be conducted under strict medical supervision. Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring is required during the transition.
*   **Hypoglycemia Unawareness:** Warning symptoms may be diminished or absent in patients with long-standing diabetes, diabetic neuropathy, or those using concurrent medications such as beta-blockers.

### Physiological Complications
*   **Hypokalemia:** All insulin products cause a shift in potassium from the extracellular to the intracellular space. If untreated, this may lead to respiratory paralysis, ventricular arrhythmia, and death. Use with caution in patients at risk for hypokalemia (e.g., those on potassium-lowering medications) [FDA].
*   **Fluid Retention and Heart Failure:** When used in combination with thiazolidinediones (TZDs) or PPAR-gamma agonists, patients should be observed for signs of heart failure. If symptoms occur, dosage reduction or discontinuation of the TZD should be considered [FDA].

### Injection Site Management
*   **Site Rotation:** Continuous rotation of the injection site within the abdominal, thigh, or deltoid areas is essential to reduce the risk of lipodystrophy and localized cutaneous amyloidosis. Injecting into affected areas may delay insulin absorption and worsen glycemic control.

# 5. Dosage and Administration
### Administration Instructions
*   **Route:** Subcutaneous injection only. Do **not** administer intravenously or via an insulin pump.
*   **Frequency:** Once daily at any time of the day, but strictly at the same time every day.
*   **Preparation:** The solution must be clear and colorless. Do not dilute or mix with any other insulin or solution, as this can alter the profile of action or cause precipitation.

### Initiation of Therapy
*   **Type 1 Diabetes:** Usually initiated at approximately one-third of the total daily insulin requirement. Short-acting, pre-meal insulin should be used to cover the remaining needs [FDA].
*   **Type 2 Diabetes:** In patients not currently on insulin, a conservative starting dose is 0.2 units/kg or up to 10 units once daily [FDA].

### Switching from Other Insulins
*   **From Once-Daily NPH:** A 1:1 dose conversion is generally used [FDA], though initial doses should be adjusted based on individual patient response [TGA].
*   **From Twice-Daily NPH:** To reduce the risk of hypoglycemia, the initial total daily dose of insulin glargine should be reduced by approximately 20% compared to the previous total daily NPH dose.
*   **From 300 units/mL Glargine:** The recommended starting dose is 80% of the 300 units/mL dose being discontinued [FDA, TGA].

# 6. Adverse Effects
*   **Hypoglycemia:** The most common adverse effect. Clinical trials indicate a lower incidence of nocturnal hypoglycemia in Type 2 patients compared to NPH insulin [TGA].
*   **Injection Site Reactions:** Redness, pain, itching, hives, swelling, and inflammation. These are usually minor and resolve within days.
*   **Lipodystrophy:** Lipoatrophy or lipohypertrophy can occur at the injection site.
*   **Weight Gain:** Attributed to the anabolic effects of insulin. Long-term studies (ORIGIN) showed a median weight gain of 1.4kg over six years [TGA].
*   **Allergic Reactions:** Rare but potentially life-threatening systemic allergies, including anaphylaxis, generalized skin reactions, and angioedema.
*   **Peripheral Edema:** Sodium retention may occur, particularly if metabolic control is rapidly improved after a period of poor control.

# 7. Drug Interactions
*   **Increased Hypoglycemic Effect:** Oral antidiabetic agents, ACE inhibitors, fibrates, fluoxetine, MAO inhibitors, salicylates, and sulfonamide antibiotics.
*   **Decreased Hypoglycemic Effect:** Corticosteroids, danazol, diuretics, estrogens, progestogens, thyroid hormones, atypical antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine, clozapine), and sympathomimetic agents (e.g., epinephrine, salbutamol).
*   **Variable Effects:** Alcohol, beta-blockers, and lithium salts may either potentiate or weaken the glucose-lowering effect. Pentamidine may cause hypoglycemia followed by hyperglycemia.
*   **Masked Symptoms:** Sympatholytic products like beta-blockers, clonidine, and guanethidine may reduce or suppress the adrenergic counter-regulatory signs of hypoglycemia.

# 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
**TGA Category B3.** There are no randomized controlled trials in pregnant women. However, a large body of post-marketing data (over 1000 outcomes) indicates no specific adverse effects on the fetus or pregnancy. Insulin requirements usually decrease in the first trimester and increase during the second and third trimesters. Careful metabolic monitoring is essential to prevent adverse outcomes associated with hyperglycemia.

### Breastfeeding
It is unknown if insulin glargine is excreted in human milk. Since endogenous insulin is a component of human milk, no adverse effects are expected in the nursing infant. However, lactating women may require adjustments in insulin dosage and dietary intake.

# 9. Pharmacokinetics
*   **Absorption:** After subcutaneous injection, the acidic solution (pH 4.0) is neutralized in the tissue, forming microprecipitates. This allows for the slow, continuous release of small amounts of insulin.
*   **Duration:** Provides a smooth, peakless concentration profile for approximately 24 hours.
*   **Metabolism:** Rapidly metabolized into two active metabolites, M1 and M2. The pharmacological effect is primarily attributed to M1.
*   **Special Populations:** While not specifically studied, renal or hepatic impairment may reduce insulin requirements due to decreased insulin metabolism or gluconeogenesis capacity.

# 10. Mechanism of Action
The primary activity of insulin glargine is the regulation of glucose metabolism. It lowers blood glucose by:
1.  Stimulating peripheral glucose uptake, particularly by skeletal muscle and adipose tissue.
2.  Inhibiting hepatic glucose production.
3.  Inhibiting lipolysis and proteolysis while enhancing protein synthesis.

# 11. Storage Conditions
### Unopened (Not in Use)
*   Store in a refrigerator between **2°C and 8°C**.
*   **Do not freeze.** Discard if the product has been frozen.
*   Keep the pen or cartridge in the outer carton to protect it from light.

### Opened (In Use)
*   **Prefilled Pens:** Once in use, pens should **not** be stored in the refrigerator [TGA/FDA]. They must be kept at room temperature (up to 30°C) and away from direct heat and light.
*   **Discard Date:** All components (pens or cartridges) must be discarded **28 days** after the first use, even if they still contain insulin.
*   **Storage Table Summary [FDA]:**
    *   Unopened (Fridge): Until expiration date.
    *   Unopened (Room Temp): 28 days.
    *   In-use (Room Temp): 28 days (Do not refrigerate).
