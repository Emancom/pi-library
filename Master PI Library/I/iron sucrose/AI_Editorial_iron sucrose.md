## 4. Therapeutic Indications

### Editorial Version
Iron sucrose is indicated for the treatment of iron deficiency anemia (IDA) in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

The diagnosis of iron deficiency must be confirmed through appropriate laboratory investigations, such as serum ferritin, transferrin saturation (TSAT), serum iron, or the presence of hypochromic red cells. (Source: Australian Product Information)

Specific regional indication parameters include:
*   **Australia:** Specifically indicated for patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis who are also receiving supplemental erythropoietin therapy. (Source: Australian Product Information)
*   **United States:** Indicated broadly for IDA in CKD, encompassing non-dialysis dependent (NDD), dialysis-dependent (HDD), and peritoneal dialysis-dependent (PDD) patients. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

----------------------------------------

## 5. Dosage and Administration

### Editorial Version
Iron sucrose must be administered exclusively by the intravenous route, either via slow injection or infusion. The dosage is always expressed in milligrams (mg) of elemental iron. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

#### Adult Dosage Regimens
The treatment course and frequency depend on the patient's dialysis status and clinical requirement.

**Hemodialysis Dependent-CKD (HDD-CKD)**
*   **Standard Dose:** 100 mg of iron administered during each dialysis session. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Frequency:** Up to three times per week. (Source: Australian Product Information)
*   **Administration Timing:** In the United States, it is recommended to administer the dose early during the dialysis session (usually within the first hour). (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Cumulative Dose:** Most patients require a total of 1000 mg (delivered over 10 sessions) to achieve a clinical response. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

**Non-Dialysis Dependent-CKD (NDD-CKD)**
*   **Standard Regimen:** 200 mg administered on five separate occasions within a 14-day period (Total dose 1000 mg). (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Alternative High-Dose Regimen:** 500 mg infused on Day 1 and Day 14. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

**Peritoneal Dialysis Dependent-CKD (PDD-CKD)**
*   **Regimen:** 1000 mg total dose delivered in three divided doses over 28 days:
    *   Two 300 mg infusions (14 days apart).
    *   One 400 mg infusion (14 days after the second dose). (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

#### Pediatric Dosage (Aged 2 years and older)
For iron maintenance in children with CKD on erythropoietin:
*   **Dose:** 0.5 mg/kg (not exceeding 100 mg per dose).
*   **HDD-CKD Frequency:** Every two weeks for 12 weeks.
*   **NDD/PDD-CKD Frequency:** Every four weeks for 12 weeks. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

#### Administration Methods
**Slow IV Injection**
*   **Undiluted:** Administer at a rate of 1 mL (20 mg iron) per minute. (Source: Australian Product Information)
*   **United States Recommendation:** 100 mg or 200 mg doses may be given over 2 to 5 minutes. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

**IV Infusion**
*   **Dilution:** Dilute 100 mg exclusively in a maximum of 100 mL of 0.9% NaCl. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Infusion Rate:** 100 mg should be infused over at least 15 minutes. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **High-Dose Infusion (US):** 300 mg over 1.5 hours; 400 mg over 2.5 hours; 500 mg over 3.5 to 4 hours. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

## 7. Contraindications

### Editorial Version
Iron sucrose is contraindicated in the following circumstances:
*   Known hypersensitivity to iron sucrose or any of its inactive ingredients. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   Anemia that is not caused by iron deficiency. (Source: Australian Product Information)
*   Conditions of iron overload (e.g., hemochromatosis, hemosiderosis) or disturbances in iron utilization. (Source: Australian Product Information)
*   **Australia specific:** Use during the first trimester of pregnancy is contraindicated. (Source: Australian Product Information)

## 8. Warnings and Precautions

### Editorial Version
#### Hypersensitivity Reactions
Parenteral iron preparations may cause serious, potentially fatal anaphylactic-type reactions. Patients may present with shock, clinically significant hypotension, loss of consciousness, or collapse. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Kounis Syndrome:** Reports exist of hypersensitivity reactions progressing to acute allergic coronary arteriospasm (Kounis syndrome), which may lead to myocardial infarction. (Source: Australian Product Information)
*   **Monitoring:** Clinicians must observe patients for at least 30 minutes following every administration. Facilities for cardiopulmonary resuscitation and anti-allergic treatments must be immediately available. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **History of Allergy:** Caution is required in patients with asthma, eczema, or other atopic allergies. There are no safety data for patients with a known allergy to iron polymaltose. (Source: Australian Product Information)

#### Hypotension
Intravenous iron may cause significant hypotension, which may be related to the total dose or the rate of administration. Monitor blood pressure during and following each treatment. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

#### Iron Overload
Excessive therapy can lead to iatrogenic hemosiderosis. Hematologic and iron parameters (ferritin, TSAT, hemoglobin) must be monitored regularly. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Note:** Serum iron measurements are unreliable for at least 48 hours following an intravenous dose due to rapid increases in TSAT. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

#### Infection
Exercise caution in patients with acute or chronic infection. In patients with bacteremia, it is recommended to discontinue iron sucrose administration. (Source: Australian Product Information)

#### Hepatic Dysfunction
Administer only after a careful risk-benefit assessment in patients with liver disease. Avoid administration if iron overload is the precipitating factor for the dysfunction. (Source: Australian Product Information)

#### Extravasation
Paravenous infiltration must be avoided as it can cause pain, inflammation, sterile abscesses, and permanent brown skin discoloration. If infiltration occurs, stop the infusion immediately. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

----------------------------------------

## 9. Pregnancy

### Editorial Version
**Pregnancy Category B3 (Australia)**

Iron sucrose should only be used during pregnancy if the potential benefit justifies the risk to the fetus. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

*   **First Trimester:** Contraindicated in Australia. (Source: Australian Product Information) United States data are insufficient to assess the risk of major birth defects or miscarriage during this period. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Second and Third Trimesters:** Human data from randomized controlled trials and observational studies have not shown adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Fetal Risk:** Maternal hypersensitivity reactions to parenteral iron can cause fetal bradycardia, particularly during the second and third trimesters. The unborn baby should be monitored during administration to pregnant women. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

----------------------------------------

## 10. Breastfeeding

### Editorial Version

## 12. Adverse Effects

### Editorial Version
The most frequently reported adverse reactions include hypotension, nausea, vomiting, headache, dizziness, and dysgeusia (metallic taste). (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

#### Frequency of Adverse Reactions in Adult CKD Patients (≥2%)
*   **Gastrointestinal:** Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain.
*   **Nervous System:** Headache, dizziness, dysgeusia.
*   **Vascular:** Hypotension, hypertension.
*   **Musculoskeletal:** Muscle cramps, arthralgia, back pain, pain in extremity.
*   **General:** Injection site reactions (burning, pain), peripheral edema, chest pain, asthenia, pyrexia.
*   **Infections:** Nasopharyngitis, upper respiratory tract infections. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

#### Pediatric-Specific Reactions (≥2%)
Headache, respiratory tract viral infection, peritonitis, vomiting, pyrexia, dizziness, cough, nausea, arteriovenous fistula thrombosis, hypotension, and hypertension. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

#### Post-Marketing and Rare Experience
The following have been reported with an unknown frequency or rarely (<0.1%):
*   **Immune:** Anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema.
*   **Cardiac:** Bradycardia, tachycardia, palpitations, congestive heart failure.
*   **Vascular:** Circulatory collapse, thrombophlebitis.
*   **Nervous:** Convulsions, loss of consciousness, confusion, paraesthesia, tremor.
*   **Respiratory:** Bronchospasm, dyspnea.
*   **Skin:** Pruritus, rash, urticaria, erythema, hyperhidrosis.
*   **Renal:** Chromaturia (darkening of urine). (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

## 18. Mechanism of Action

### Editorial Version
Iron sucrose is an aqueous complex of polynuclear iron (III)-hydroxide in sucrose. Upon intravenous administration, the complex is dissociated into iron and sucrose. The iron is then taken up by the reticuloendothelial system (specifically macrophages in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow) and transported via transferrin to target cells, such as erythroid precursor cells, where it is incorporated into hemoglobin as red blood cells mature. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

----------------------------------------

## 20. Pharmacokinetics

### Editorial Version
#### Distribution
The iron component distributes primarily within the blood and extravascular fluid. Studies using labelled iron (${}^{52}\text{Fe}/{}^{59}\text{Fe}$) demonstrate that the bone marrow is an "iron trapping" compartment rather than a reversible volume of distribution. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

#### Metabolism and Elimination
*   **Dissociation:** The complex dissociates into polynuclear iron and sucrose.
*   **Sucrose Elimination:** The sucrose component is mainly eliminated via urinary excretion (approx. 68% in 4 hours and 75% in 24 hours). (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Iron Elimination:** Approximately 5% of the iron dose is eliminated in the urine over 24 hours. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Half-life:** The elimination half-life of the iron component in healthy adults is approximately 6 hours. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Clearance:** Total clearance is 1.2 L/h. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)

#### Specific Populations
*   **Pediatrics:** In children (ages 12-16) with NDD-CKD, the half-life was approximately 8 hours, and exposure (AUC) was higher compared to dose-adjusted adult values. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Gender/Age:** The effects of gender and age on pharmacokinetics have not been formally studied. (Source: Australian Product Information)

----------------------------------------

**Australian Product Information**
• Lists non-steady state volume of distribution (10.0 L) and steady-state volume (7.9 L).

## 21. Clinical Trials

### Editorial Version
Iron sucrose has been evaluated in multiple clinical trials involving over 600 adult patients and over 130 pediatric patients across various CKD populations.

*   **HDD-CKD (Adults):** Pivotal trials demonstrated that 100 mg doses administered over 10 dialysis sessions significantly increased hemoglobin and hematocrit compared to historical controls or oral iron. One study showed a mean hemoglobin increase of 1.0–1.3 g/dL. (Source: Australian Product Information; United States Prescribing Information)
*   **NDD-CKD (Adults):** Patients receiving five 200 mg doses or two 500 mg doses showed a significantly higher proportion of responders (hemoglobin increase ≥1 g/dL) compared to those on oral iron (44.3% vs. 28%). (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **PDD-CKD (Adults):** A total dose of 1000 mg (split into 300 mg and 400 mg doses) resulted in a mean hemoglobin increase of 1.3 g/dL, compared to 0.6 g/dL in those receiving erythropoietin alone. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)
*   **Pediatric Maintenance:** A dose-ranging study (0.5, 1, and 2 mg/kg) found that all doses maintained hemoglobin between 10.5 and 14.0 g/dL in approximately 50% of patients over 12 weeks. (Source: United States Prescribing Information)

----------------------------------------

• Provides detailed summaries of Study A through F, covering HDD, NDD, PDD, and pediatric cohorts.
