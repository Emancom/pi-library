# Editorial Product Information: Isoniazid

## Editorial Note
- **Contributing Jurisdictions:** Australian (Therapeutic Goods Administration)
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview of Isoniazid product information for use by healthcare professionals. It synthesises the available data into a clinically relevant reference.
- **Disclaimer:** This is an editorial synthesis and does not substitute for the official Product Information (PI) required for prescribing in a specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should consult the current local PI for regulatory compliance.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Isoniazid (isonicotinylhydrazine), a tuberculostatic agent.
- **Brand Names:** Not specified in available sources [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** Not specified in available sources.
- **Dosage Form(s) and Strength(s):** Scored white tablets containing 100 mg of isoniazid [Australian].
- **Route(s) of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Arrotex Pharmaceuticals Pty Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Tuberculosis (TB):** Indicated for the treatment of both pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis. It must be used in combination with other antitubercular agents to prevent the development of resistance [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adult)
- **Standard Dose:** 4 to 5 mg/kg body weight daily, administered in divided doses. The maximum daily dose is 300 mg [Australian].
- **Tuberculous Meningitis:** Up to 10 mg/kg daily may be administered during the initial 1 to 2 weeks of therapy [Australian].

### Paediatric Dosing
- **Standard Dose:** 5 to 20 mg/kg body weight daily [Australian].

### Administration Instructions
- Isoniazid is administered orally.
- **Food Effect:** Absorption and peak plasma concentrations may be reduced when taken with food [Australian].
- **Antacids:** If aluminium hydroxide gel is required, isoniazid should be administered at least 1 hour prior to the antacid [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to isoniazid or any excipients (including sodium benzoate, propyl hydroxybenzoate, lactose monohydrate, and gluten) [Australian].
- History of severe hypersensitivity reactions to isoniazid, including drug-induced hepatitis [Australian].
- Previous hepatic injury associated with isoniazid [Australian].
- Acute hepatic disease of any etiology [Australian].
- History of severe adverse reactions such as drug fever, chills, or arthritis [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
### Severe Hepatitis
- **Boxed Warning (Equivalent):** Severe and potentially fatal hepatitis may occur, even after several months of therapy. The risk is age-related and increased by daily alcohol consumption and pre-existing liver disease [Australian].
- **Age-Related Risk Rates:** 
    - <20 years: 0/1,000
    - 20–34 years: 3/1,000
    - 35–49 years: 12/1,000
    - 50–64 years: 23/1,000
    - >65 years: 8/1,000
- **Monitoring:** Baseline liver function tests (LFTs) are essential, followed by regular monitoring. Treatment must be discontinued if serum AST exceeds three times the upper limit of normal or if bilirubin increases [Australian].

### Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions (SCARs)
- Life-threatening reactions, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP), have been reported. Isoniazid must be permanently discontinued if these occur [Australian].

### Neurological Effects
- **Peripheral Neuropathy:** The most common side effect; dose-related and more frequent in "slow acetylators," or patients with malnutrition, diabetes, or alcoholism.
- **Cerebellar Syndrome:** May present as ataxia, tremor, or nystagmus, particularly in patients with chronic kidney disease.
- **Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) Supplementation:** Recommended (100–300 mg daily) for patients at high risk of neuropathy or deficiency [Australian].

### General Precautions
- **Ophthalmological:** Optic neuritis and atrophy have been reported; baseline and periodic eye exams (including ophthalmoscopy) are recommended.
- **Psychiatric:** Use with caution in patients with a history of psychosis.
- **Hereditary Issues:** Patients with galactose intolerance or total lactase deficiency should avoid this medicine due to lactose content [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
- **Hepatic:** Transient transaminase elevation (10–20% of patients); progressive hepatic damage (rare in youth, up to 2.3% in patients over 50 years).
- **Nervous System:** Peripheral neuropathy (common), hyperreflexia (at doses ≥10 mg/kg), convulsions, toxic encephalopathy, memory impairment, and toxic psychosis.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Nausea, vomiting, epigastric distress, dry mouth, constipation, and acute pancreatitis.
- **Haematological:** Agranulocytosis, haemolytic/aplastic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and eosinophilia.
- **Hypersensitivity:** Fever, lymphadenopathy, vasculitis, and various skin eruptions.
- **Metabolic/Endocrine:** Pyridoxine deficiency, pellagra, hyperglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, and gynaecomastia.
- **Withdrawal:** Headache, insomnia, and irritability may occur upon cessation.

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic Interactions
- **Metabolic Inhibition:** Isoniazid inhibits the metabolism of phenytoin, carbamazepine, primidone, certain benzodiazepines (diazepam, triazolam), chlorzoxazone, and disulfiram, potentially leading to toxicity.
- **Rifampicin:** Increased risk of hepatotoxicity due to altered metabolism [Australian].
- **Zalcitabine:** Clearance of isoniazid may be doubled in HIV-positive patients [Australian].
- **Ketoconazole/Itraconazole:** Isoniazid may significantly decrease serum levels of these antifungals.

### Pharmacodynamic Interactions
- **MAO/DAO Inhibition:** Isoniazid inhibits monoamine oxidase and diamine oxidase. Ingestion of foods high in tyramine or histamine (e.g., matured cheese, red wine, tuna) may cause palpitations, flushing, and hypotension [Australian].
- **Paracetamol:** Increased formation of toxic metabolites via CYP450 induction.
- **Levodopa:** Reduced therapeutic effect.

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** [Australian Category A]. Isoniazid has been used by a large number of pregnant women without a proven increase in the frequency of malformations. It should only be prescribed when therapeutically necessary. Pyridoxine supplementation is recommended [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Isoniazid is excreted into breast milk at concentrations equal to maternal plasma. Breastfed infants should be monitored for adverse effects [Australian].
- **Fertility:** No data available [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** No specific problems identified, but newborns have limited acetylation capacity, resulting in a prolonged half-life [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Highest incidence of hepatitis (particularly those >50 years) [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Careful monitoring is required in severe renal dysfunction. Vertigo and tinnitus are more common in end-stage renal disease [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Requires intensive monitoring; isoniazid is contraindicated in acute hepatic disease [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** A tuberculostatic agent with bacteriostatic activity specific to *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. It likely interferes with the synthesis of the bacterial cell wall.
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption:** Rapid and complete oral absorption; peak levels in 1 to 2 hours.
    - **Distribution:** Distributed to all tissues and fluids, including CSF (90–100% of plasma levels). Crosses the placenta.
    - **Metabolism:** Primarily via hepatic acetylation and dehydrazination. Acetylation rate is genetically determined (50% of Caucasians/Blacks are "slow inactivators," while most Orientals/Eskimos are "rapid inactivators") [Australian].
    - **Elimination:** 50–70% excreted in urine within 24 hours.

- **Conditions:** Protect from light and moisture.
