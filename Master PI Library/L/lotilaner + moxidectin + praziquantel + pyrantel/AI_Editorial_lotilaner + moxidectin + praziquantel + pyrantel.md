# Editorial Product Information: lotilaner + moxidectin + praziquantel + pyrantel

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **lotilaner + moxidectin + praziquantel + pyrantel**
- Chemical class:
  - Lotilaner: isoxazoline (ectoparasiticide)
  - Moxidectin: macrocyclic lactone (endectocide; milbemycin class derivative)
  - Praziquantel: isoquinolone anthelmintic
  - Pyrantel: tetrahydropyrimidine anthelmintic (pyrantel pamoate salt)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Prevention of heartworm disease caused by Dirofilaria immitis in dogs and puppies 8 weeks of age and older, and weighing ≥ 3.3 lb.
- Treatment and control of intestinal nematodes:
  - Roundworms: immature adult and adult Toxocara canis; adult Toxascaris leonina
  - Hookworms: fourth-stage larvae, immature adult, and adult Ancylostoma caninum; adult Uncinaria stenocephala
- Treatment and control of cestodes (tapeworms):
  - Dipylidium caninum, Taenia pisiformis, Echinococcus granulosus
- Flea treatment and prevention:
  - Kills adult fleas (Ctenocephalides felis) — treatment and prevention of flea infestations
- Tick treatment and control (efficacy as noted below for listed species for approximately one month):
  - Amblyomma americanum (lone star tick)
  - Dermacentor variabilis (American dog tick)
  - Ixodes scapularis (black-legged tick)
  - Rhipicephalus sanguineus (brown dog tick)
  - Haemaphysalis longicornis (longhorned tick)
- Prevention of Borrelia burgdorferi infection as a consequence of killing Ixodes scapularis vector ticks
- [US — conditional approval] Treatment of infestations caused by New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) larvae (myiasis) in dogs and puppies 8 weeks of age and older and weighing ≥ 3.3 lb (conditional/CA1 approval pending full demonstration of effectiveness).

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (minimum labeled)
- Minimum labeled doses (per administration):
  - Lotilaner: 20 mg/kg (equivalent stated in some sources as 9 mg/lb)
  - Moxidectin: 0.02 mg/kg
  - Praziquantel: 5 mg/kg
  - Pyrantel (as pamoate salt): 5 mg/kg
- Frequency:
  - Generally administered once monthly for heartworm prevention, flea and tick control, and monthly control of gastrointestinal nematodes.
  - Single dose administration for treatment of existing intestinal nematode and cestode infections (monthly use will control subsequent infections).
  - [US — CA1] A single dose is reasonably expected to treat New World screwworm present at the time of treatment; mechanical removal of larvae and appropriate wound care should accompany treatment.

### Dosing by indication
- Heartworm prevention: monthly at minimum labeled dose; begin year-round or at least 1 month before first seasonal exposure to mosquitoes and continue until at least 1 month after last exposure.
- Intestinal nematode/cestode treatment: single dose at minimum labeled dose; repeat monthly if ongoing prophylaxis is required or to control reinfection.
- Flea and tick control: monthly at minimum labeled dose. Efficacy durations for listed species are reported as approximately one month.
- New World screwworm (where applicable [US — conditional]): single dose; expect larval expulsion and/or larvicidal activity by 24 hours; mechanical removal of remaining larvae and wound care recommended.

### Administration instructions
- Administer orally with food or within 30 minutes after feeding. This is required because lotilaner bioavailability is reduced in the fasted state.
- Ensure the dog consumes the entire dose; if vomiting occurs within 1 hour of administration, readminister a full new dose.
- If a monthly dose is missed, administer immediately and resume monthly schedule.

### Titration schedules
- Not specified in available sources

## 5. Contraindications
- There are no known absolute contraindications stated in the available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
- **Neurologic adverse reactions:** Lotilaner is an isoxazoline; this class has been associated with neurologic signs including tremors, ataxia, and seizures. Seizures have occurred in animals treated with isoxazoline compounds, including dogs without prior seizure history.
  - Use with caution in dogs with a history of seizures or other neurologic disorders.
- Test for pre-existing heartworm infection prior to initiating therapy. At the discretion of the veterinarian, dogs diagnosed with adult heartworm infection should be treated with an adulticide; this product is not effective against adult Dirofilaria immitis.
- **Breeding, pregnancy, lactation:** Safe use in breeding, pregnant, or lactating dogs has not been evaluated.
- New World screwworm treatment (where used): effective management includes mechanical removal of larvae (live and dead), lesion cleaning, debridement of necrotic tissue, wound care, and analgesia.
- Keep out of reach of children and humans; product is for veterinary use only. Wash hands after handling. If accidental human ingestion occurs, seek medical attention.
- Monitor for gastrointestinal and neurologic signs after dosing, particularly during initial months of use.
- Use caution in dogs with conditions that may predispose to adverse reactions (e.g., existing neurologic disease, severe systemic disease).
- In field studies, concurrent use with a range of common veterinary medications (vaccines, antimicrobials, anthelmintics, antiemetics, steroidal and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories, anesthetics, analgesics) was not associated with observed adverse reactions; however, clinicians should exercise standard caution when co-administering multiple therapeutics.

## 7. Adverse Effects
- All adverse effects listed below are reported in the available sources from field studies and safety trials. Frequencies are assigned using the following convention: very common (>10%), common (1–10%), uncommon (0.1–1%), rare (<0.1%). Frequencies given in source data are presented where available.

### Summary of adverse reactions and laboratory findings (reported in studies)
- Gastrointestinal: diarrhea (with or without blood), vomiting, anorexia, discolored feces, hypersalivation, weight loss
- Neurologic/behavioral: lethargy, seizures, ataxia, tremors, nystagmus
- Dermatologic: dermatitis, pruritus (itching), alopecia
- Ophthalmic/neurologic signs: anisocoria, nystagmus
- Laboratory/pathology findings: increased bile acids (in some dose groups), minimal mononuclear cell infiltration of liver (histopathology), extramedullary hematopoiesis (minimal, variable across groups)

### Serious adverse effects
- Seizures and cluster seizures have been reported; these are considered serious. Neurologic events (tremors, ataxia, nystagmus) have also been observed.
- Significant gastrointestinal intolerance (severe diarrhea, vomiting) occurred in some high-dose safety studies but generally resolved without treatment.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Pharmacokinetic interactions:
  - Lotilaner oral bioavailability is reduced in the fasted state; therefore administer with food (or within 30 minutes after feeding) to ensure adequate absorption.
  - Reported oral bioavailability (fed state, Beagle dogs): lotilaner ~93%; praziquantel ~41%; pyrantel ~31%. Bioavailability for moxidectin not reported due to insufficient IV elimination-phase data.
- Pharmacodynamic interactions:
  - No specific pharmacodynamic drug–drug interactions were identified in the available sources.
- Concurrent medications:
  - In field safety studies, concurrent administration with vaccines, antimicrobials, anthelmintics, antiemetics, steroidal and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, anesthetics, and analgesics was not associated with observed adverse reactions.
- Clinically significant interactions with management notes:
  - No specific contraindicated concomitant medications were identified in available sources.
  - Exercise clinical caution when co-administering multiple neuroactive agents or drugs with seizure threshold effects given reported neurologic adverse events.

  - Indicated and studied in dogs and puppies 8 weeks of age and older, weighing ≥ 3.3 lb (approximately 1.5 kg). Safety studies included 8‑week‑old Beagle puppies.
- Elderly use:
  - No specific dose adjustments provided for geriatric animals. Clinical observations in geriatric dogs are described in study reports (e.g., a geriatric dog experienced transient neurologic signs and recovered).
- Renal impairment:

- Hepatic impairment:
  - Dosing adjustments for hepatic impairment are not specified in available sources.
- Avermectin-sensitive breeds:
  - An avermectin-sensitive Collie safety study was performed; salivation and vomiting occurred in a dose-dependent fashion. Use caution when treating avermectin-sensitive dogs; monitor closely.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Lotilaner: an isoxazoline that inhibits insect and acarid gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-gated chloride channels, causing uncontrolled neuromuscular activity and death of insects/acarines; differential sensitivity between parasites and mammals underlies selectivity.
- Moxidectin: macrocyclic lactone that interferes with chloride channel–mediated neurotransmission in parasites, producing paralysis and death.
- Praziquantel: induces muscular paralysis in cestodes accompanied by rapid calcium influx and disruption of the parasite tegument; exact molecular mechanism not fully defined.
- Pyrantel: a depolarizing neuromuscular-blocking agent in susceptible nematodes, causing paralysis and death or expulsion.

### Pharmacokinetics (reported in dogs)
- Absorption:
  - Fed-state oral bioavailability (Beagle dogs): lotilaner ~93%; praziquantel ~41%; pyrantel ~31%. Moxidectin bioavailability not reported.
  - Time to peak concentration (Tmax): lotilaner ~10 h; moxidectin ~4.5 h; praziquantel ~0.75 h; pyrantel ~2.5 h.
  - Peak plasma concentrations (Cmax): lotilaner ~9070 ng/mL; moxidectin ~15.3 ng/mL; praziquantel ~390 ng/mL; pyrantel ~122 ng/mL.
- Distribution/metabolism:
  - Specific distribution volumes and metabolic pathways not detailed in available sources.
- Elimination:
  - Mean plasma elimination half-lives reported: lotilaner ~806 h; moxidectin ~634 h; praziquantel ~3.64 h; pyrantel ~4.87 h.
- Accumulation:
  - Repeated dosing every 28 days at labeled dose showed accumulation for lotilaner and moxidectin (geometric mean AUC accumulation ratios reported ~6.2 for lotilaner and ~7.9 for moxidectin over study period); praziquantel and pyrantel concentrations prior to each dose were below limits of quantification in that study.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Clinical parasite kill onset:
  - Lotilaner (component for ectoparasites) begins killing fleas within hours (reported onset as early as 4 hours), with >99% flea kill within 8 hours in laboratory studies; sustained activity observed for several weeks depending on parasite and study.
  - Reported tick kill/reduction occurs within 4–48 hours depending on species and study.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Heartworm prevention:
  - Two well-controlled laboratory studies: single oral dose was 100% effective in preventing development of adult Dirofilaria immitis when dogs were inoculated with infective larvae 30 days before treatment.
  - One well-controlled field study in the United States: dogs treated monthly for 11 months (156 treated dogs vs 149 active control in one report) — no dogs treated with the product tested positive for heartworm disease at study completion.
- Intestinal nematode and cestode efficacy:
  - Well-controlled laboratory studies demonstrated ≥ 97.0% efficacy against immature adult and adult Toxocara canis, adult Toxascaris leonina, fourth-stage larvae/immature adult/adult Ancylostoma caninum, and adult Uncinaria stenocephala.
  - In laboratory and field studies, praziquantel component demonstrated 100% efficacy against Echinococcus granulosus, Dipylidium caninum, and Taenia pisiformis when tested alone in separate studies.
  - A multi-center U.S. field study (10-day) showed a 99.9% reduction in Ancylostoma caninum fecal egg counts after a single dose in evaluable dogs.
- Flea efficacy:
  - Laboratory studies: 100% efficacy against adult fleas 24 hours after administration or infestation for 36 days when the combination product was tested.
  - Lotilaner alone demonstrated onset of flea kill as early as 4 hours and >99% within 8 hours for up to 35 days in separate laboratory studies; lotilaner alone prevented flea egg production and subsequent infestations for 30 days after treatment of existing infestations.
  - Field study (households with existing flea infestations) with lotilaner alone showed 99.5%–100% flea reductions over three monthly treatments.
- Tick efficacy:
  - Laboratory studies reported ≥ 97.1% efficacy for the combination against Rhipicephalus sanguineus and Haemaphysalis longicornis at 48 hours for up to 32 days.
  - Lotilaner alone showed >97% efficacy against Amblyomma americanum, Dermacentor variabilis, Ixodes scapularis, and Rhipicephalus sanguineus at 48 hours for up to 30 days in separate laboratory studies.
  - In European laboratory work, lotilaner alone began killing Ixodes ricinus within 4 hours of administration.
  - Two laboratory studies demonstrated prevention of Borrelia burgdorferi infection when dogs were infested with Ixodes scapularis vector ticks after treatment.
- New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax):
  - [US — conditional] A pilot/clinical study (do Vale et al.) evaluating lotilaner alone in naturally infested dogs (n=11) reported 100% overall effectiveness (expulsion plus larvicidal activity) at 24 hours, with larval expulsion observed in a majority of dogs at earlier timepoints. The combination product’s conditional approval for NWS relies on extrapolation from the same lotilaner dose and non-interference data.
- Safety studies:
  - Repeated-dose margin-of-safety studies in 8-week-old Beagle puppies administered 0X, 1X, 3X, and 5X the maximum labeled doses every 28 days for nine treatments reported dose-dependent gastrointestinal signs (discolored feces, diarrhea, vomiting) and some clinical pathology/histopathology findings (e.g., increased bile acids in some dogs, minimal liver mononuclear infiltration observed variably across control and treated groups).
  - An avermectin-sensitive Collie study (0X,1X,2X,5X for three treatments) reported dose-dependent salivation and vomiting; diarrhea observed in all groups.
  - A heartworm-positive safety study (0X,1X,3X for three treatments) showed diarrhea and/or vomiting in all dogs at the 3X dose; no hypersensitivity events (anaphylaxis/shock) were observed.
