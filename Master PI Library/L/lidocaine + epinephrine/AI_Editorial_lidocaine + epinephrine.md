# Editorial Product Information: lidocaine + epinephrine

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **lidocaine + epinephrine**
- Chemical class: Amide-type local anesthetic (lidocaine) combined with a sympathomimetic vasoconstrictor (epinephrine)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Parenteral injection (infiltration, peripheral nerve block, central neuraxial techniques, intravenous regional techniques) — specific route use and cautions described in Sections 3–6

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Production of local or regional anesthesia by infiltration techniques (e.g., percutaneous injection).
- Peripheral nerve block techniques (e.g., brachial plexus, intercostal).
- Central neuraxial techniques (e.g., lumbar and caudal epidural blocks) when accepted procedures and precautions for these techniques are observed.
- Use for intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block) is described with specific technique cautions; solutions containing vasoconstrictors should not be used for intravenous regional anesthesia (see Sections 4 and 6).

Note: Indications above reflect the clinical uses described in available sources. If an indication is restricted or differs by jurisdiction, that is not specified in available sources.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Specific numeric dosing regimens, maximum doses, and concentration-based dosing tables are Not specified in available sources.

### Dosing by indication
- Specific dosing by indication (e.g., infiltration, peripheral nerve block, epidural, caudal, intercostal, intravenous regional anesthesia) is Not specified in available sources.

### Administration instructions (clinically important)
- Use the lowest dosage that produces effective anesthesia to reduce risk of systemic toxicity.
- Always aspirate before injecting to reduce the risk of intravascular injection; reposition the needle and repeat aspiration until no blood is obtained. Note: absence of blood on aspiration does not guarantee avoidance of intravascular injection.
- During epidural administration:
  - Administer an initial test dose and monitor the patient for CNS and cardiovascular toxicity and for signs of unintended intrathecal administration before proceeding.
  - Consider using a test dose that contains epinephrine when clinically appropriate because circulatory changes may warn of intravascular injection.
- For indwelling catheters, perform syringe aspirations before and during each supplemental injection.
- For paracervical blocks: inject slowly, aspirate frequently, allow a 5-minute interval between sides.
- For intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block): use proper tourniquet technique as described in standard texts; solutions containing vasoconstrictors (e.g., epinephrine) should not be used for this technique.
- Do not use local anesthetic solutions that contain antimicrobial preservatives (e.g., methylparaben) for epidural or spinal anesthesia.
- Solutions containing sodium metabisulfite (sulfite antioxidant) may cause allergic-type reactions in susceptible persons; consider this when selecting product formulations.
- Resuscitative equipment (oxygen, airway and ventilatory support), appropriate resuscitation drugs and trained personnel should be immediately available whenever local anesthetics are administered.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to local anesthetics of the amide type (e.g., history of allergy to lidocaine or other amide anesthetics).
- There are no additional explicit contraindications listed in available sources; other cautions regarding preservatives, vasoconstrictor use, and procedural technique are included in Sections 6 and 7.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warning
- **[US]** Local anesthetic injections for infiltration and nerve block should be administered only by clinicians experienced in diagnosis and management of dose-related toxicity and other acute emergencies associated with nerve blocks, and only when oxygen, resuscitative drugs, cardiopulmonary equipment, and trained personnel are immediately available. Delay in proper management of systemic toxicity, under‑ventilation, and/or altered sensitivity can result in acidosis, cardiac arrest, and death.

### General warnings and precautions
- Systemic toxicity is dose-related and may result from excessive dosage, rapid absorption, or inadvertent intravascular injection. The lowest effective dose should be used.
- Methemoglobinemia has been reported with local anesthetic use. Patients at increased risk include those with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, congenital or idiopathic methemoglobinemia, cardiac or pulmonary compromise, infants under 6 months of age, and patients concurrently exposed to oxidizing agents. Monitor for cyanosis or abnormal blood coloration; treatment may require methylene blue, exchange transfusion or hyperbaric oxygen.
- Post‑marketing reports of chondrolysis have been associated with intra‑articular infusions of local anesthetics following arthroscopic and other surgical procedures. Most reported cases involve the shoulder and prolonged infusions (e.g., 48–72 hours). Avoid intra‑articular continuous infusions unless benefits are judged to outweigh risks.
- Sulfite sensitivity: solutions containing sodium metabisulfite may provoke allergic-type reactions, including anaphylaxis or severe asthmatic episodes, in susceptible individuals (more common in asthmatics).
- Do not use preservative-containing (methylparaben) solutions for epidural or spinal anesthesia due to lack of established safety for intrathecal exposure.
- Repeated dosing may lead to accumulation of lidocaine or metabolites; monitor cumulative dose especially with repeated supplemental dosing or continuous infusions.
- Reduce doses in debilitated, elderly, acutely ill patients, and children commensurate with age, weight, and physical condition.
- Use caution in patients with severe shock or heart block.
- Use extreme caution in patients with existing neurologic disease, spinal deformities, septicemia, or severe hypertension when considering lumbar or caudal epidural anesthesia.
- Use solutions containing vasoconstrictors cautiously in areas supplied by end arteries (e.g., fingers, toes, ears, nose, penis) or tissues with compromised blood supply; ischemic injury or necrosis may result.
- Use preparations containing vasoconstrictors with caution during or after potent general anesthetic agents because cardiac arrhythmias may occur.
- Because lidocaine is hepatic‑metabolized, exercise caution in hepatic impairment; elimination half‑life may be prolonged twofold or more in severe hepatic dysfunction.
- Use caution in patients with impaired cardiovascular function because they may poorly tolerate changes in conduction or contractility.
- The potential for amide local anesthetics to trigger malignant hyperthermia is unknown; ensure availability of standard malignant hyperthermia management protocols when general anesthesia may be required.
- Head and neck blocks (including dental, retrobulbar, stellate ganglion) carry risk of intra‑arterial injection with retrograde flow to cerebral circulation producing confusion, convulsions, respiratory depression or arrest, and cardiovascular stimulation or depression. Monitor respiration and circulation and have resuscitative equipment and trained personnel immediately available.
- Observe patients for early warning signs of CNS toxicity: restlessness, anxiety, tinnitus, dizziness, blurred vision, tremors, depression, or drowsiness.

### Monitoring requirements
- Continuous monitoring of cardiovascular and respiratory status and level of consciousness after each local anesthetic injection.
- For obstetric use: continuous fetal heart rate monitoring is highly advisable; monitor maternal blood pressure and pulse.
- Consider laboratory monitoring for methemoglobin if clinical signs suggest methemoglobinemia.

### Specific clinical precautions
- To reduce risk of intravascular injection, aspirate before injection and during incremental injection.
- Intramuscular injection of lidocaine may raise serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK); interpret CPK accordingly if used to diagnose myocardial infarction without isoenzyme separation.
- Use caution and clinical judgment when coadministering drugs that may interact with epinephrine or affect lidocaine metabolism.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Administration of epinephrine-containing local anesthetic solutions to patients receiving monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors or tricyclic antidepressants may produce severe, prolonged hypertension. Monitor carefully; concurrent use should generally be avoided.
- Phenothiazines and butyrophenones may reduce or reverse the pressor effect of epinephrine.
- Concurrent administration of vasopressor drugs (e.g., for treatment of hypotension related to obstetric blocks) and ergot-type oxytocic drugs may cause severe, persistent hypertension or cerebrovascular accidents.
- Potent inhalational general anesthetics administered during or after local anesthetic with vasoconstrictor may increase risk of cardiac arrhythmias; use vasoconstrictors cautiously in these settings.

### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Lidocaine is extensively metabolized by the liver. Conditions or coadministered drugs that affect hepatic blood flow or hepatic metabolic enzymes may alter lidocaine kinetics and systemic exposure (specific enzyme interactions are Not specified in available sources).
- Accumulation of active metabolites may occur in renal dysfunction even though parent drug kinetics are not markedly changed.

### Other clinically significant interactions and management
- Concomitant exposure to drugs associated with methemoglobinemia increases risk; specific drug examples are Not specified in available sources. Monitor for clinical signs and manage accordingly.
- CNS stimulants or depressants and acidosis affect CNS levels of lidocaine required to produce systemic effects; adjust dosing and monitor.

### Jurisdiction-specific interactions
- None specified that differ by region in available sources.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category: **[US] Pregnancy Category B.**
- Animal reproduction studies: Reproduction studies in rats at doses up to 6.6 times the human dose revealed no evidence of fetal harm attributable to lidocaine in these studies.
- Clinical considerations:
  - Lidocaine crosses the placenta rapidly. When used for epidural, paracervical, pudendal or caudal block anesthesia, maternal, fetal and neonatal toxicity can occur depending on dose, technique, and procedure.
  - Maternal hypotension from regional anesthesia can result in decreased uteroplacental perfusion; take measures to maintain maternal blood pressure (e.g., left lateral positioning, leg elevation).
  - Paracervical block anesthesia may be associated with fetal bradycardia in approximately 20–30% of cases and may be associated with fetal acidosis. Fetal heart rate should be monitored during paracervical anesthesia.
  - Cases compatible with unintended fetal intracranial injection have been reported after paracervical or pudendal blocks; neonatal depression and seizures within hours of birth have been described. Manage affected neonates with supportive measures and consider forced urinary excretion of the local anesthetic when indicated.
  - Use in pregnancy should weigh potential benefits against potential risks; care is required especially in early pregnancy when organogenesis occurs.

### Breastfeeding
- It is not known whether lidocaine is excreted in human milk. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk, exercise caution when administering to nursing women.

### Fertility
- Studies to evaluate carcinogenic and mutagenic potential or effects on fertility have not been conducted (carcinogenesis, mutagenesis, fertility: Not specified).

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Dosages in children should be reduced commensurate with age, body weight and physical condition. Specific pediatric dosing regimens are Not specified in available sources.

### Elderly use
- Elderly patients may have decreased clearance and increased sensitivity; dose reduction is recommended commensurate with age and physical condition. Specific dosage adjustments are Not specified in available sources.

### Renal impairment
- Renal dysfunction does not markedly affect parent-lidocaine kinetics but may increase accumulation of metabolites; specific dose adjustments by eGFR/stage are Not specified in available sources.

### Hepatic impairment
- Lidocaine is rapidly metabolized by the liver. Hepatic impairment may prolong elimination half-life (may be prolonged twofold or more in patients with liver dysfunction). Use with caution; specific dose adjustments by severity are Not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **Lidocaine**: Stabilizes neuronal membranes by inhibiting ionic fluxes (primarily sodium) required for initiation and conduction of nerve impulses, producing local anesthesia.
- **Epinephrine**: Vasoconstrictor that reduces local blood flow, thereby decreasing systemic absorption of lidocaine and prolonging the duration of local anesthesia; also produces local hemostasis.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Lidocaine is completely absorbed following parenteral administration; rate of absorption varies by site of administration and presence/absence of vasoconstrictor. Highest systemic levels reported after intercostal nerve block; lowest after subcutaneous injection.
- Distribution: Plasma protein binding is concentration-dependent; at free base concentrations of 1–4 μg/mL approximately 60–80% is protein bound. Binding is influenced by alpha-1-acid glycoprotein levels. Lidocaine crosses the blood‑brain barrier and the placenta by passive diffusion.
- Metabolism: Rapid hepatic metabolism via oxidative N‑dealkylation (major pathway yielding monoethylglycinexylidide and glycinexylidide), ring hydroxylation, cleavage of the amide linkage and conjugation. Approximately 90% excreted as metabolites; <10% excreted unchanged in urine. Primary urinary metabolite: conjugate of 4‑hydroxy‑2,6‑dimethylaniline.
- Elimination half‑life: Following an IV bolus, elimination half‑life is typically 1.5–2 hours in subjects with normal hepatic function. Half‑life may be prolonged twofold or more in patients with hepatic dysfunction.
- Special populations: Renal dysfunction may increase metabolite accumulation but does not significantly alter parent drug kinetics. Acidosis and concomitant CNS stimulants/depressants alter CNS levels required to produce overt effects.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Excessive plasma levels can alter cardiac output, total peripheral resistance and mean arterial pressure. With central neural blockade, changes may be due to autonomic block, direct depressant effects of lidocaine on cardiovascular components, and/or beta‑adrenergic stimulation from epinephrine. Net effect with recommended dosages is usually modest hypotension.

- Toxic thresholds: Objective adverse manifestations become increasingly apparent with venous plasma free-base lidocaine levels above approximately 6 μg/mL. Animal data (rhesus monkey) showed convulsive thresholds at arterial blood levels of about 18–21 μg/mL.
