# Editorial Product Information: black + taipan + death + tiger + brown

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **black + taipan + death + tiger + brown**
- Chemical class: Equine-derived polyvalent antivenom (equine immunoglobulins / antibodies raised to snake venoms)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous (IV) infusion only. Administration by the intramuscular route should not be used. [Australian]

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first approval/registration: 21 July 2000 [Australian]
- Date of last revision/update of source PI: 26 March 2026 [Australian]
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of patients with clinical manifestations of systemic snake envenoming when the biting snake is not definitely identified. [Australian]
- Use is intended for situations where polyvalent coverage against five Australian/Papua New Guinea venom immunotypes is required.
- Approved for use in patients in Papua New Guinea and in all Australian states except Victoria and Tasmania. [Australian]
- Regional practice notes:
  - In Tasmania, Tiger Snake antivenom (monovalent) is the preferred treatment over polyvalent antivenom. [Australian]
  - In Victoria, a combination of Tiger Snake antivenom plus Brown Snake antivenom (monovalents) is the preferred treatment. [Australian]
- The product should not be used if the snake has been identified and the appropriate monovalent antivenom is available, since monovalent antivenom provides targeted neutralisation while exposing the patient to less equine protein. [Australian]

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Initial dose: the contents of one vial (40,000 units as supplied in source PI) administered intravenously after dilution. The dose is the same for adults and children. [Australian]

### Dosing by clinical response
- The aim of therapy is to neutralise circulating and migrating venom. Additional vials may be required if the patient's condition deteriorates or if clinical/laboratory features of envenoming persist.
- Severe systemic envenoming, particularly with coagulopathy, may require multiple vials. Doses up to six vials have been used in severe cases; it should be recognised that such quantities of this product contain large amounts of equine protein. [Australian]
- Children may become critically ill more rapidly than adults and may require more antivenom.

### Administration instructions
- Dilute antivenom prior to infusion. The standard recommended dilution is 1 in 10; a 1-in-5 dilution may be more appropriate in patients at risk of fluid overload. Expert advice should be sought to balance adequate dilution with the risk of fluid overload. [Australian]
- Suitable diluents include Hartmann's solution or 0.9% w/v sodium chloride. Once diluted, the antivenom must be used immediately; diluted product should not be stored. [Australian]
- Administer slowly by intravenous infusion. Administration by the intramuscular route should not be used. [Australian]
- The patient should ideally receive antivenom in an intensive care unit or in a clinical setting where immediate resuscitation facilities are available. [Australian]
- Before starting the infusion, adrenaline should be prepared and available for immediate use, as severe hypersensitivity reactions may develop rapidly. [Australian]
- If anaphylaxis occurs, stop the infusion and institute treatment immediately; subsequent antivenom administration should be considered only after weighing the relative risks of ongoing envenoming versus hypersensitivity. [Australian]
- To minimise systemic spread of venom prior to antivenom availability, maintain first-aid measures (immobilisation and firm pressure bandage). If effective first aid has been applied, removal of the bandage and splint may precipitate systemic envenoming—do not remove until antivenom is available for infusion. [Australian]

### Titration schedules
- Titration should be guided by clinical status and laboratory monitoring of envenoming (neuromuscular function, coagulation parameters, markers of myolysis, renal function). Specific titration schemes are not provided in available sources.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not applicable in the usual sense; antivenom is administered as urgent therapy for envenoming. For incomplete or interrupted doses, clinical reassessment and further doses should follow expert guidance. Not otherwise specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- There are no absolute contraindications listed; however:
  - The product should not be given unless there is clear evidence of systemic envenoming with potential for serious toxicity. [Australian]
- Use with caution (see Warnings and Precautions) in patients with known hypersensitivity to horse proteins or horse dander; such patients are at increased risk of severe, potentially life-threatening allergic reactions.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / critical warnings
- **Severe hypersensitivity including anaphylactic shock can occur with this equine-derived antivenom; adrenaline must be immediately available and prepared for use prior to administration.** [Australian]
- **Delayed serum sickness is relatively common following administration of animal-derived antivenoms and increases in incidence with larger volumes of equine protein.** [Australian]

### General warnings and precautions
- Products derived from animal plasma carry a theoretical risk of transmission of infectious agents that cannot be completely excluded; historically, no documented viral transmissions have been recorded with this product. [Australian]
- Many snake bites result in injection of little or no venom; antivenom should be reserved for patients with clinical or laboratory evidence of systemic envenoming. Onset of systemic features is usually within 2 hours but can be delayed, especially if first-aid immobilisation and a firm pressure bandage have been applied. [Australian]
- Suspected snake-bite patients should be observed for at least 12 hours after the bite or after removal of a bandage prior to discharge, preferably in a monitored setting. Monitor for neuromuscular impairment, coagulopathy, myolysis, renal impairment, and other abnormalities. [Australian]
- Venom detection kits applied at the bite site or tested in urine may assist identification of the venom type and allow selection of an appropriate monovalent antivenom; blood-based venom tests are less reliable for this purpose. [Australian]
- Skin testing to predict allergic reactions is unreliable and should not be performed. [Australian]
- Historical use of premedication with subcutaneous adrenaline and intravenous antihistamine in patients considered at elevated risk has been reported, but this practice remains controversial. Clinical teams should follow local guidelines or seek expert advice. [Australian]

### Specific clinical precautions
- Individuals with known allergy to horse or horse dander are at increased risk of life-threatening hypersensitivity. A history of atopy or allergy to other animals (e.g., sheep, dog, cat) may increase the risk of cross-reactivity with equine proteins. Previous exposure to equine-derived products (e.g., equine-derived tetanus antitoxin, particularly if received before 1974 in Australia) should also be considered. A careful evaluation of allergy history and discussion of risks versus benefits is important prior to administration. [Australian]
- Clinicians should be prepared to manage both envenoming and anaphylaxis simultaneously if necessary; anaphylaxis should be treated according to appropriate resuscitation protocols, followed by reassessment of the need for further antivenom based on overall clinical priorities. [Australian]
- Delayed serum sickness may develop days or weeks after administration; onset as early as 12 hours can occur following a second injection of a similar animal protein. Patients should be counselled about the signs and symptoms—which commonly include fever, skin eruptions, joint pain, lymphadenopathy, and protein in the urine—and instructed to seek urgent medical attention if these develop. Less common manifestations include arthritis, kidney inflammation, neuropathy, and vasculitis. The incidence of serum sickness is higher with larger volumes of equine serum and may be expected in at least approximately 5% of patients receiving horse serum for the first time. [Australian]
- To minimise exposure to equine protein, monovalent antivenom should be used wherever possible when the offending snake has been identified. Expert advice should be sought when supply of monovalent antivenom is limited. [Australian]

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor patients closely for immediate hypersensitivity during and after infusion; observe for at least 6 hours post-administration. Monitor for delayed serum sickness and provide appropriate follow-up instructions. [Australian]
- Monitor clinical and laboratory indices of envenoming (neuromuscular function, coagulation profile, markers of myolysis, renal function) as clinically indicated. [Australian]

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- The antivenom contains equine antibodies raised against venoms of five snake species. These antibodies bind to and neutralise venom components, preventing or reversing the pathological effects of envenoming.
- Clinical effects of the targeted venoms include:
  - Neurotoxic components capable of inducing respiratory failure through paralysis of respiratory muscles.
  - Coagulant components (present in all targeted venoms except the death adder) that activate the conversion of prothrombin to thrombin, ultimately depleting fibrinogen and causing secondary haemorrhage.
  - Myolytic components that damage muscle tissue and may lead to secondary renal impairment.
- The standard dose is formulated to neutralise the in vitro average yield of venom from each listed species; actual clinical requirements may be substantially greater, especially when treatment is delayed or envenoming is severe. [Australian]
