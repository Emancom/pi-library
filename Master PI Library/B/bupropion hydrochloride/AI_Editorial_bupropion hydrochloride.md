# Editorial Product Information: bupropion hydrochloride

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **bupropion hydrochloride**
- Chemical class: Not specified in available sources
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of source product information: Not specified in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Short‑term adjunctive therapy for treatment of nicotine dependence in patients committed to quitting smoking, to be used in conjunction with counselling for smoking cessation/abstinence.  
  - Note: This indication and associated dosing recommendations are presented as in the available product information [Australian].

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Initial therapy: 150 mg once daily for three days, then increase to 150 mg twice daily.
- Maximum single dose: 150 mg.
- Maximum total daily dose: 300 mg.
- Interval between successive doses: at least 8 hours.

### Dosing by indication
- Smoking cessation (adjunctive to counselling): as above. Patients are recommended to start treatment while still smoking and set a target quit date within the first two weeks of treatment, preferably in the second week.

### Duration of therapy and treatment continuation
- Patients should be treated for at least 7 weeks.
- Consider discontinuation if the patient has not made significant progress toward abstinence by the seventh week of therapy.

### Administration instructions
- Tablets should be swallowed whole and must not be crushed or chewed (crushing/chewing may increase risk of adverse effects including seizures).
- Avoid taking a dose at bedtime where insomnia occurs (ensure at least 8 hours between doses).

### Titration schedules
- Begin at 150 mg once daily for 3 days, then increase to 150 mg twice daily as tolerated.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
(Include all contraindications listed in the available product information)
- Hypersensitivity to bupropion or any component of the preparation.
- Current seizure disorder or any history of seizures.
- Known central nervous system (CNS) tumour.
- Undergoing abrupt withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines.
- Concurrent treatment with any other preparation containing bupropion.
- Current or previous diagnosis of bulimia or anorexia nervosa.
- Concomitant use of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs). At least 14 days should elapse between discontinuation of an MAOI and initiation of bupropion.
- Children (use in patients under 18 years of age is contraindicated per available information).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
- Bold critical warning: **Risk of seizures — bupropion hydrochloride is associated with a dose‑related risk of seizures; the recommended dose must not be exceeded.**
  - Incidence of seizure at doses up to 300 mg/day of sustained release formulation is approximately 0.1% (1/1,000).
  - Discontinue and do not re‑challenge patients who experience a seizure during treatment.

- Predisposing risk factors lowering seizure threshold (bupropion must not be used where these are present unless clear benefit outweighs risk):
  - Concomitant use of medicines known to lower seizure threshold (see Section 8: Drug Interactions).
  - Excessive alcohol use or sedative dependence/withdrawal.
  - History of head trauma.
  - Diabetes treated with hypoglycaemics or insulin.
  - Use of stimulants or anorectic products.

- Hypersensitivity reactions:
  - Discontinue promptly if hypersensitivity occurs. Symptoms may persist beyond discontinuation and require appropriate management.

- Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs):
  - Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP), and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) have been reported. Withdraw drug immediately if suggestive signs appear; do not re‑start if SCAR occurred.

- Neuropsychiatric symptoms:
  - Psychotic and manic symptoms have been observed (particularly in those with psychiatric history); bupropion may precipitate mania in bipolar disorder.
  - Depressed mood (including suicidal ideation) has been reported during smoking cessation attempts and during treatment. Be alert to emergence of significant depressive symptoms or suicidal ideation; monitor and advise patients accordingly.
  - Note: a meta‑analysis in antidepressant trials (general context reported in the source) showed increased risk of suicidal thinking/behaviour in patients <25 years treated with antidepressants — clinicians should be aware and monitor appropriately.

- Cardiovascular:
  - Hypertension, sometimes severe, has been reported with bupropion alone and in combination with nicotine replacement. Consider discontinuation if clinically significant increases in blood pressure occur.
  - Combination with nicotine transdermal system (NTS) was associated with higher rates of treatment‑emergent hypertension in limited clinical data; if combination therapy is used, exercise caution and perform weekly blood pressure monitoring.
  - Limited clinical experience in patients with recent myocardial infarction or unstable heart disease; use with care in these groups.

- Inappropriate routes of administration:
  - Inhalation of crushed tablets or parenteral injection (non‑oral administration) has been reported and may cause rapid release and faster absorption, with seizures and/or deaths reported. Bupropion is for oral use only.

- Ophthalmic:
  - May cause mydriasis and therefore has potential to precipitate angle‑closure glaucoma in predisposed patients; use with caution in those with raised intraocular pressure or risk of angle‑closure glaucoma.

- Serotonin syndrome:
  - Has been reported with concomitant use of serotonergic drugs (e.g., SSRIs, SNRIs) or with overdose; monitor patients when initiating or increasing doses of concomitant serotonergic medicines.

- Cardiac sodium channel (Brugada) syndrome:
  - Bupropion may unmask Brugada syndrome; caution in patients with known Brugada syndrome or risk factors (e.g., family history of sudden cardiac death).

- Hepatic impairment:
  - Use with caution in patients with hepatic impairment. Mild–moderate hepatic cirrhosis: increased inter‑patient variability in pharmacokinetics — consider reduced dosing frequency. Severe hepatic cirrhosis: use extreme caution; peak bupropion levels substantially increased and accumulation likely — dose should not exceed 150 mg on alternate days in severe hepatic cirrhosis.

- Renal impairment:
  - Active metabolites are renally excreted; bupropion and metabolites may accumulate in renal impairment — consider reduced dosing frequency and/or dose and monitor for adverse effects (e.g., insomnia, dry mouth, seizures).

- Elderly:
  - Clinical experience has not identified differences in tolerability between elderly and other adults, but greater sensitivity in some elderly individuals cannot be ruled out; dose/frequency reduction may be required.

- Laboratory tests:
  - Bupropion can interfere with some rapid urine drug screens and may produce false positive amphetamine results; confirm with a more specific assay if needed.

- Alcohol:
  - Although no clear pharmacokinetic interaction identified, rare reports of reduced alcohol tolerance or adverse neuropsychiatric events with concurrent alcohol consumption; minimise or avoid alcohol during treatment.

- Monitoring recommendations:
  - Assess all patients for seizure risk factors prior to initiation.
  - Monitor for emergence or worsening of psychiatric symptoms (including suicidal ideation), blood pressure (particularly when combined with NTS or in patients with hypertension risk), skin reactions, signs of hypersensitivity, and signs of serotonin syndrome.
  - In hepatic or renal impairment, monitor for adverse effects indicating accumulation.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Metabolism:
  - Bupropion is metabolised primarily to hydroxybupropion by CYP2B6.
  - Active metabolites are further metabolised and excreted renally.
  - Bupropion and hydroxybupropion are inhibitors of CYP2D6 in vitro and in vivo; bupropion increased plasma levels of desipramine in a human study, with effect persisting for at least 7 days after last bupropion dose.

- Drugs that may affect bupropion exposure:
  - Metabolism inducers (may reduce bupropion exposure): carbamazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin, ritonavir, efavirenz.  
    - Ritonavir (100 mg twice daily or 600 mg twice daily) reduced bupropion and metabolite exposure by ~20–80% in healthy volunteers. Lopinavir/ritonavir combination reduced exposure comparably.
    - Efavirenz 600 mg once daily reduced bupropion exposure by ~55%.
    - Clinical implication: patients receiving these inducers may require increased bupropion doses, but the maximum recommended bupropion dose should not be exceeded.
  - Metabolism inhibitors (may increase bupropion exposure): valproate (reported as an inhibitor).
  - Drugs affecting CYP2B6 (may affect bupropion metabolism): orphenadrine, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, ticlopidine, clopidogrel — exercise caution.

- Effects on other drugs:
  - CYP2D6 substrates: co‑administration may lead to increased plasma concentrations of drugs predominantly metabolised by CYP2D6 (e.g., certain beta‑blockers such as metoprolol, antiarrhythmics such as flecainide, tricyclic antidepressants, some antipsychotics). Initiate such concomitant medications at the lower end of their dose range and consider dose reduction of the concomitant drug if bupropion is added.
  - Tamoxifen: drugs that require metabolic activation by CYP2D6 (e.g., tamoxifen) may have reduced efficacy when co‑administered with bupropion (CYP2D6 inhibition).
  - Citalopram: bupropion increased citalopram Cmax and AUC by approximately 30% and 40% respectively in one study; citalopram did not affect bupropion pharmacokinetics.
  - Digoxin: co‑administration with bupropion may decrease digoxin levels.
  - Ritonavir/lopinavir and efavirenz: reduce bupropion exposure (see above).

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Concomitant serotonergic agents (SSRIs, SNRIs, other serotonergic drugs): increased risk of serotonin syndrome; monitor closely, particularly at treatment initiation and dose increases.
- Drugs that lower seizure threshold: concomitant administration increases seizure risk. Examples include:
  - Antipsychotics
  - Antidepressants (including SSRIs and tricyclic antidepressants)
  - Antimalarials
  - Tramadol
  - Theophylline
  - Systemic steroids
  - Quinolones
  - Sedating antihistamines
- Levodopa and amantadine: limited clinical data suggest a higher incidence of neuropsychiatric adverse events with concurrent use; use with caution.
- Nicotine transdermal system (NTS): combination may increase smoking cessation rates but is associated with a higher rate of treatment‑emergent hypertension — monitor blood pressure weekly if combination therapy is used.
- St John’s Wort (hypericum perforatum): possible interaction may increase undesirable effects (evidence not clear).

### Management notes for clinically significant interactions
- Assess all concomitant medications for seizure‑threshold lowering potential and for substantial CYP2D6 or CYP2B6 metabolism.
- When bupropion is combined with drugs metabolised by CYP2D6, consider dose reduction of the concomitant drug, particularly if it has a narrow therapeutic index.
- When bupropion is co‑administered with metabolic inducers (e.g., ritonavir, efavirenz), monitor efficacy and consider dose adjustment of bupropion within recommended limits; do not exceed max recommended dose.
- When combining with NTS, perform weekly blood pressure monitoring and counsel patients about hypertension symptoms.
- If serotonin syndrome is suspected, discontinue serotonergic agents (including bupropion) and initiate supportive treatment.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category/system:
  - [Australian] Category B2.
- Clinical data and observations:
  - Some epidemiological studies reported associations between first‑trimester bupropion exposure and increased risk of certain congenital cardiovascular malformations; findings are not consistent across studies.
  - Reported data examples (from available information):
    - International Pregnancy Register: cardiac birth defects in 9/675 (1.3%) with first‑trimester exposure.
    - Retrospective managed‑care database (n=7005 infants): no greater proportion of congenital malformations or cardiovascular malformations with first‑trimester bupropion exposure compared to other antidepressants or bupropion outside first trimester.
    - Case–control analyses reported conflicting findings for specific cardiac defects (e.g., left outflow tract defects, ventricular septal defects) with varying odds ratios across studies.
  - Animal studies:
    - No evidence of adverse embryofetal development at certain doses in mice, rats, and rabbits; exposures in animals may vary compared with humans.
  - Clinical recommendation:
    - Encourage quitting smoking without pharmacotherapy where possible.
    - Use in pregnancy only if expected maternal benefit justifies potential fetal risk.

### Breastfeeding
- Bupropion and metabolites are excreted in human breast milk.
- Recommendation: mothers should be advised not to breastfeed while taking bupropion hydrochloride (per available product information).

### Fertility
- Human fertility data: Not specified in available sources.
- Animal fertility data: A rat reproductive study up to 300 mg/kg/day revealed no evidence of impaired fertility (as reported in the available information).

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Safety and efficacy in patients under 18 years have not been established; use in children is contraindicated according to the available product information.

### Elderly
- No specific dosing by age was provided; clinical experience has not identified differences in tolerability, but some elderly patients may be more sensitive — consider dose/frequency reduction and monitor.

### Renal impairment
- Active metabolites are renally excreted; reduced dosing frequency and/or dose should be considered because accumulation of parent drug/metabolites can occur. Monitor for adverse effects such as insomnia, dry mouth, seizures indicating high levels.

### Hepatic impairment
- Mild–moderate hepatic cirrhosis: pharmacokinetic variability increased; consider reduced dosing frequency.
- Severe hepatic cirrhosis: use extreme caution; peak levels substantially increased and accumulation likely. In severe hepatic cirrhosis do not exceed 150 mg on alternate days.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- The available product information states that the pharmacology of bupropion resembles that of some antidepressants. No detailed mechanism (e.g., precise neurotransmitter effects) is specified in the available sources.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Not specified in available sources.
- Distribution: Not specified in available sources.
- Metabolism:
  - Extensively metabolised in the liver; primary formation of active metabolite hydroxybupropion primarily via CYP2B6.
  - Bupropion and hydroxybupropion inhibit CYP2D6 in vitro and in vivo.
- Elimination:
  - Active metabolites are further metabolised and excreted by the kidneys.
  - In hepatic impairment, variability and accumulation may occur (see Section 10).
- Notable interactions affecting pharmacokinetics:
  - Ritonavir and efavirenz reduce bupropion exposure (reported reductions of ~20–80% for ritonavir and ~55% for efavirenz); lopinavir/ritonavir has similar effects.
  - Citalopram’s exposure was increased by bupropion (one study: +30% Cmax, +40% AUC).

### Pharmacodynamics
- Not specified in available sources beyond the statement that pharmacology resembles some antidepressants and the clinical adverse effect profile (neuropsychiatric effects, seizure risk, effects on blood pressure).

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Summary from available product information:
  - Bupropion hydrochloride was generally well tolerated in clinical trials for nicotine dependence.
  - A pivotal comparative trial (Study 405) is referenced for reporting adverse event frequencies during the treatment phase; the detailed tabulated results were presented in the original product information but are not fully reproduced in the available source excerpt.
  - Limited clinical data suggest combination of bupropion with nicotine transdermal system may increase smoking cessation rates compared with either alone, but combination therapy was associated with a higher rate of treatment‑emergent hypertension.
  - Specific trial designs, primary outcome measures, statistical details, and extensive efficacy data are not specified in the available sources.
