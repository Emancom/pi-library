# Editorial Product Information: Buspirone

## Editorial Note
- The following jurisdictions' Product Information (PI) documents contributed to this editorial: Australian (TGA).
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview of buspirone hydrochloride for use by healthcare professionals. 
- **Disclaimer:** This synthesis is an editorial summary and does not serve as a substitute for the official, jurisdiction-specific Product Information required for prescribing within a particular region.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Buspirone hydrochloride, an anxiolytic agent belonging to the azapirone chemical class.
- **Brand Names:** ANKSILON [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** N05BE01.
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - **Oral Tablet:** 5 mg and 10 mg strengths [Australian].
    - Tablets are typically oval, biconvex, white, and scored to facilitate divided dosing [Australian].
- **Route of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Orion Pharma (Aus) Pty Limited [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Short-term treatment of anxiety:** Indicated for the management of anxiety disorders or the short-term relief of symptoms of anxiety. Treatment is generally not required for the ordinary tension associated with daily life stress [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
- **General Adult Dosing:** 
    - Initial dose: 15 mg daily, administered as 5 mg three times daily [Australian].
    - Titration: Dosage may be increased by 5 mg per day every 2 to 3 days as required to achieve optimal therapeutic response [Australian].
    - Maintenance: Most patients respond to 20–30 mg daily in divided doses [Australian].
    - Maximum Dose: 60 mg per day [Australian].
- **Administration Instructions:** 
    - Buspirone should be taken at the same time each day [Australian].
    - The drug must be taken consistently either with food or without food, as food intake significantly increases bioavailability [Australian].
    - Grapefruit juice must be avoided during treatment [Australian].
- **Switching from Benzodiazepines:** Buspirone does not prevent benzodiazepine withdrawal. Benzodiazepines should be tapered gradually before initiating buspirone. A drug-free interval is preferred to distinguish withdrawal symptoms from unrelieved anxiety [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
Buspirone is contraindicated in patients with:
- Hypersensitivity to buspirone hydrochloride or any excipients (e.g., lactose) [Australian].
- Severe hepatic impairment (biopsy-proven cirrhosis) [Australian].
- Severe renal insufficiency (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m²) [Australian].
- Acute angle-closure glaucoma [Australian].
- Myasthenia gravis [Australian].
- Epilepsy [Australian].
- Acute intoxication with alcohol, hypnotics, analgesics, or antipsychotic drugs [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs):** Concomitant use may cause elevated blood pressure. Use with MAOIs is not recommended [Australian].
- **Convulsive Disorders:** Use is not recommended in patients with a history of seizures, as buspirone lacks anticonvulsant activity [Australian].
- **Central Dopaminergic Effects:** Buspirone binds to central dopamine receptors. Clinicians should monitor for acute or chronic neurological changes, including dystonia, pseudo-Parkinsonism, akathisia, and tardive dyskinesia [Australian].
- **Long-term Toxicity:** Because the mechanism of action is not fully elucidated, long-term CNS toxicity cannot be predicted. Treatment for several months requires periodic reassessment and intensive monitoring [Australian].
- **Psychomotor Performance:** While less sedating than other anxiolytics, individual susceptibility varies. Patients should be cautioned against driving or operating machinery until they are certain the drug does not adversely affect them [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
Approximately 10% of patients discontinue treatment due to adverse events, most commonly CNS-related disturbances [Australian].

| Frequency | Adverse Effects |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Very Common (>10%)** | Dizziness (12%) |
| **Common (1–10%)** | Nausea (8%), headache (6%), nervousness (5%), light-headedness (3%), excitement (2%), sweating/clamminess, drowsiness, insomnia, fatigue, tachycardia/palpitations, blurred vision, tinnitus, sore throat, nasal congestion, dry mouth, abdominal distress, diarrhoea, constipation, vomiting, musculoskeletal pains. |
| **Uncommon (0.1–1%)** | Akathisia, hallucinations, suicidal ideation, depersonalisation, increased appetite, anorexia, weight gain/loss, syncope, hypotension, hypertension, hyperventilation, shortness of breath, increased liver enzymes, flushing, pruritus, urinary frequency/retention, menstrual disorders. |
| **Rare (<0.1%)** | Serotonin syndrome, extrapyramidal symptoms (dyskinesia, dystonia), thyroid dysfunction, blood count disorders (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), allergic reactions (angioedema, urticaria), heart failure, myocardial infarction, psychosis. |

## 7. Drug Interactions
- **Pharmacokinetic Interactions (CYP3A4):**
    - **Potent Inhibitors:** Itraconazole, nefazodone, and erythromycin significantly increase buspirone plasma levels. A reduced buspirone starting dose and clinical titration are required [Australian].
    - **Moderate Inhibitors:** Diltiazem and verapamil increase buspirone concentrations; dose adjustments may be necessary [Australian].
    - **Inducers:** Rifampicin, phenytoin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine may decrease buspirone levels, potentially necessitating a dose increase to maintain anxiolytic effect [Australian].
- **Pharmacodynamic Interactions:**
    - **Serotonergic Drugs:** Use caution with SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans, tramadol, and lithium due to the risk of serotonin syndrome [Australian].
    - **CNS Depressants:** Caution is advised when used with other CNS-active drugs, though buspirone does not significantly augment alcohol-induced impairment [Australian].
    - **Haloperidol:** May lead to increased serum haloperidol concentrations [Australian].
    - **Warfarin:** Rare reports of increased prothrombin time [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** 
    - **Australian Category B1:** Animal studies have not shown evidence of fetal damage. There are no adequate, well-controlled studies in humans. Use only if the benefit clearly outweighs the potential risk [Australian].
- **Breastfeeding:** Buspirone and its metabolites are excreted in the milk of rats. Use in breastfeeding women should only occur if the maternal benefit outweighs the potential risk to the infant [Australian].
- **Fertility:** No impairment of fertility was observed in rat or rabbit studies at doses up to 36 mg/kg [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Safety and effectiveness in children and adolescents under 18 years of age have not been established; use is not recommended [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Pharmacokinetics do not differ significantly from younger adults. However, it is recommended that the daily dose in the elderly not exceed 30 mg/day [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Use with caution in mild-to-moderate impairment; the dose should be reduced. Contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m²) [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Use with caution and at reduced doses in mild-to-moderate impairment. Contraindicated in severe hepatic impairment (cirrhosis) [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** The precise mechanism is unknown. It is a selective anxiolytic that lacks anticonvulsant and muscle relaxant properties. It has high affinity for serotonin (5-HT1A) receptors and moderate affinity for brain D2-dopamine receptors [Australian].
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption:** Rapidly absorbed; undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism. Peak plasma levels occur 60–90 minutes post-ingestion [Australian].
    - **Distribution:** Approximately 95% protein-bound. It does not typically displace other highly protein-bound drugs like warfarin or phenytoin [Australian].
    - **Metabolism:** Primarily oxidative metabolism via CYP3A4. Produces an active metabolite, 1-pyrimidinylpiperazine (1-PP), which has roughly 25% of the parent drug's potency [Australian].
    - **Elimination:** 29–63% excreted in urine (primarily as metabolites) and 18–38% in faeces. The mean elimination half-life is approximately 2 to 11 hours [Australian].
