# Editorial Product Information: cholestyramine

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **cholestyramine**
- Chemical class: Basic anion‑exchange resin (chloride form); bile acid sequestrant
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of source PI(s): Last revision noted as 11/2024 in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Adjunctive therapy to diet for the reduction of elevated serum cholesterol in patients with primary hypercholesterolemia (elevated LDL‑C) who do not respond adequately to diet.
- Relief of pruritus associated with partial biliary obstruction (reduction in serum bile acids and decreased dermal deposition); variable effects on serum cholesterol in these patients.
- Notes:
  - Use of lipid‑altering therapy should be part of a comprehensive risk‑factor intervention program and preceded by dietary measures.
  - For patients with hypertriglyceridemia where triglycerides are the primary abnormality, use may not be appropriate.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Recommended starting dose: 4 g anhydrous cholestyramine resin once or twice daily (equivalent to one measured dose as described in available sources).
- Recommended maintenance dose: 8–16 g anhydrous cholestyramine resin daily, divided into two doses.
- Maximum recommended daily dose: 24 g anhydrous cholestyramine resin per day.
- Dosing frequency: Usual schedule is twice daily; may be administered in 1 to 6 doses per day as clinically required.

### Dosing by indication
- Hypercholesterolemia: begin with the starting dose above; titrate to achieve LDL‑C goals per clinical guidelines and lipid monitoring.
- Pruritus of partial biliary obstruction: dosing as above; clinical response may be variable.

### Paediatric dosing
- Usual pediatric dose reported in standard texts: 240 mg/kg/day of anhydrous cholestyramine resin in two to three divided doses, normally not to exceed 8 g/day; titrate based on response and tolerance.
- Effects of long‑term administration and maintenance of lowered cholesterol in pediatric patients: Not specified in available sources.

### Administration instructions
- Always mix cholestyramine with an adequate volume of non‑carbonated liquid (typical guidance: 2–6 ounces / ~60–180 mL per dose) and drink immediately; do not take dry.
- May be mixed with highly fluid soups or pulpy fruits (e.g., applesauce, crushed pineapple) with high moisture content.
- Suggested administration time is with meals but may be adjusted to minimize interactions with concomitant oral medications.

### Titration and missed dose
- Increase dose gradually with periodic lipid monitoring (intervals of not less than 4 weeks) to reduce risk of constipation and fecal impaction.
- If a dose is missed, administer as soon as remembered unless it is nearly time for the next scheduled dose; do not double doses to make up for missed doses. (Specific missed‑dose guidance not detailed in available sources — clinicians should use standard clinical judgment.)

## 5. Contraindications
- Complete biliary obstruction (where bile is not secreted into the intestine).
- Known hypersensitivity to cholestyramine or any component of the formulation.
- Formulation‑specific: certain low‑calorie/aspartame‑containing formulations contain phenylalanine (see Warnings) and are contraindicated in patients with phenylketonuria (refer to formulation labeling). [If formulation differences exist, see the product labeling for details.]

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- Not specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- **Vitamin K deficiency and bleeding**: Chronic use may cause hypoprothrombinemia from reduced vitamin K absorption; bleeding tendency may occur. Treat promptly with parenteral vitamin K when indicated and consider oral vitamin K to prevent recurrence.
- **Fat‑soluble vitamin malabsorption**: Because cholestyramine binds bile acids and can impair fat absorption, absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) may be reduced during prolonged use. Consider supplementation with water‑miscible (or parenteral) forms of fat‑soluble vitamins during long‑term therapy.
- **Folate deficiency**: Reduction of serum or red‑cell folate has been reported with long‑term administration; consider folic acid supplementation when indicated.
- **Electrolyte/acidosis risk**: Prolonged use (chloride form) may produce hyperchloremic acidosis, especially in younger/smaller patients receiving relatively higher dosages. Exercise caution in patients at risk.
- **Constipation and fecal impaction**: Cholestyramine commonly causes or worsens constipation; increase dose gradually. In patients with pre‑existing constipation start with a low dose (example: one measured dose once daily for 5–7 days) and then increase with monitoring. Encourage increased fluid and fiber intake; stool softeners may be required. Avoid constipation in patients with symptomatic coronary artery disease.
- **Renal impairment / volume depletion**: Use caution in renal insufficiency or volume‑depleted patients.
- **Concomitant medications affecting potassium/sodium balance**: Exercise caution when used with spironolactone.
- **Dental effects**: Prolonged sipping or holding the suspension in the mouth may cause tooth surface changes (discoloration, enamel erosion, decay); advise patients on oral hygiene.
- **Phenylketonurics**: Certain low‑calorie/aspartame‑containing formulations provide phenylalanine (e.g., 14.0 mg phenylalanine per 5 g dose reported for a low‑calorie formulation); warn patients with phenylketonuria.

### Monitoring requirements
- Lipid profile (Total cholesterol, HDL‑C, triglycerides) prior to therapy and periodically thereafter; monitor LDL‑C to initiate and assess treatment response where possible.
- Serum triglycerides: monitor to detect significant increases; cholestyramine therapy has been associated with increases in serum triglycerides.
- Coagulation parameters (prothrombin time/INR) if bleeding risk or suspicion of vitamin K deficiency.
- Periodic assessment of fat‑soluble vitamin status and folate with long‑term therapy.
- Monitor for signs/symptoms of gastrointestinal obstruction or impaction.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Discontinuation hazard: Stopping cholestyramine may increase absorption of concomitant drugs that were titrated while the patient was receiving cholestyramine (e.g., digitalis); evaluate carefully when cholestyramine is discontinued.
- Pediatric caution: Reports of intestinal obstruction, including fatalities, have been reported in pediatric patients; monitor closely for GI adverse events.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Cholestyramine may bind or adsorb concomitant oral medications in the gastrointestinal tract and thereby delay or reduce their absorption. Drugs reported to interact include:
  - Phenylbutazone
  - Warfarin
  - Thiazide diuretics (acidic)
  - Propranolol (basic)
  - Tetracycline
  - Penicillin G
  - Phenobarbital
  - Thyroid and thyroxine preparations
  - Estrogens and progestins (including oral contraceptives)
  - Digitalis glycosides
  - Oral phosphate supplements (interference observed with other positively‑charged bile acid sequestrants)
- Cholestyramine may interfere with drugs that undergo enterohepatic circulation.
- Discontinuation of cholestyramine can change the bioavailability of drugs that were titrated while the patient was receiving cholestyramine and may result in toxicity (notably digitalis has been highlighted).

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Binding of bile acids and interruption of fat digestion may reduce absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and folate, potentially altering the pharmacodynamic milieu for drugs relying on those nutrients.

### Management notes
- It is recommended that other oral medications be taken at least 1 hour before or 4–6 hours after cholestyramine (or at as great an interval as possible) to minimize binding and reduced absorption.
- For long‑term cholestyramine therapy, consider monitoring and supplementation of fat‑soluble vitamins in water‑miscible or parenteral forms when needed.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Pregnancy risk classification (as stated in available sources):
  - US pregnancy category: C.
- There are no adequate and well‑controlled studies in pregnant women. **cholestyramine** is not systemically absorbed, but may interfere with absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins; therefore prenatal supplementation may not be adequate. Use only if potential benefit outweighs potential risk to the mother and fetus.

### Breastfeeding
- Caution is advised when cholestyramine is administered to a nursing mother because reduced maternal absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins could affect the nursing infant. Specific data on excretion into breast milk are not provided in available sources.

### Fertility
- Data addressing effects on human fertility are not specified in available sources. (Animal studies addressing carcinogenesis and related intestinal effects are reported; clinical relevance uncertain.)

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Usual pediatric dose cited in authoritative texts: 240 mg/kg/day of anhydrous cholestyramine resin divided in 2–3 doses, normally not to exceed 8 g/day. Dose titration should be based on response and tolerance.
- Pediatric safety concerns: rare reports of intestinal obstruction including deaths in pediatric patients; hyperchloremic acidosis has been reported in children.
- Long‑term effects and maintenance of cholesterol lowering in pediatric patients: Not specified in available sources.

### Elderly use
- Elderly patients (>60 years) appear more predisposed to constipation and related complications; titrate dose cautiously and monitor bowel function. Specific dosing adjustment recommendations are not provided in available sources.

### Renal impairment
- Caution is advised in patients with renal insufficiency; no specific dosing adjustments or eGFR‑based guidance is provided in available sources.

### Hepatic impairment
- Contraindicated in complete biliary obstruction. Use in partial biliary obstruction is indicated for relief of pruritus; cholestyramine's effect on serum cholesterol in patients with biliary disease can be variable.
- No specific dose adjustments by degree of hepatic impairment provided in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **cholestyramine** is a non‑absorbable, basic anion‑exchange resin that binds bile acids in the intestine to form an insoluble complex excreted in the feces. By interrupting enterohepatic circulation of bile acids and increasing fecal bile acid loss, hepatic conversion of cholesterol to bile acids increases, lowering plasma LDL‑cholesterol and total cholesterol.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: **cholestyramine** resin is not systemically absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract.
- Distribution, metabolism, elimination: Because **cholestyramine** is confined to the GI tract and forms insoluble complexes with bile acids that are excreted in feces, systemic distribution, metabolic transformation and renal elimination of the resin are not applicable in the usual sense. Specific pharmacokinetic parameters (Cmax, t1/2, bioavailability) are not specified in available sources.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Reduction in LDL‑C and total cholesterol is mediated via enhanced hepatic conversion of cholesterol to bile acids and increased hepatic cholesterol synthesis; however plasma cholesterol levels overall fall. Effects on triglycerides vary, with clinical trial evidence indicating modest increases in triglyceride levels compared with placebo in some patients.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Key trial summaries described in available sources:
  - Lipid Research Clinics Coronary Primary Prevention Trial (LRC‑CPPT): Large, placebo‑controlled, multiclinic trial in men aged 35–59 with baseline serum cholesterol >265 mg/dL and no prior heart disease. Cholestyramine treatment produced mean reductions in total and LDL cholesterol that exceeded diet and placebo by approximately 7.2% and 10.4%, respectively. Over a 7‑year in‑trial period, the cholestyramine group experienced a 19% relative reduction in combined coronary heart disease death plus non‑fatal myocardial infarction compared with placebo (cumulative incidences: 7.0% cholestyramine vs 8.6% placebo). Extrapolation to women and other patient groups is uncertain.
  - NHLBI Type II Coronary Intervention Trial: Randomized 116 patients with angiographically documented coronary artery disease to cholestyramine or placebo for 5 years. Final arteriography showed progression of coronary artery disease in 49% of placebo patients vs 32% of cholestyramine‑treated patients (p<0.05), indicating slowed progression with cholestyramine.
  - St Thomas Atherosclerosis Regression Study (STARS): Randomized hypercholesterolemic men with coronary artery disease to usual care, lipid‑lowering diet, or diet plus cholestyramine for 36 months. Progression of disease was observed in 46% (usual care), 15% (diet alone), and 12% (diet plus cholestyramine) (p<0.02). Coronary segment width decreased in usual care, increased slightly in diet group, and increased more in diet plus cholestyramine group (mean absolute width change +0.103 mm in diet plus cholestyramine; p<0.05). These trials demonstrated that cholestyramine therapy can retard progression and promote regression of coronary atherosclerotic lesions in selected patients with coronary artery disease.
  - Additional trials comparing conventional therapy versus intensive lipid‑lowering combination therapy (including anion‑exchange resins plus other lipid‑lowering agents) have demonstrated greater reductions in progression and increases in regression of coronary atherosclerosis with intensive therapy; these findings support combined therapy approaches in selected patients.
- Triglycerides: In the LRC‑CPPT, a dose‑related increase in serum triglycerides was observed in cholestyramine‑treated subjects (reported group increases ranged from approximately 10.7% to 17.1% versus 7.9% to 11.7% in placebo groups across study years). Adjusted mean increases above pre‑entry levels were approximately 5% in year 1 and 4.3% in year 7 for the cholestyramine group.

## Overdosage
- Reported instances: One reported overdosage (150% of recommended maximum dose for several weeks) produced no ill effects in the reported case.
- Chief potential harm from overdosage is gastrointestinal obstruction; management depends on location and degree of obstruction and gut motility. Supportive care and medical/surgical intervention as clinically warranted.
