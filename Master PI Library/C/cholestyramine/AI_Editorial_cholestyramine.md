# Editorial Product Information: cholestyramine

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **cholestyramine**
- Chemical class: basic anion-exchange resin (chloride salt); bile acid sequestrant
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI (if stated):
  - US source PI last revised: November 2024
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Adjunctive therapy to diet for reduction of elevated serum cholesterol in primary hypercholesterolemia (elevated LDL-C) for patients who do not respond adequately to diet.
- May be useful to lower LDL-C in patients who also have hypertriglyceridemia, but not indicated when hypertriglyceridemia is the primary abnormality of concern.
- Indicated for relief of pruritus associated with partial biliary obstruction. (Note: effect on serum cholesterol in these patients may be variable.)
- Use of lipid-altering therapy should be part of multi-risk-factor intervention and initiated following appropriate dietary measures and exclusion of secondary causes of hypercholesterolemia.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General adult dosing
- Recommended starting adult dose: one measured dose once or twice daily.
- Typical maintenance adult dose: 8 to 16 g anhydrous cholestyramine resin daily divided into two doses (equivalent to 2 to 4 measured doses/day as provided in the source product description).
- Maximum recommended daily dose (as stated in the available source): 24 g anhydrous cholestyramine resin per day (equivalent to six measured doses/day).
- Dosing may be administered as 1 to 6 doses per day depending on tolerance and response.

### Dosing by indication
- Hypercholesterolemia: titrate dose to achieve LDL-C lowering; beginning and maintenance doses as above. Monitor lipid response and adjust dose or consider combination therapy if goal not reached.
- Pruritus associated with partial biliary obstruction: use as indicated; dose regimens as above. Note: contraindicated in complete biliary obstruction (see Section 5).

### Administration instructions
- Always mix cholestyramine powder with water or other non-carbonated liquid immediately before ingestion; do not take in dry form.
- Recommended mixing volume (per measured dose in the available source): approximately 2 to 6 ounces (60–180 mL) of water or other non-carbonated beverage; may also be mixed with highly fluid soups or moist pulpy fruit (e.g., applesauce, crushed pineapple) to form a uniform suspension.
- Suggested time of administration: at mealtime but may be modified to minimize interactions with concomitant oral medications.

### Titration and monitoring schedule
- Increase dose gradually; assess lipoprotein/lipid levels at intervals of not less than 4 weeks when adjusting dose.
- In patients with pre-existing constipation, initiate at a low dose (e.g., one measured dose once daily for 5–7 days) and increase gradually with monitoring for constipation and serum lipoproteins.

### Missed dose
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to cholestyramine or any component of the formulation.
- Complete biliary obstruction (bile is not secreted into the intestine).
- Phenylketonurics: some light formulations contain phenylalanine (e.g., 14 mg phenylalanine per 5 g dose in the light formulation described in the available source); use caution and select an appropriate formulation for patients with phenylketonuria.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- Not specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- **Vitamin K–dependent bleeding**: Chronic use may be associated with hypoprothrombinemia (vitamin K deficiency) leading to increased bleeding tendency. This generally responds to parenteral vitamin K and recurrent events can be prevented with oral vitamin K supplementation where appropriate.
- **Fat‑soluble vitamin malabsorption**: Because cholestyramine binds bile acids and interferes with fat digestion/absorption, long-term use may reduce absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Consider supplementation with water‑miscible or parenteral forms of fat‑soluble vitamins during prolonged therapy.
- **Folate**: Reduction in serum or red cell folate has been reported during long‑term use; consider folic acid supplementation if deficiency is suspected.
- **Hyperchloremic acidosis**: Prolonged use of the chloride form of anion‑exchange resin may cause hyperchloremic acidosis, particularly in young or small patients receiving relatively high doses.
- **Constipation and fecal impaction**: Cholestyramine may produce or worsen constipation, and fecal impaction may occur especially when dose is increased rapidly. Increase dose gradually, encourage adequate fluid and dietary fiber intake, and consider stool softeners when indicated. Use caution and monitor closely in patients with pre-existing constipation or symptomatic coronary artery disease (constipation may aggravate hemorrhoids and increase cardiac risk).
- **Gastrointestinal obstruction**: Rare cases of intestinal obstruction, including fatal outcomes in pediatric patients, have been reported.
- **Renal and volume status**: Exercise caution in patients with renal insufficiency or volume depletion, and in patients receiving concomitant spironolactone.
- **Dental effects**: Sipping or holding the suspension in the mouth for prolonged periods may damage tooth enamel (discoloration, erosion, caries); advise good oral hygiene.
- **Pregnancy and lactation**: See Section 9.

### Monitoring requirements
- Serum lipid profile (Total cholesterol, HDL‑C, triglycerides, and calculation or direct measurement of LDL‑C) before treatment initiation and periodically during therapy; a favorable trend should be evident within the first month.
- Monitor serum triglycerides periodically (therapy can increase triglycerides).
- Monitor coagulation parameters (prothrombin time/INR) if bleeding or prolonged use is suspected to affect vitamin K status.
- Monitor folate levels if long‑term therapy is used and clinical signs suggest deficiency.
- Monitor for signs of fat‑soluble vitamin deficiency (A, D, E, K) during prolonged therapy.
- Monitor for constipation, abdominal pain, signs of bowel obstruction.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Prior to initiating therapy for hypercholesterolemia, exclude secondary causes of elevated cholesterol (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, dysproteinemias, obstructive liver disease, alcohol use, concurrent medications).
- In hypertriglyceridemic patients (TG > 400 mg/dL), LDL‑C estimation by Friedewald equation is inaccurate; consider direct LDL measurement or ultracentrifugation. Cholestyramine may not be appropriate when hypertriglyceridemia is the predominant abnormality.
- When combining with other lipid‑lowering agents, follow recommended monitoring and dose‑adjustment procedures.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions (binding interactions)
- Cholestyramine may delay or reduce absorption of concomitantly administered oral medications by binding them in the gastrointestinal tract. Drugs specifically listed in the available source as potentially affected include:
  - Phenylbutazone
  - Warfarin
  - Thiazide diuretics (acidic)
  - Propranolol (basic)
  - Tetracycline
  - Penicillin G
  - Phenobarbital
  - Thyroid preparations and thyroxine
  - Estrogens and progestins (oral contraceptives and hormone therapy)
  - Digitalis glycosides
- Cholestyramine may interfere with absorption of oral phosphate supplements (observed with another positively charged bile acid sequestrant); monitor phosphate therapy when co‑administered with a bile acid sequestrant.
- Cholestyramine may interact with drugs that undergo enterohepatic circulation.
- Clinical note: discontinuation of cholestyramine after titration of a drug whose dosing relied on reduced absorption (e.g., digitalis) may unmask higher systemic exposure and pose hazard; take care when stopping cholestyramine.

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Cholestyramine may reduce absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and therefore can affect anticoagulant status (via vitamin K) and other vitamin‑dependent physiologies.

### Management and administration guidance for interactions
- To minimize interaction with concomitant oral medications, it is recommended that other oral drugs be taken at least 1 hour before or 4 to 6 hours after cholestyramine administration (or at the greatest possible interval).
- For long‑term cholestyramine therapy, consider monitoring and supplementing fat‑soluble vitamins with water‑miscible formulations or parenteral preparations as appropriate.
- Monitor coagulation parameters when patients are taking anticoagulants (e.g., warfarin) and cholestyramine concurrently; adjust anticoagulant dosing guided by INR/prothrombin time as required.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- US pregnancy category (as stated in the available source): Category C.
- There are no adequate and well‑controlled studies in pregnant women. The resin is not systemically absorbed; however, cholestyramine can interfere with absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins — prenatal supplementation may be inadequate when cholestyramine is used. Use in pregnancy should weigh potential maternal benefits against potential hazards to the fetus.

### Breastfeeding
- Caution is advised when administering cholestyramine to nursing mothers because of potential effects on maternal nutritional status and fat‑soluble vitamin availability that could affect the nursing infant.

### Fertility
- Impairment of fertility: Not specified in available sources beyond statements in carcinogenesis/impairment sections that long‑term effects remain uncertain. (See Section 11—Carcinogenesis/Mutagenesis/Impairment of fertility for details included in the source.)

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Pediatric dosing (from standard pediatric references cited in the source): a usual pediatric dose is 240 mg/kg/day of anhydrous cholestyramine resin in two to three divided doses, normally not to exceed 8 g/day, with dose titration based on response and tolerance.
- In calculating pediatric dosages using specific powder formulations, product‑specific conversions were provided in the source document; formulation specifics vary by product and are not reproduced here beyond the pediatric dosing guidance above.
- Effects of long‑term administration and efficacy for maintaining lowered cholesterol levels in pediatric patients are unknown.
- Rare reports of intestinal obstruction and two deaths have been reported in pediatric patients; exercise caution.

### Elderly
- Increased age (over 60 years) predisposes to constipation and related complaints; monitor for gastrointestinal adverse effects and titrate dose cautiously.

### Renal impairment
- Caution is advised in patients with renal insufficiency or volume depletion due to risk of metabolic disturbances (e.g., hyperchloremic acidosis) and constipation-related complications. No specific dose adjustments by eGFR provided in the available source.

### Hepatic impairment
- Not specified in available sources with specific dose adjustments. Use caution in patients with obstructive liver disease; cholestyramine is ineffective in complete biliary obstruction and contraindicated in that setting.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **Cholestyramine** is a basic anion‑exchange resin that binds bile acids in the intestine to form an insoluble complex that is excreted in the feces. By interrupting enterohepatic circulation of bile acids and increasing fecal loss of bile acids, it increases hepatic conversion of cholesterol to bile acids, lowers LDL‑cholesterol and total cholesterol concentrations, and can reduce deposition of bile acids in dermal tissues (relieving pruritus in partial biliary obstruction).

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Cholestyramine resin is not systemically absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract.
- Distribution: Confined to the GI lumen; does not enter systemic circulation.
- Metabolism: Not systemically metabolized (acts locally in the gut).
- Elimination: Eliminated in the feces as insoluble cholestyramine–bile acid complexes.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Lowers LDL‑cholesterol (observed reductions vs diet/placebo in clinical trials).
- May increase hepatic cholesterol synthesis while still lowering plasma cholesterol.
- Has been associated with increases in serum triglycerides (dose‑related in clinical studies; see Section 12).

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Key clinical trials described in the available source include:

1. Lipid Research Clinics Coronary Primary Prevention Trial (LRC‑CPPT)
   - Design/population: Large, placebo‑controlled, multiclinic study of hypercholesterolemic men aged 35–59 with baseline serum cholesterol >265 mg/dL and no prior heart disease.
   - Outcomes:
     - Mean reductions in total and LDL‑cholesterol with cholestyramine exceeded those for diet and placebo by approximately 7.2% and 10.4%, respectively.
     - Over a seven‑year study period, the cholestyramine group experienced a 19% reduction (relative to placebo) in the combined incidence of coronary heart disease death plus non‑fatal myocardial infarction (cumulative incidences: 7.0% cholestyramine vs 8.6% placebo).
     - The study population was predominantly male; extrapolation to females and other hypercholesterolemic populations is uncertain.
     - Triglycerides: LRC‑CPPT showed dose‑related increases in serum triglycerides ranging from 10.7% to 17.1% in treated groups vs 7.9% to 11.7% in placebo; adjusted increases over baseline were approximately 5% in year 1 and 4.3% at year 7.

2. NHLBI Type II Coronary Intervention Trial
   - Design/population: Randomized controlled trial of 116 patients (80% male) with angiographically documented coronary artery disease treated with cholestyramine or placebo for 5 years.
   - Outcomes:
     - Progression of coronary artery disease on follow‑up arteriography: 49% of placebo patients vs 32% of cholestyramine patients (p < 0.05), indicating a reduction in progression with cholestyramine.

3. St. Thomas Atherosclerosis Regression Study (STARS)
   - Design/population: Randomized, blinded study of 90 hypercholesterolemic men with coronary artery disease allocated to usual care, lipid‑lowering diet, or diet plus cholestyramine and followed for 36 months with coronary arteriography.
   - Outcomes:
     - Progression of disease: 46% in usual care, 15% in diet, and 12% in diet plus cholestyramine (p < 0.02).
     - Mean absolute coronary segment width: decreased in usual care, increased slightly in diet, and increased more in diet plus cholestyramine (diet + cholestyramine mean increase 0.103 mm, p < 0.05 vs usual care).

- Additional clinical information:
  - Intensive lipid‑lowering combination therapy (diet plus a bile acid sequestrant similar to cholestyramine plus nicotinic acid or an HMG‑CoA reductase inhibitor) reduced progression and increased regression of coronary atherosclerotic lesions versus conventional measures in other randomized controlled trials.

Carcinogenesis/Mutagenesis/Impairment of Fertility (clinical/trial observations)
- In animal studies where cholestyramine resin was used experimentally, increased incidence of tumors of the alimentary system was observed in rats exposed to potent carcinogens; the relevance to clinical use is unknown.
- In the LRC‑CPPT, overall incidence of fatal and non‑fatal neoplasms was similar between treated and placebo groups; some alimentary system cancers were somewhat more prevalent in the cholestyramine group, but numbers were small and inconclusive.
- A post‑trial follow‑up (six years post‑trial; total ~13.4 years including in‑trial follow‑up) revealed no significant difference in cause‑specific mortality or cancer morbidity between cholestyramine and placebo groups.
