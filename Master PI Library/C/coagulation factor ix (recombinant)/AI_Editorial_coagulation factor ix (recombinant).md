# Editorial Product Information: coagulation factor ix (recombinant)

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **coagulation factor ix (recombinant)**
- Chemical class: Recombinant human coagulation factor IX protein (rFIX)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous (IV) bolus infusion only

## 2. Regulatory History
- Initial U.S. approval: 2013.
- Date of last revision/update reported in available sources: May 2026.
- Withdrawals, suspensions, restrictions: Not specified in available sources.
- Other jurisdictions' first registration/approval dates and last revision dates: Not specified in available sources.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment and control of bleeding episodes in adults and children with hemophilia B (previously treated patients).
- Perioperative management of bleeding in patients with hemophilia B.
- Routine prophylaxis to reduce frequency of bleeding episodes in patients with hemophilia B.
- Not indicated for induction of immune tolerance in patients with hemophilia B.

(If any jurisdiction-specific differences in approved indications were present in source documents, they would be noted here. None were specified beyond the above.)

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General principles
- Dosing must be individualized according to severity of factor IX deficiency, location and extent of bleeding, patient clinical status, age, and pharmacokinetic parameters (incremental recovery and half-life).
- Monitor factor IX activity (one‑stage clotting assay recommended) to confirm attainment and maintenance of target plasma levels; adjust dose and frequency accordingly.
- Evaluate for development of factor IX inhibitors if expected factor IX activity levels are not achieved or if bleeding is not controlled with appropriate dosing.

### Incremental recovery (empirical guidance)
- For patients <12 years of age: one international unit (IU) of product per kg body weight is expected to increase circulating factor IX activity by ~0.7 IU/dL (0.7% of normal).
- For patients ≥12 years of age: one IU/kg is expected to increase circulating factor IX activity by ~0.9 IU/dL (0.9% of normal).
- Example dose-calculation formula:
  - Initial Dose (IU) = body weight (kg) × desired factor IX increase (IU/dL or % of normal) × reciprocal of observed recovery (IU/kg per IU/dL).
- In pediatric patients an average 22% lower recovery was observed; calculation examples in clinical materials reflect use of 1.4 dL/kg reciprocal for <12 years (based on 0.7 IU/dL per IU/kg) and 1.1 dL/kg reciprocal for ≥12 years (based on 0.9 IU/dL per IU/kg).

### On-demand treatment and control of bleeding (guidance)
- Target circulating factor IX levels and recommended dosing intervals (general guide):
  - Minor bleeding/uncomplicated hemarthrosis, superficial muscle/soft tissue bleeding: target 20–30% (IU/dL); repeat every 12–24 hours; duration at least 1 day until healing.
  - Moderate bleeding/intramuscular or soft tissue with dissection, mucous membranes, hematuria: target 25–50%; repeat every 12–24 hours; 2–7 days until bleeding stops and healing achieved.
  - Major bleeding/pharyngeal, retropharyngeal, retroperitoneal, central nervous system: target 50–100%; repeat every 12–24 hours; 7–10 days until bleeding stops and healing achieved.

### Perioperative management (guidance)
- Minor procedures (e.g., tooth extraction): target 30–60%; dosing interval ~24 hours; at least 1 day until healing.
- Major surgery (e.g., intracranial, intra‑abdominal, intrathoracic, joint replacement): target 80–100%; dosing interval 8–24 hours; duration 7–10 days until bleeding stops and healing achieved.

### Routine prophylaxis (previously treated patients)
- Patients <12 years of age: 60–80 IU/kg twice weekly.
- Patients ≥12 years of age: 40–60 IU/kg twice weekly.
- Adjust dose and frequency based on individual age, bleeding phenotype, physical activity, and pharmacokinetic profile.

### Special dosing notes and examples
- Use individual incremental recovery for dose calculation where available; patients at low end of observed recovery range may require higher doses.
- Examples (illustrative in source material):
  - 20 kg child: 1,500 IU expected to raise factor IX by ~53.6 IU/dL when using pediatric recovery assumptions.
  - 70 kg adult: 4,550 IU expected to raise factor IX by ~58.5 IU/dL given adult recovery assumptions.
  - To achieve a 70% level in a 60 kg adult: calculate using reciprocal based on 0.9 IU/dL per IU/kg gives ~4,667 IU.

### Preparation and reconstitution
- Use aseptic technique.
- Allow product vial and diluent to reach room temperature prior to reconstitution.
- Cleanse vial stoppers with a germicidal solution and let dry.
- Use the supplied reconstitution device as instructed (device-specific instructions were provided in source material); do not reuse the device for a second vial.
- Insert diluent into the product vial to allow vacuum transfer of diluent; swirl gently until powder is completely dissolved.
- Do not refrigerate after reconstitution.
- Use the reconstituted product within 3 hours; discard any unused portion.

### Administration
- Administer by intravenous bolus infusion only.
- Continuous infusion safety/efficacy not established.
- Use a plastic syringe for administration.
- Do not inject air.
- Maximum infusion rate reported: 10 mL/min.
- Contents from multiple vials may be drawn into the same syringe for administration if required.
- Inspect solution for particulate matter or discoloration; solution should be clear and colorless. Do not use if turbidity or particulates are present.
- Observe standard precautions to prevent transmission of bloodborne pathogens (sharps handling, immediate attention for needle-stick injuries).

### Missed dose
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to **coagulation factor ix (recombinant)** or any of its excipients, including hypersensitivity to residual rodent (Chinese hamster ovary/hamster) proteins present in trace amounts.
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
- Signs of fibrinolysis.

(These contraindications reflect all contraindications present in available source material.)

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed warnings
- None specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis: Allergic reactions including anaphylaxis may occur; risk may be higher in previously untreated patients and in those with certain high‑risk gene mutations. Early signs include angioedema, chest tightness, hypotension, lethargy, nausea/vomiting, paresthesia, restlessness, wheeze, and dyspnea. Discontinue administration and initiate appropriate treatment if allergic-type reactions occur. Consider alternative hemostatic measures in severe allergic reactions.
- Trace amounts of non-human mammalian protein (CHO/hamster) are present and may provoke hypersensitivity.
- Development of factor IX inhibitors: Regularly evaluate patients for inhibitors. If expected plasma factor IX activity is not achieved or bleeding is not controlled with appropriate dosing, perform an assay for factor IX inhibitor concentration. Patients with inhibitors may be less responsive; high-titer inhibitors may render product ineffective; contact specialized hemophilia treatment centres where appropriate.
- Nephrotic syndrome: Cases of nephrotic syndrome have been reported following attempted immune tolerance induction with factor IX products in hemophilia B patients with inhibitors. Safety and efficacy of this product for immune tolerance induction have not been established.
- Thromboembolic complications: Factor IX–containing products have been associated with thromboembolic events (pulmonary embolism, venous and arterial thrombosis). Monitor for early signs of thromboembolic events and consumptive coagulopathy, particularly in patients with liver disease, signs of fibrinolysis, peri- and post-operative states, or other risk factors for thrombosis or DIC. Weigh benefit vs risk in patients at high risk.
- Monitoring: Measure factor IX activity (one‑stage clotting assay) to ensure adequate levels are achieved and maintained. Monitor for inhibitor development and, where indicated, thrombogenicity markers.
- Re-exposure in inhibitor-positive patients: Patients with factor IX inhibitors are at increased risk of severe hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis upon re‑exposure; alternative therapies should be considered.

### Monitoring requirements
- Factor IX activity: one‑stage clotting assay recommended to guide dosing and assess recovery.
- Inhibitor testing: assay results expressed in Bethesda units (BUs) when testing for factor IX inhibitors.
- Consider monitoring thrombogenicity markers (e.g., thrombin–antithrombin III, prothrombin fragment 1.2, D‑dimer) in settings of increased thrombotic risk or clinical concern.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Use in surgery: monitor factor levels perioperatively and adjust dosing to achieve recommended target ranges.
- Pediatric considerations: pediatric patients show lower incremental recovery and increased clearance per kg; adjust dosing informed by pharmacokinetic assessment.
- Infection control: standard precautions for IV administration and sharps handling apply.

| Vascular/thrombotic events | Thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis (class effect with factor IX products) | Not known (postmarketing/class adverse reaction) |

Notes:
- In the combined clinical development trial of 99 previously treated patients (PTPs), there were 14,018 infusions and 337 adverse events reported in 80 subjects; the specific events listed above were the ones that occurred in >1% of trial subjects.
- No neutralizing (inhibitory) antibodies to factor IX were detected in the clinical trial population.
- Signals for binding antibodies (furin, CHO protein) were observed; many signals were indeterminate or transient and were not associated with clinical adverse findings in the trial.
- Postmarketing reports have included hypersensitivity and cutaneous reactions; frequency cannot be reliably estimated from spontaneous reports.

### Serious adverse effects (highlighted)
- Anaphylaxis and severe hypersensitivity reactions — may be life‑threatening; discontinue product and treat immediately.
- Development of factor IX inhibitors — may result in loss of efficacy and increased risk of hypersensitivity upon re-exposure.
- Thromboembolic events and consumptive coagulopathy — rare but serious class-related events; monitor and evaluate risks prior to administration.

## 10. Use in Special Populations

### Paediatric use
- Safety, efficacy and pharmacokinetics evaluated in 23 previously treated pediatric patients (11 subjects <6 years; 12 subjects 6 to <12 years).
- Observed pediatric differences:
  - Average 22% lower incremental recovery in pediatric patients <12 years versus adults.
  - Clearance per kg was higher in children compared with adults (reported increases: 30% higher in 6–12 years cohort; 59% higher in <6 years cohort).
- Dosing adjustments are required in pediatric patients; use individual pharmacokinetic parameters (incremental recovery, half‑life) to guide dosing.

## 11. Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action
- **coagulation factor ix (recombinant)** supplies exogenous recombinant factor IX to increase circulating factor IX activity and thereby support hemostasis in patients with factor IX deficiency (hemophilia B).
- Empirical recovery: ~0.7 IU/dL per IU/kg in patients <12 years; ~0.9 IU/dL per IU/kg in patients ≥12 years (used clinically to calculate dose).

### Pharmacokinetics
- Incremental recovery: approximately 0.7 IU/dL per IU/kg in children <12 years and approximately 0.9 IU/dL per IU/kg in patients ≥12 years.
- Pediatric vs adult differences: pediatric patients exhibited lower incremental recovery and higher clearance per kg (approximately 30% higher clearance in 6–12 years, 59% higher in <6 years, relative to adults).
- Half‑life and detailed absorption/distribution/metabolism/elimination parameters: Not specified in available sources.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Restoration of factor IX plasma activity correlates with hemostatic efficacy; monitoring via one‑stage clotting assay is recommended.
- Thrombogenicity markers (thrombin–antithrombin III, prothrombin fragment 1.2, D‑dimer) were assessed in pharmacokinetic evaluations and showed no pattern indicative of clinically relevant thrombogenicity in trial subjects.

## 12. Clinical Trials

### Key clinical development data (summarized from available sources)
- Combined clinical development trial population: 99 previously treated male patients with hemophilia B (≥150 exposure days to factor IX products prior to study).
  - Age distribution: 11 subjects <6 years; 12 subjects 6 to <12 years; 3 adolescents (12 to <16 years); 73 adults (≥16 years).
  - Exposure: total of 14,018 infusions; median of 163 infusions per subject (range 8–327); median of 156 exposure days (range 8–316).
  - Indications in trial: on‑demand treatment, perioperative management (major/minor surgery, dental/invasive procedures), routine prophylaxis, pharmacokinetic evaluation.
- Safety findings:
  - 337 adverse events in 80 of 99 subjects.
  - Adverse events reported in >1% included dysgeusia, pain in extremity, and positive furin antibody test signals.
  - No neutralizing (inhibitory) anti‑factor IX antibodies detected in the trial population.
  - Low‑titer, non‑neutralizing anti‑factor IX binding antibodies observed in 21.2% of subjects at one or more time points; no associated clinical adverse findings reported.
  - Signals for antibodies against furin in ~19.2% of subjects (many indeterminate or present at baseline).
- Thrombogenicity:
  - No clinical evidence of thromboembolic events in trial subjects.
  - Laboratory thrombogenicity markers showed out‑of‑range values in some assays but no pattern suggesting clinically relevant thrombogenicity.

### Trial design and primary outcomes
- Trial designs included open‑label dosing for on‑demand, perioperative and prophylactic use and pharmacokinetic crossover evaluations in previously treated patients.
- Primary clinical efficacy outcomes as described in the source documents: not fully detailed in the available excerpt; efficacy for bleeding control, perioperative hemostasis and prophylaxis was the clinical intent but specific primary endpoint statistics were not specified in available sources.
