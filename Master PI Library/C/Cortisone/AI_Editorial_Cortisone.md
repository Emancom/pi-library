# Editorial Product Information: Cortisone (Cortisone Acetate)

## Editorial Note
- The information in this editorial synthesis is derived from the official Product Information (PI) provided by the following regulatory authority: **Australian (TGA)**. 
- This document serves as a harmonized clinical overview for healthcare professionals. 
- **Note:** This is an editorial synthesis and does not substitute for the official PI required for prescribing in any specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should refer to the local approved labeling for their specific region.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Cortisone acetate (a glucocorticoid with mineralocorticoid activity).
- **Brand Name(s):** Cortate [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** H02AB10.
- **Dosage Form(s) and Strength(s):** 
    - 5 mg tablets (round, flat, white) [Australian].
    - 25 mg tablets (round, flat, white, scored) [Australian].
- **Route(s) of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Aspen Pharmacare Australia Pty Ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
Cortisone acetate is indicated for the following conditions [Australian]:
- **Endocrine Disorders:** Addison's disease.
- **Allergic States:** Status asthmaticus, angioneurotic oedema, serum sickness, and drug sensitisation.
- **Collagen/Vascular Disorders:** Periarteritis nodosa, disseminated lupus erythematosus, and giant cell arteritis.

## 3. Dosage and Administration
- **General Dosing:** Requirements are highly variable and must be individualized based on disease severity and patient response.
- **Initiation:** In adults, an initial dose of 25 mg every 6 hours may be utilized [Australian].
- **Maintenance/Titration:** Once clinical remission is achieved, the daily dose should be tapered by 10 to 20 mg every few days until the minimum effective maintenance dose is reached.
- **Acute/Severe States:** Anaphylactic or acute sensitivity states may necessitate significantly higher doses.
- **Withdrawal:** For patients who have received systemic therapy for >3 weeks, withdrawal must not be abrupt. Once a dose equivalent to 7.5 mg of prednisolone is reached, reduction should be slower to allow the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis to recover [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
- Hypersensitivity to cortisone acetate or any tablet excipients (e.g., lactose monohydrate).
- Uncontrolled systemic infections.
- Active or doubtfully quiescent tuberculosis (unless used as an adjunct to tuberculostatic therapy) [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **HPA Axis Suppression:** Prolonged use may lead to adrenal cortical atrophy and HPA axis suppression. Abrupt withdrawal may precipitate acute, potentially fatal adrenal insufficiency.
- **Stress Situations:** During periods of trauma, surgery, or infection, a temporary increase in dosage is usually required.
- **Infection Masking:** Glucocorticoids may mask signs of infection and decrease resistance to new bacterial, fungal, viral, or parasitic pathogens. 
- **Ocular Effects:** Prolonged use may result in posterior subcapsular cataracts or glaucoma (with potential optic nerve damage). Cortisone is associated with central serous chorioretinopathy and potential retinal detachment. Use with extreme caution in ocular herpes simplex due to the risk of corneal perforation [Australian].
- **Osteoporosis:** High risk of bone density loss, particularly in postmenopausal women.
- **Co-morbidities:** Use with caution in patients with:
    - Hypertension or congestive heart failure (due to sodium/water retention).
    - Diabetes mellitus (increased insulin requirements).
    - Peptic ulcer, ulcerative colitis (risk of perforation), or diverticulitis.
    - Epilepsy or emotional instability/psychotic tendencies.
    - Hypothyroidism or cirrhosis (enhanced steroid effect).
- **Vaccination:** Live vaccines are contraindicated during therapy. Other immunizations should be avoided due to potential neurological complications and lack of antibody response (except in replacement therapy) [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
- **Enzyme Inducers (e.g., Phenobarbital, Phenytoin, Rifampicin):** Increase clearance of cortisone; may require dose increase.
- **Enzyme Inhibitors (e.g., Ketoconazole, Estrogens):** May decrease clearance; may require dose reduction to avoid toxicity.
- **Anticoagulants:** Variable effects on prothrombin time; requires close monitoring of coagulation indices.
- **Salicylates:** Cortisone increases salicylate metabolism. Withdrawal of cortisone may lead to salicylate toxicity.
- **Antacids/Cholestyramine/Colestipol:** May decrease cortisone absorption by adsorption.
- **Diuretics/Amphotericin B:** Increased risk of hypokalaemia.
- **Cardiac Glycosides:** Hypokalaemia increases the risk of digitalis toxicity.
- **Insulin/Antidiabetics:** Cortisone causes hyperglycaemia; dosage adjustment of antidiabetics may be necessary.
- **Alcohol:** Increased risk of gastric irritation and GI ulceration [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** 
    - **Australian Category A:** Drugs which have been taken by a large number of pregnant women and women of childbearing age without any proven increase in the frequency of malformations or other direct or indirect harmful effects on the fetus having been observed.
    - Prolonged treatment may result in reduced placental and birth weight. Infants born to mothers receiving significant doses should be monitored for adrenal insufficiency.
- **Breastfeeding:** Cortisone is excreted in breast milk; therefore, administration to nursing mothers is **not recommended** [Australian].
- **Fertility:** No data available in source documents; however, sperm motility and count changes have been reported.

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Children on long-term therapy must be monitored for growth retardation, obesity, and HPA axis suppression. Cortisone is approximately 1/10th as potent as prednisone regarding growth retardation [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Recommended caution as the elderly are more susceptible to adverse reactions, especially osteoporosis and fluid retention.
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Reduction in dosage may be necessary. In chronic active liver disease, major adverse reactions occur in approximately 30% of patients. In cirrhosis, the half-life of active hydrocortisone is prolonged to approximately 5 hours [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Use with caution; renal insufficiency may unmask latent hypoparathyroidism via phosphate retention [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** A corticosteroid with both glucocorticoid (anti-inflammatory, metabolic) and mineralocorticoid (salt-retaining) activity. It modifies the immune response and causes varied metabolic effects, including increased gluconeogenesis.
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Metabolism:** Cortisone is a prodrug. It is inactive until the 11-oxo group is reduced by 11-β-hydroxy-dehydrogenase to the active form, hydrocortisone (cortisol).
    - **Bioavailability:** Approximately 80% relative to hydrocortisone.
    - **Distribution:** Hydrocortisone is >90% protein-bound. The biological half-life is approximately 100 minutes.
    - **Elimination:** Primarily metabolized to inactive tetrahydrocortisone and tetrahydrocortisol, which are excreted in the urine as glucuronide conjugates [Australian].
