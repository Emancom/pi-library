# Editorial Product Information: cephalexin

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **cephalexin**
- Chemical class: First‑generation cephalosporin (beta‑lactam antibiotic)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- [Australian] Date of first approval (250 mg strength): 15 September 2000
- [Australian] Date of first approval (500 mg strength): 20 February 2007
- [Australian] Date of last revision/update of the product information: 12 July 2024
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- Registration/approval dates for other jurisdictions: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
Cefalexin is indicated for the treatment of infections caused by susceptible organisms. Indications listed in the available sources include:
- Respiratory tract infections: Streptococcus pneumoniae and group A beta‑haemolytic Streptococci (note: penicillin is the usual drug of choice for streptococcal infections and prevention of rheumatic fever; cefalexin can eradicate streptococci from the nasopharynx but data on prevention of rheumatic fever are not available)
- Bacterial sinusitis: Streptococci, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus (methicillin‑sensitive strains only)
- Otitis media: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococci
- Skin and skin structure infections: Staphylococci and/or Streptococci
- Genitourinary tract infections, including acute prostatitis: Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella species

Notes:
- The effectiveness of cefalexin for infections of the brain and spinal column has not been established; cefalexin is not indicated for these conditions.
- Appropriate culture and susceptibility testing should be performed prior to and during therapy when possible to confirm causative organisms and susceptibility.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adults)
- Usual total daily dose range: 1 to 4 g per day, given in divided doses.
- Common regimen: 250 mg every 6 hours (i.e., 1 g/day).
- For certain indications (streptococcal pharyngitis/tonsillitis, mild uncomplicated urinary tract infections, and skin and soft tissue infections): 500 mg every 12 hours may be used.
- For more severe infections or infections due to less susceptible organisms, larger doses within the 1–4 g/day range may be required.
- If daily cefalexin requirements exceed 4 g, consider switching to appropriate parenteral cephalosporin therapy.
- Twice‑daily dosing is not recommended when the total daily dose exceeds 1 g.

### Paediatric dosing
- Usual recommended total daily dose for children: 25–50 mg/kg/day given in divided doses.
- For streptococcal pharyngitis (patients >1 year), tonsillitis, mild uncomplicated urinary tract infections and skin/soft tissue infections: total daily dose may be divided and given every 12 hours.
- In severe infections, the daily dose may be doubled (i.e., toward the upper end of the range).
- Otitis media: clinical studies support 75–100 mg/kg/day divided into four doses.
- Example dosing guidance (suspension concentrations cited in the available sources):
  - For 125 mg/5 mL suspension (four times daily): approximately
    - 10 kg: 2.5–5 mL four times daily
    - 20 kg: 5–10 mL four times daily
    - 40 kg: 10–20 mL four times daily
  - For 250 mg/5 mL suspension (four times daily):
    - 20 kg: 2.5–5 mL four times daily
    - 40 kg: 5–10 mL four times daily
  - Twice‑daily equivalents (per source tables) are provided for certain weights and concentrations (see source dosing tables).
- In the treatment of beta‑haemolytic streptococcal infections, cefalexin therapy should be continued for at least 10 days.

### Administration instructions
- Route: oral.
- Cefalexin is acid‑stable and may be administered without regard to meals.
- For patients with markedly impaired renal function, cefalexin should be used with caution and careful clinical and laboratory monitoring; safe dosage may be lower than usual (specific dosing adjustments by eGFR or creatinine clearance are not specified in the available sources).

### Titration schedules
- Not specified in available sources.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to cephalosporins (cephalosporin group).
- History of a major allergic reaction to penicillins (previous major allergy to penicillin) — use of cefalexin is contraindicated in such patients due to risk of cross‑reactivity.
- No other jurisdiction‑specific contraindications are specified in the available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- No boxed warnings specified in the available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Prior to initiating therapy, determine whether the patient has known hypersensitivity to cephalosporins, penicillins, or other drugs. Exercise caution when administering to penicillin‑sensitive patients because partial cross‑allergenicity exists.
- Severe hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, have been reported with cephalosporins and penicillins. If an allergic reaction occurs, discontinue cefalexin and treat appropriately (e.g., adrenaline, antihistamines, corticosteroids, supportive care).
- Pseudomembranous colitis (Clostridioides difficile–associated diarrhoea) has been reported with virtually all broad‑spectrum antibiotics, including cephalosporins. The onset can be during therapy or up to several weeks after cessation. Consider the diagnosis in patients who develop diarrhoea or colitis; mild cases may resolve on discontinuation of the antibiotic, whereas moderate to severe cases require specific therapy directed at C. difficile, with supportive measures (fluids, electrolytes, protein replacement) as indicated. Antiperistaltic agents (e.g., opiates, diphenoxylate/atropine) may exacerbate the condition and should be avoided.
- Prolonged use may result in overgrowth of non‑susceptible organisms; monitor and manage superinfections as appropriate.
- Neurotoxicity: encephalopathy, seizures and/or myoclonus have been reported with cephalosporin treatment. Risk factors for neurotoxicity include advanced age, renal impairment, pre‑existing central nervous system disorders and intravenous administration of cephalosporins. Consider withdrawal of cefalexin if signs of neurotoxicity occur; anticonvulsant therapy may be given if clinically indicated.
- Impaired renal function: use cefalexin cautiously when renal function is markedly impaired. Careful clinical and laboratory monitoring is recommended; safe dosage may be lower than usual.
- In surgical infections, perform indicated surgical procedures in conjunction with antimicrobial therapy.

### Monitoring requirements
- Obtain appropriate cultures and susceptibility testing prior to and during therapy when feasible to ensure the causative organism is susceptible to cefalexin.
- Monitor renal function in patients with or at risk of renal impairment; adjust therapy as clinically indicated (specific renal dosing is not provided).
- Monitor for signs of hypersensitivity, diarrhoea (including severe or persistent diarrhoea), superinfection, hepatic dysfunction, haemolysis, and other adverse reactions.
- Laboratory test interactions: cefalexin may interfere with certain tests (see below).

### Specific clinical precautions
- Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as Stevens‑Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) have been reported in patients taking beta‑lactam antibiotics. If SCAR is suspected, discontinue cefalexin immediately and consider alternative therapy.
- Effects on laboratory tests:
  - Quantitative measurement of urinary protein excretion using strong acids may be misleading during cefalexin therapy due to precipitation of cefalexin in urine.
  - False‑positive urine glucose tests may occur with Benedict's or Fehling's solutions or with Clinitest®.
  - Positive direct Coombs’ tests have been reported during cephalosporin therapy; positive antiglobulin tests in newborns whose mothers received cephalosporins before parturition may be drug‑related.

## 7. Adverse Effects
Adverse reactions reported in the available sources are listed below. The product information states that adverse drug reactions reported with cefalexin are very rare (<0.01%). Where a specific statement of frequency is not provided for an individual reaction, the source either lists the reaction without a frequency or notes frequency as not known.

- Serious adverse effects highlighted: anaphylaxis, pseudomembranous colitis (C. difficile associated), severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SJS, TEN, DRESS, AGEP, SDRIFE), haemolytic anaemia, cholestatic jaundice/transient hepatitis, interstitial nephritis, seizures, encephalopathy, myoclonus.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Probenecid: As with other beta‑lactams, co‑administration of probenecid inhibits renal excretion of cefalexin (i.e., increases cefalexin exposure).
- Metformin: In healthy subjects given single 500 mg doses of cefalexin concurrently with metformin, metformin Cmax and AUC increased (average increases of ~34% and ~24%, respectively) and metformin renal clearance decreased (~14% average). The interaction after multiple‑dose co‑administration has not been studied. Administration of a cephalosporin to a patient on metformin may result in increased metformin exposure; monitor patients accordingly.

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Not specified in available sources beyond the interactions above.

### Clinically significant interactions — management notes
- When co‑administering probenecid, be aware that cefalexin plasma concentrations may be increased; adjust therapy if clinically indicated.
- When co‑administering with metformin, monitor for increased metformin exposure and potential adverse effects of metformin; consider monitoring renal function and metformin‑related adverse effects.
- Avoid antiperistaltic agents in patients with antibiotic‑associated diarrhoea suspected to be C. difficile‑associated, as these may worsen the clinical course.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- [Australian] Pregnancy category: Category A. Laboratory animal studies and clinical experience have not demonstrated evidence of teratogenicity with cefalexin; however, as with all drugs, cefalexin should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed and with caution.
- Pregnancy category or classification in other jurisdictions: Not specified in available sources.

### Breastfeeding
- Cefalexin is excreted in human milk. Exercise caution when administering cefalexin to a breastfeeding woman. Consider alternative feeding arrangements for the infant if clinically appropriate.

### Fertility
- Effects on fertility: Not specified in available sources.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Dosing: see Section 4 for recommended paediatric dosing (25–50 mg/kg/day in divided doses; otitis media 75–100 mg/kg/day divided into four doses). Clinical dosing tables for oral suspension concentrations were provided in the available sources.
- Safety data statement in the product information: "Paediatric use: No data available." (Note: dosing recommendations are provided in the PI; the source also states lack of specific data in certain paediatric safety sections.)

### Elderly use
- Safety data statement in the product information: "Use in the elderly: No data available."
- Clinical caution: elderly patients are at increased risk for neurotoxicity with cephalosporins; exercise caution and monitor renal function and neurological status.

### Renal impairment
- Cefalexin should be administered with caution in patients with markedly impaired renal function. The PI states that careful clinical and laboratory monitoring should be undertaken because the safe dosage may be lower than usual. Specific dosage adjustment tables by creatinine clearance or eGFR are not provided in the available sources.

### Hepatic impairment

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Cefalexin is bactericidal via inhibition of bacterial cell wall synthesis (beta‑lactam mechanism).

### Antibacterial spectrum / Pharmacodynamics
- Active in vitro against: beta‑haemolytic Streptococci, Staphylococci (including coagulase‑positive, coagulase‑negative and penicillinase‑producing strains), Streptococcus (Diplococcus) pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella species.
- Not active against most strains of Enterobacter spp., Morganella morganii, Proteus vulgaris, Pseudomonas spp., or Acinetobacter calcoaceticus.
- Many Enterococci (Enterococcus faecalis) and some Staphylococci are resistant.
- Staphylococci may show cross‑resistance between cefalexin and methicillin‑type antibiotics.

### Microbiological susceptibility testing guidance (as provided)
- Disc/dilution methods should follow standardised procedures.
- MIC breakpoints (as provided using cephalothin disc zone proxies):
  - Susceptible: zone diameter ≥ 18 mm; MIC ≤ 8 µg/mL
  - Moderately susceptible (intermediate): zone diameter 15–17 mm; MIC 1–16 µg/mL
  - Resistant: zone diameter ≤ 14 mm; MIC > 16 µg/mL
- Local prevalence of resistance may vary; use local susceptibility data where available.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Cefalexin is acid‑stable and rapidly absorbed after oral administration; food not required.
- Peak serum concentrations: After oral doses of 250 mg, 500 mg and 1 g, average peak serum levels were approximately 9, 18 and 32 µg/mL respectively, achieved about 1 hour after dosing. Measurable serum levels may be present at 6 hours post‑dose.
- Distribution: Not detailed beyond serum level data in available sources.
- Metabolism: Not extensively metabolised; majority is excreted unchanged.
- Elimination: Excreted primarily by the kidney via glomerular filtration and tubular secretion; over 90% of an oral dose is excreted unchanged in urine within 8 hours. Reported peak urine concentrations after 250 mg, 500 mg and 1 g doses were approximately 1,000, 2,200 and 5,000 µg/mL, respectively.
- Genotoxicity and carcinogenicity: No data available in the available sources.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- The available product information states "No data available" for clinical trials and does not describe pivotal trial summaries, trial designs, or primary outcome data in the provided sources.

## Overdose
- Reported clinical experience of severe overdose with cefalexin is limited. Clinical features of overdose may be similar to those seen with other beta‑lactams and penicillins: convulsions, hallucinations, hyperreflexia, electrolyte imbalances, gastrointestinal disturbances and haematuria.
- Treatment: General supportive care and close clinical and laboratory monitoring of haematological, renal and hepatic function and coagulation parameters until the patient stabilises.
- Measures such as forced diuresis, peritoneal dialysis, haemodialysis or charcoal haemoperfusion have not been established as beneficial for cefalexin overdose.
- For regional poison information and management, local poison control services should be consulted. (Poison centre contact numbers are not specified here except where present in local clinical resources.)
