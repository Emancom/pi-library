# Editorial Product Information: carbidopa + levodopa

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): carbidopa + levodopa
- Chemical class:
  - Levodopa: aromatic amino acid, metabolic precursor of dopamine
  - Carbidopa: peripheral aromatic L‑amino acid decarboxylase inhibitor (does not cross the blood–brain barrier)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of Parkinson's disease.
- Post‑encephalitic parkinsonism.
- Symptomatic parkinsonism following carbon monoxide intoxication or manganese intoxication.
- Additional indication or jurisdictional differences: Not specified in available sources.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General
- Available marketed strengths (as reported in source material): combinations containing 10 mg carbidopa/100 mg levodopa; 25 mg carbidopa/100 mg levodopa; and 25 mg carbidopa/250 mg levodopa. (These are reported strengths; consult local product labelling for marketed presentations.)
- Pharmacologic effect relevant to dosing: co‑administration of carbidopa reduces the amount of levodopa required to produce a given clinical response by about 75% and increases levodopa plasma levels and half‑life.

### Administration instructions and switching
- When switching patients already receiving levodopa monotherapy to a carbidopa + levodopa product, levodopa should be discontinued at least 12 hours before initiating carbidopa + levodopa.
- Nonselective monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors must be discontinued at least 2 weeks before initiating therapy with carbidopa + levodopa.
- Carbidopa allows more rapid upward titration of levodopa in many patients by reducing peripheral levodopa‑induced nausea and vomiting; therapy should be individualized and titrated according to clinical response and tolerability.
- Levodopa absorption may be delayed or reduced by high‑protein meals and by excessive gastric acidity; iron salts (including multivitamins containing iron) may reduce bioavailability via chelation — coadministration should be managed with caution.

## 5. Contraindications
- Concomitant use with nonselective MAO inhibitors (must discontinue these agents at least 2 weeks before starting carbidopa + levodopa).
- Known hypersensitivity to carbidopa, levodopa, or any excipient of the formulation.
- Narrow‑angle glaucoma.
- Other contraindications or jurisdictional variations: Not specified in available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / highlighted warnings
- Not specified in available sources as a jurisdictional boxed warning; however, several critical safety concerns are described below.

### Major general warnings and precautions
- **Abrupt dose reduction or withdrawal** of dopaminergic therapy (including carbidopa + levodopa) has been associated with a syndrome resembling neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS): fever, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability and laboratory abnormalities (e.g., elevated creatine phosphokinase, leukocytosis, myoglobinuria). Observe patients carefully when reducing or discontinuing therapy, especially if neuroleptics are coadministered.
- **Falling asleep/somnolence:** patients treated with dopaminergic agents, including carbidopa + levodopa, have reported sudden onset of sleep without warning while engaged in activities of daily living (including driving). Assess for somnolence risk factors (concomitant sedating medications, sleep disorders). Patients who experience excessive daytime sleepiness or episodes of sudden sleep onset should not drive or operate machinery; consider dose reduction or discontinuation if events occur.
- **Psychiatric effects:** hallucinations, psychotic‑like behavior, confusion, agitation, delirium and other abnormal thinking/behavior have been reported. Patients with major psychotic disorders ordinarily should not receive carbidopa + levodopa because of potential exacerbation of psychosis.
- **Impulse control/compulsive behaviors:** dopaminergic therapy can be associated with intense urges (gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive spending, binge eating and other urges). These behaviors may remit on dose reduction or discontinuation. Specifically ask patients and caregivers about new or increased gambling urges, sexual urges, or other compulsive behaviors.
- **Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) deficiency:** therapy with carbidopa + levodopa may contribute to reduced vitamin B6 levels, particularly at higher doses. Reports exist of seizures associated with vitamin B6 deficiency that were refractory to antiseizure drugs and resolved only after B6 supplementation. Evaluate and monitor vitamin B6 levels prior to initiation and periodically during therapy; supplement as necessary.
- **Cardiovascular caution:** use cautiously in patients with severe cardiovascular disease, including those with a history of myocardial infarction and residual arrhythmias (atrial, nodal, ventricular). Cardiac monitoring is advised during initial dosage adjustment for patients with significant cardiac disease.
- **Orthostatic hypotension:** symptomatic postural hypotension has been reported, including in combination with antihypertensive agents; evaluate and adjust concomitant antihypertensive therapy as needed.
- **Pulmonary, renal, hepatic, endocrine disease:** exercise caution and monitor function in patients with severe pulmonary disease (including bronchial asthma), renal impairment, hepatic impairment, or endocrine disease.
- **Peptic ulcer disease:** as with levodopa, treatment may increase the possibility of upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage in patients with a history of peptic ulcer.

### Monitoring requirements
- Periodic monitoring of hepatic, hematopoietic, cardiovascular, and renal function during extended therapy.
- Monitor for psychiatric symptoms, impulse control disorders, signs of somnolence/sudden sleep onset.
- Monitor vitamin B6 status before and periodically during therapy, and supplement if indicated.
- Periodic skin examinations — epidemiologic data indicate a higher rate of melanoma in patients with Parkinson’s disease; monitor for suspicious skin lesions.
- Laboratory monitoring: liver function tests (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, LDH, bilirubin), BUN, creatinine, uric acid, and hemolytic parameters as clinically indicated. Exercise caution interpreting catecholamine and metabolite levels in patients on levodopa therapy.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Carbidopa does not cross the blood–brain barrier and does not inhibit central decarboxylation of levodopa; therefore central adverse effects related to increased brain dopamine (dyskinesias, psychiatric effects) may occur at lower doses and earlier when carbidopa is coadministered.
- Patients and caregivers should be advised about possible urine/saliva/sweat discoloration (red, brown, or black) — clinically insignificant but may stain clothing.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic interactions (clinically significant)
- Monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors:
  - Nonselective MAO inhibitors: contraindicated. Must be discontinued at least 2 weeks prior to initiation.
  - Selective MAO‑B inhibitors (e.g., selegiline): may be administered concomitantly at manufacturer‑recommended doses; however, concomitant therapy has been associated with severe orthostatic hypotension (clinically significant). Monitor blood pressure and orthostatic symptoms.
- Antihypertensive agents:
  - Symptomatic postural hypotension has been reported when carbidopa + levodopa is added to antihypertensive regimens; monitor and consider antihypertensive dose adjustment.
- Tricyclic antidepressants:
  - Rare reports of hypertension and dyskinesia when used concomitantly.
- Dopamine receptor antagonists and other agents that reduce dopaminergic effects:
  - Dopamine D2 receptor antagonists (e.g., phenothiazines, butyrophenones, risperidone) and isoniazid can reduce the therapeutic effect of levodopa; observe for loss of response.
  - Phenytoin and papaverine have been reported to reverse the beneficial effects of levodopa.
- Dopamine‑depleting agents:
  - Reserpine, tetrabenazine, and other agents known to deplete monoamine stores: concomitant use is not recommended.
- Iron salts / multivitamins with iron:
  - Iron can chelate levodopa and carbidopa, reducing bioavailability; coadministration should be undertaken with caution (separate administration or clinical monitoring).
- Metoclopramide:
  - May increase levodopa bioavailability by increasing gastric emptying but may worsen disease control because it is a dopamine receptor antagonist.
- Pyridoxine (vitamin B6):
  - Oral pyridoxine (10–25 mg) can enhance peripheral decarboxylation of levodopa and reverse levodopa effects; carbidopa inhibits this pyridoxine effect, allowing coadministration with B6 supplements when clinically appropriate.
- Other interactions:
  - Concomitant use with selegiline and carbidopa + levodopa may cause severe orthostatic hypotension not attributable to carbidopa + levodopa alone.

### Management notes
- Review concomitant medications for agents that antagonize dopamine receptors or deplete monoamines.
- Monitor blood pressure, especially when starting or adjusting therapy in patients on antihypertensive agents or MAO inhibitors.
- Consider separating administration from iron supplements or administering under clinical supervision if clinically necessary.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
- Paediatric use:
  - Not specified in available sources (no paediatric dosing or approval details provided).
- Elderly use:
  - Pharmacokinetic observations: studies reported increased systemic exposure (AUC) of levodopa in elderly subjects compared with young subjects; reported increases included a 55% increase in one small study (healthy elderly vs healthy young) and a 28% increase in another study of patients with Parkinson’s disease (elderly ≥65 vs <65). Cmax of levodopa increased by ~24% in elderly patients in one study. AUC of carbidopa was increased by approximately 29% in elderly subjects in one study following IV administration. These increases were not considered to necessitate specific dose adjustments in the source text but require clinical monitoring and cautious titration.
- Renal impairment:
  - Specific dosing adjustments by eGFR or renal stage: Not specified in available sources. Exercise caution and monitor renal function during therapy.
- Hepatic impairment:
  - Specific dosing adjustments by severity: Not specified in available sources. Periodic monitoring of hepatic function is recommended.
- Other special population notes:
  - Use with caution and monitor closely in patients with severe cardiovascular or pulmonary disease, bronchial asthma, renal, hepatic or endocrine disease.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **Levodopa**: a metabolic precursor of dopamine that crosses the blood–brain barrier and is converted to dopamine in the brain, restoring striatal dopamine levels and improving Parkinsonian motor symptoms.
- **Carbidopa**: an aromatic L‑amino acid decarboxylase inhibitor that inhibits peripheral decarboxylation of levodopa, thereby increasing the proportion of administered levodopa available to cross the blood–brain barrier. Carbidopa does not cross the blood–brain barrier and does not inhibit central decarboxylation.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Carbidopa reduces peripheral conversion of levodopa to dopamine, decreasing peripheral dopamine‑mediated adverse effects (notably nausea and vomiting) and permitting lower levodopa doses to achieve clinical effect.
- Since more levodopa reaches the brain when given with carbidopa, central dopamine‑related adverse effects (e.g., dyskinesias, psychiatric effects) may occur sooner or at lower levodopa doses than with levodopa alone.
- Levodopa competes with certain large neutral amino acids for absorption and transport; high‑protein meals can impair levodopa absorption and efficacy.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption:
  - Levodopa is rapidly decarboxylated in extracerebral tissues when given alone; coadministration of carbidopa decreases peripheral decarboxylation and increases plasma availability of levodopa.
  - At steady state, carbidopa bioavailability from combined administration is reported as approximately 99% relative to coadministration (source phrasing).
- Distribution:
  - Not specified in available sources (detailed distribution parameters not provided).
- Metabolism:
  - Levodopa is decarboxylated to dopamine peripherally; carbidopa inhibits peripheral decarboxylation. Levodopa metabolites include dopamine and homovanillic acid; carbidopa reduces plasma and urinary dopamine and homovanillic acid levels relative to levodopa alone.
- Elimination:
  - Plasma half‑life of levodopa: approximately 50 minutes without carbidopa; increased to about 1.5 hours when carbidopa is coadministered.
- Special population pharmacokinetics:
  - Elderly: increased systemic exposure (AUC) and higher Cmax of levodopa reported in elderly compared with younger subjects (see Section 10).
- Other pharmacokinetic considerations:
  - Iron salts may chelate levodopa/carbidopa and reduce bioavailability.
  - Metoclopramide may increase levodopa bioavailability by accelerating gastric emptying, but may blunt clinical benefit due to dopamine receptor antagonism.
