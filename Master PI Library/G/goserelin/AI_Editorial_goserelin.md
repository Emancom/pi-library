# Editorial Product Information: goserelin

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): goserelin  
- Chemical class: Synthetic gonadotropin‑releasing hormone (GnRH/LHRH) agonist (decapeptide analogue)  
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources  
- Route(s) of administration: Subcutaneous implant (anterior abdominal wall)

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources  
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources  
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Palliative treatment of metastatic or locally advanced prostate cancer in adult men suitable for hormonal manipulation.  
- Adjuvant and neoadjuvant therapy in combination with radiotherapy for management of locally advanced prostate cancer in men suitable for hormonal manipulation.  
- Use in females: the 10.8 mg implant formulation is not indicated for use in females (insufficient evidence of reliable suppression of serum estradiol for this formulation). For female indications, alternative goserelin formulations/dosing are referenced in source materials but are not described here.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Adult men (including elderly): one 10.8 mg subcutaneous implant into the anterior abdominal wall every 3 months.

### Dosing by indication
- Prostate cancer (palliative, locally advanced): as above.  
- Adjuvant/neoadjuvant with radiotherapy: follow recommended implant schedule; neoadjuvant/ adjuvant regimens used in clinical trials most commonly employed monthly 3.6 mg formulation for some study designs. When using goserelin therapy with radiotherapy, short-term anti‑androgen therapy may be considered at initiation to reduce tumour‑flare risk.

### Administration instructions
- Insert subcutaneously into the anterior abdominal wall. Use caution due to proximity of the inferior epigastric artery and branches. Do not penetrate muscle or peritoneum.  
- The applicator device must be prepared such that the implant is visible before injection. Do not withdraw the plunger once the needle is positioned. Depress the plunger fully to expel the implant into subcutaneous tissue and to engage any protective features of the applicator. Follow the manufacturer’s administration instructions for the specific applicator provided.  
- The applicator is not a device for aspiration; if the needle inadvertently penetrates a large vessel, blood will be visible in the syringe chamber — withdraw, control bleeding, and monitor for abdominal haemorrhage. After the patient is haemodynamically stable, a new implant may be administered with a new applicator at an alternative site if required.  
- If surgical removal of an implant becomes necessary, localisation by ultrasound is possible.

### Titration / treatment duration
- Not specified in available sources beyond the stated dosing interval and clinical trial regimens (see Clinical Trials). Duration of adjuvant therapy in trials varied (months to years) according to trial protocol.

### Missed dose instructions
- Do not omit or delay scheduled injections, as serum testosterone may rise and clinical control may be lost. Clinical data indicate castrate testosterone levels are generally achieved within ~3 weeks and, in most patients, may be maintained up to 16 weeks after a 3‑month implant; nevertheless, timely repeat dosing is recommended.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to GnRH/LHRH, GnRH/LHRH agonist analogues, or any component of the medicinal product.  
- Other contraindications: Not specified in available sources beyond hypersensitivity.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- Not specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Injection site injury: pain, haematoma, haemorrhage and vascular injury have been reported. Very rarely, administration errors have resulted in vascular injury and haemorrhagic shock requiring blood transfusion and surgical intervention. Monitor after injection for bleeding or signs of abdominal haemorrhage.  
- Use extra caution when administering to patients with low body mass index or those receiving full anticoagulation due to increased risk of bleeding/haematoma.  
- Initial testosterone surge: as a GnRH agonist, goserelin causes a transient increase in serum testosterone after initiation that can last up to approximately two weeks; temporary worsening of bone pain or tumour‑flare phenomena (including spinal cord compression or ureteric obstruction) has been reported. Consider short‑term anti‑androgen therapy at initiation to reduce flare-related complications in at‑risk patients. Monitor closely during the first month of therapy for signs of spinal cord compression or ureteric obstruction; institute standard treatments if these complications occur.  
- Bone mineral density: goserelin causes loss of bone mineral density. Assess fracture risk and manage bone health according to current clinical practice.  
- Metabolic effects: hyperglycaemia and an increased risk of developing diabetes have been reported with GnRH agonists. Monitor blood glucose and/or HbA1c periodically; manage hyperglycaemia or diabetes per standard care.  
- Cardiovascular risk: increased risk of myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death and stroke has been reported in men receiving GnRH agonists. Evaluate individual cardiovascular risk factors when choosing therapy. Monitor for signs/symptoms of cardiovascular disease and manage per current clinical practice.  
- QT/QTc interval: androgen‑deprivation therapy may prolong the QT/QTc interval. Weigh benefits versus risks in patients with congenital long QT syndrome, congestive heart failure, frequent electrolyte abnormalities, or in patients receiving other QT‑prolonging medications. Correct electrolyte disturbances and consider periodic ECG and electrolyte monitoring.  
- Fertility: profound suppression of gonadal function and impairment of fertility are expected; counsel patients appropriately.  
- Paediatric use: the 10.8 mg implant is not indicated for children; safety and efficacy not established.  
- Effects on ability to drive/use machines: not assessed.

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor injection site for bleeding and local complications.  
- Monitor serum testosterone as clinically appropriate when assessing treatment response or when doses are delayed.  
- Periodically monitor blood glucose/HbA1c in patients at risk for or with preexisting diabetes.  
- Monitor for signs/symptoms of cardiovascular disease.  
- Consider ECG/electrolyte monitoring in patients at risk of QT prolongation.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Pharmacokinetic interactions: No specific clinical pharmacokinetic interaction data available in the provided source documents.  
- Pharmacodynamic interactions: Androgen‑deprivation therapy may prolong QT/QTc interval; use caution with concomitant medicinal products known to prolong QT or associated with torsades de pointes. Correct electrolytes and monitor ECG as indicated.  
- Other interactions / management notes: Observational data suggest an increased cardiovascular risk when GnRH agonists are used, particularly in combination with anti‑androgens; evaluate combined therapy risks and monitor accordingly.  
- Summary statement: No detailed interaction studies available in the source; manage concomitant medications per their known effects (e.g., QT prolongation, anticoagulation — see Warnings and Precautions regarding injection‑site bleeding).

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- Pregnancy: The 10.8 mg implant formulation is not indicated for use in females. Pregnancy category/classification systems (if any) are not specified in available sources.  
- Breastfeeding: The 10.8 mg implant formulation is not indicated for use in females; safety in breastfeeding is not specified in available sources.  
- Fertility: Expected pharmacological effect is suppression of gonadal function to castrate levels and profound impairment of fertility. Animal data indicate decreased gonadal organ weights, suppression of spermatogenesis in males, and suppression of ovarian function in females; partial or near‑complete reversibility was seen in some animal models after cessation of dosing, but reduced fertility was observed in some offspring studies. Clinical counsel: assume reversible or irreversible impairment of fertility may occur; discuss fertility preservation prior to therapy where relevant.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
- Paediatric use: Not indicated for children; safety and efficacy not established.  
- Elderly use: No dosage adjustment specified for elderly adults; standard adult dosing applies.  
- Renal impairment: No dosage adjustment necessary in patients with renal impairment according to available sources. Pharmacokinetics show increased half‑life but overall clearance remains relatively rapid and non‑renal clearance exists.  
- Hepatic impairment: No dosage adjustment necessary in patients with hepatic impairment according to available sources; no clinically significant pharmacokinetic changes reported.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **Goserelin** is a potent synthetic GnRH (LHRH) agonist. Initial administration causes stimulation of luteinizing hormone (LH) release from the pituitary and a transient rise in serum testosterone. Continued (chronic) administration results in down‑regulation of pituitary GnRH receptors, suppression of gonadotropin secretion, reduction of LH and follicle‑stimulating hormone (FSH), and sustained suppression of gonadal steroid production to levels comparable to surgical castration.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Following initiation, a transient testosterone rise occurs (flare), then pituitary LH suppression is achieved within approximately 3 weeks. Sustained testosterone suppression is maintained while therapy continues. Data indicate that in most patients castrate testosterone levels may be sustained for up to 16 weeks after a 3‑month implant, but regular dosing per schedule is recommended.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption/distribution: Implant formulation releases goserelin continuously from a biodegradable matrix after subcutaneous insertion. Peak serum concentrations occur during the first 24 hours after administration; mean concentration at 2 hours post‑administration reported as 8.6 ± 2.9 ng/mL with considerable interindividual variability.  
- Elimination: Serum elimination half‑life reported approximately 4.2 hours for goserelin (compared with ≈13 minutes for endogenous LHRH). Mean systemic clearance reported in the range of ~100–200 mL/min with interindividual variability. Serum concentrations become low by the end of the dosing interval; therefore delays/omissions of scheduled doses can permit testosterone to rise. Non‑renal (presumed hepatic) clearance contributes to elimination.

### Preclinical toxicology
- Genotoxicity: No evidence for mutagenic effects in standard testing.  
- Carcinogenicity: In rats given repeated subcutaneous implants, benign pituitary microadenomas increased; this effect is similar to changes seen after surgical castration in this species and may be species‑specific. In mice at high doses, some increases in certain neoplasms (histiocytic sarcomas) and digestive system changes were reported; clinical relevance to humans is not established.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Multiple randomized controlled trials assessed goserelin in combination with radiotherapy for locally advanced prostate cancer. Key trial findings include:

  - RTOG 85‑31 (n=945; median follow‑up ≈7.6 years): Adjuvant long‑term goserelin plus radiotherapy versus radiotherapy alone (with goserelin at relapse). Ten‑year estimates: overall survival 47% vs 38% (p=0.0043); disease‑free survival 30% vs 9% (p<0.0001).

  - EORTC 22863 (n=415; median follow‑up ≈5.5 years): Adjuvant goserelin plus radiotherapy vs radiotherapy alone. Five‑year estimates: overall survival 78% vs 62% (hazard ratio 0.51 [95% CI 0.36–0.73]); disease‑free survival 74% vs 40% (HR 0.34 [0.26–0.46]).

  - RTOG 86‑10 (n=456; median follow‑up ≈6.7 years): Neoadjuvant goserelin (2 months before and during radiotherapy) plus radiotherapy vs radiotherapy alone. Eight‑year estimates: overall survival 53% vs 44% (p=0.10); disease‑free survival 49% vs 34% (p=0.004).

  - RTOG 92‑02 (n=1514; median follow‑up ≈5.8 years): Neoadjuvant plus adjuvant goserelin plus radiotherapy compared with neoadjuvant goserelin plus radiotherapy only. Five‑year estimates: overall survival not significantly different (≈80.0% vs 78.5%); disease‑free survival improved with combined neo‑ and adjuvant therapy (46% vs 28%, p<0.0001).

  - TROG 96‑01 (n=818; median follow‑up ≈5.9 years): Neoadjuvant goserelin for 3 or 6 months plus radiotherapy vs radiotherapy alone. Five‑year disease‑free survival estimates improved with neoadjuvant therapy (3‑month and 6‑month regimens vs RT alone) with hazard ratios indicating benefit for DFS; no consistent overall survival benefit presented for all comparisons.

- Trial treatment regimens varied; several trials utilized a monthly 3.6 mg subcutaneous formulation of goserelin (administered every 4 weeks) in the protocols referenced. Trial results demonstrate improvements in disease‑free survival with neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant GnRH agonist therapy in combination with radiotherapy; adjuvant long‑term therapy was associated with improvements in overall survival in at least some trials.
