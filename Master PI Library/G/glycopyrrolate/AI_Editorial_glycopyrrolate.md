# Editorial Product Information: glycopyrrolate

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **glycopyrrolate**
- Chemical class: Anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) agent
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous (IV), Intramuscular (IM)

## 2. Regulatory History
- Initial U.S. approval: 1975
- Date of last revision/update of available source PI: Revised 3/2026
- Any withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- Registration/approval dates in other jurisdictions: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Reduction of salivary, tracheobronchial, and pharyngeal secretions; reduction of volume and acidity of gastric secretions; and blockade of cardiac inhibitory reflexes during induction of anesthesia and intubation — adults and pediatric patients.
- Intraoperative use to counteract surgically- or drug-induced or vagal reflex–associated arrhythmias — adults and pediatric patients.
- Protection against peripheral muscarinic effects of cholinergic agents (e.g., neostigmine, pyridostigmine) given to reverse neuromuscular blockade due to non-depolarizing agents — adults and pediatric patients.
- Adjunctive treatment to reduce symptoms of peptic ulcer when rapid anticholinergic effect is desired or oral medication is not tolerated — adults only.
  - Limitation: Not indicated as monotherapy for ulcer healing because healing efficacy has not been established.

(If a given indication differs by jurisdiction, that difference is not specified in the available sources.)

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General
- Inspect parenteral preparation visually for particulate matter or discoloration prior to administration whenever solution and container permit.
- Glycopyrrolate injection may be administered IM or IV without dilution unless otherwise required for administration route.
- Contains benzyl alcohol (preservative); see special-population warnings.

### Recommended doses — Adults
- Preanesthetic (IM): 0.004 mg/kg IM, given 30–60 minutes prior to induction of anesthesia.
- Intraoperative to counteract vagal reflexes/arrhythmias (IV): single dose 0.1 mg IV; repeat as needed at 2–3 minute intervals.
- Reversal of neuromuscular blockade (with anticholinesterases): 0.2 mg glycopyrrolate IV for each 1 mg neostigmine (or for each 5 mg pyridostigmine). To minimize cardiac side effects, the anticholinesterase and glycopyrrolate may be administered simultaneously IV.
- Peptic ulcer (adults): 0.1 mg IV or IM every 4 hours, 3–4 times daily. Where more profound effect is needed, 0.2 mg may be given; frequency should be dictated by response up to a maximum of four times daily. Some patients may require only a single dose.

### Recommended doses — Pediatric patients
- Preanesthetic (IM): 0.004 mg/kg IM, 30–60 minutes prior to induction. Patients <2 years of age may require up to 0.009 mg/kg.
- Intraoperative (IV): 0.004 mg/kg IV, not to exceed 0.1 mg in a single dose; may be repeated every 2–3 minutes as needed.
- Reversal of neuromuscular blockade: same ratio as adults — 0.2 mg IV glycopyrrolate for each 1 mg neostigmine (or for each 5 mg pyridostigmine).
- Peptic ulcer: Not indicated for treatment of peptic ulcer in pediatric patients.

### Preparation and admixture
- Compatible diluents: dextrose 5% and 10% in water, saline, dextrose 5% in sodium chloride 0.45%, sodium chloride 0.9%, and Ringer’s injection.
- Incompatible diluent: lactated Ringer’s solution.
- Reported physical admixture compatibility with numerous injectable agents (listed in source PI); may be administered via tubing of a running normal saline infusion.
- Admixture incompatibilities / physical incompatibility: do not combine in the same vial with agents that raise pH above ~6.0 (e.g., methohexital sodium; chloramphenicol sodium succinate; dimenhydrinate; pentobarbital sodium; thiopental sodium; secobarbital sodium; sodium bicarbonate; diazepam; dexamethasone sodium phosphate; pentazocine lactate) — mixtures may cause gas production or precipitation.

### Administration precautions
- Attempt to determine the cause of intraoperative arrhythmia and correct surgical or anesthetic factors contributing to parasympathetic imbalance.
- Because preanesthetic administration has a long duration of action, additional intraoperative dosing for anticholinergic effect is rarely necessary if premedication given.
- Consider total benzyl alcohol exposure when administering multiple medications containing this preservative.
- Missed dose instructions: Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to glycopyrrolate or any of its inactive ingredients, including benzyl alcohol.
- Peptic ulcer patients with any of the following concurrent conditions:
  - Glaucoma
  - Obstructive uropathy (e.g., bladder neck obstruction due to prostatic hypertrophy)
  - Obstructive disease of the gastrointestinal tract (e.g., achalasia, pyloroduodenal stenosis)
  - Paralytic ileus
  - Intestinal atony of the elderly or debilitated patient
  - Unstable cardiovascular status in acute hemorrhage
  - Severe ulcerative colitis
  - Toxic megacolon complicating ulcerative colitis
  - Myasthenia gravis

(These contraindications are listed in the available source PI.)

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed/Highlighted warnings
- Contains benzyl alcohol: exposure has been associated with toxicity (hypotension, metabolic acidosis) and, in neonates and small preterm infants, with an increased incidence of kernicterus and rare fatal outcomes. Do not use glycopyrrolate injection in neonates (<1 month of age); avoid in small preterm infants. Consider total benzyl alcohol load from all sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Precipitation of acute glaucoma: may cause mydriasis and increased intraocular pressure; advise patients with glaucoma to seek prompt care for symptoms of acute angle closure (ocular pain, redness, dilated pupil).
- Central effects: may cause drowsiness, blurred vision, and light sensitivity; advise caution for activities requiring mental alertness until resolved.
- Heat prostration: anticholinergic effect reduces sweating; risk of heat prostration with fever, high environmental temperature, or exercise — particularly in children and the elderly. Advise avoiding exertion and high temperatures.
- Intestinal obstruction: diarrhea can be an early sign of incomplete intestinal obstruction; use is inappropriate and potentially harmful in patients with diarrhea and ileostomy/colostomy — glycopyrrolate is contraindicated in these situations.
- Tachycardia and cardiovascular effects: may increase heart rate; investigate tachycardia before administration. Use with caution in patients with coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, arrhythmias, hypertension, or hyperthyroidism.
- Renal impairment: renal elimination may be significantly reduced in renal failure; dosage adjustments may be necessary (specific adjustment guidance not provided).
- Autonomic neuropathy / hepatic disease / ulcerative colitis / prostatic hypertrophy / hiatal hernia: use with caution; anticholinergics may aggravate these conditions. Consider dose reduction and close monitoring.
- Delayed gastric emptying/gastric stasis: may occur with anticholinergic therapy; monitor for vomiting, dyspepsia, early satiety, abdominal distension, or worsening abdominal pain; discontinue if symptoms develop or worsen.
- Light sensitivity: patients may experience ocular photosensitivity; protect eyes from bright light after dosing.
- Benzyl alcohol toxicity: see boxed warning; consider cumulative preservative exposure.

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor heart rate and rhythm when administering for reversal of neuromuscular blockade or as intravascular premedication.
- Monitor for signs of heat prostration, urinary retention, decreased bowel motility, worsening ulcerative colitis, or increased intraocular pressure in at-risk patients.
- Monitor renal function in patients with renal impairment and consider dose adjustment.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Concomitant use with other anticholinergics or medications that have anticholinergic activity (e.g., phenothiazines, antiparkinson drugs, tricyclic antidepressants) may potentiate antimuscarinic effects and increase anticholinergic adverse effects.
- Combined administration with anticholinesterase agents (e.g., neostigmine, pyridostigmine) is an intended clinical interaction for reversal of neuromuscular blockade; glycopyrrolate is administered to mitigate peripheral muscarinic effects. Simultaneous IV administration of glycopyrrolate with anticholinesterase is recommended to minimize cardiac side effects. Post-marketing reports associate combined use with heart block and QTc prolongation.

### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Interaction increasing severity of potassium chloride–induced gastrointestinal lesions: concomitant use with potassium chloride in a wax matrix may increase lesion severity due to slowed gastrointestinal transit time.

### Admixture compatibilities / incompatibilities (administration-related)
- Reported physical compatibility for mixing/injection with multiple agents (e.g., atropine sulfate; physostigmine salicylate; diphenhydramine HCl; various opioids and antiemetics; neostigmine; pyridostigmine; scopolamine; etc.) — compatibility list included in source PI.
- Do not combine with agents that raise pH above ~6.0 (see Dosage and Administration — Preparation and admixture) due to risk of precipitation or gas formation.

### Management notes
- When co-administering other anticholinergics, monitor for additive anticholinergic toxicity and consider dose reduction or alternative therapy.
- Account for total benzyl alcohol exposure when multiple preservative-containing products are used.
- Investigate arrhythmias prior to administering glycopyrrolate and monitor cardiac status during and after administration when used intraoperatively or for reversal.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Risk summary: Limited human data from decades of use do not identify a drug-associated risk of birth defects or miscarriage; most reported exposures occurred after first trimester (e.g., during Cesarean section) and have not identified adverse maternal outcomes or differences in infant Apgar scores. Glycopyrrolate concentrations in umbilical venous and arterial blood and in amniotic fluid are reported to be low after intramuscular administration to parturients, suggesting limited placental transfer in that setting.
- Animal data: Reproduction studies showed no teratogenic effects in rats (dosed orally) at exposures approximately 320 times the human maximum recommended daily dose (MRHD) on a mg/m2 basis and in rabbits (IM dosing) at exposures approximately 5 times the MRHD on a mg/m2 basis. A preclinical rat study reported decreased conception rates and decreased survival at weaning in reproductive performance testing.
- Studies during organogenesis in humans are not available; therefore risk for first-trimester exposure and miscarriage remains uncertain.
- Jurisdictional pregnancy categories (e.g., Australian pregnancy categories, US lettered categories): Not specified in available sources.

### Breastfeeding
- Data are lacking on presence of glycopyrrolate in human milk or effects on the breastfed infant or on milk production.
- Glycopyrrolate (as other anticholinergics) may suppress lactation.
- Consider the benefits of breastfeeding and the mother’s clinical need for glycopyrrolate when making treatment decisions.

### Fertility
- Animal data indicate decreased rate of conception and reduced pup survival at weaning in one rat reproductive performance study.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Indications: reduction of secretions and blockade of cardiac inhibitory reflexes for induction/intubation; intraoperative use for vagal reflex-associated arrhythmias; protection against peripheral muscarinic effects of anticholinesterases used to reverse neuromuscular blockade.
- Dosing: See Section 4 (Dosage and Administration) for pediatric dosing specifics (preanesthetic 0.004 mg/kg IM; patients <2 years may require up to 0.009 mg/kg; intraoperative 0.004 mg/kg IV not to exceed 0.1 mg; reversal 0.2 mg per 1 mg neostigmine).
- Increased sensitivity: infants, patients with Down syndrome, and pediatric patients with spastic paralysis or brain damage may have increased responses to anticholinergics, increasing potential for adverse effects. Large doses may provoke hyperexcitability (paradoxical reaction).
- Neonates: Glycopyrrolate injection contains benzyl alcohol and should not be used in neonates (<1 month) and should be avoided in small preterm infants due to risk of benzyl alcohol toxicity.

### Elderly
- Use with caution in elderly patients; anticholinergic agents can aggravate conditions such as intestinal atony and cause confusion or excitement. Consider dose reduction and close monitoring.
- Intestinal atony of the elderly is a contraindicated condition when present with peptic ulcer.

### Renal impairment
- Renal elimination may be significantly reduced in patients with renal failure; dosage adjustments may be necessary. Specific eGFR- or creatinine-based dosing recommendations are not provided in the available sources.

### Hepatic impairment
- Use with caution in patients with hepatic disease; specific dose adjustments by severity are not provided in the available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Glycopyrrolate is an antimuscarinic (anticholinergic) agent that blocks peripheral muscarinic receptors, reducing secretions (salivary, tracheobronchial, pharyngeal), decreasing gastric acid and volume, and antagonizing peripheral muscarinic effects of cholinergic agents.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Not specified in available sources.
- Distribution: Placental transfer appears limited in parturients after IM administration (low umbilical blood and amniotic fluid concentrations reported).
- Metabolism: Not specified in available sources.
- Elimination: Renal elimination is an important route; clearance may be significantly reduced in renal impairment.
- Half-life, time to peak concentration, and other PK parameters: Not specified in available sources.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Antimuscarinic effects include decreased secretions, reduced gastrointestinal motility (antral stasis), pupil dilation (mydriasis), increased intraocular tension, tachycardia, decreased sweating, and reduced bladder tone.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Human trial data in pregnancy: Published randomized controlled trials comparing glycopyrrolate with other antimuscarinic agents during Cesarean section have not identified adverse maternal or infant outcomes; glycopyrrolate at standard doses (0.004 mg/kg) did not appear to significantly affect fetal heart rate or variability.
- Pivotal efficacy trials, primary endpoints, and detailed trial design information for other indications (e.g., preanesthetic reduction of secretions, intraoperative arrhythmia management, reversal of neuromuscular blockade) are not described in detail in the available source PI.
- Post-marketing experience includes reports of serious adverse events such as malignant hyperthermia, cardiac arrhythmias including cardiac arrest, seizures, respiratory arrest, and injection-site reactions; because post-marketing reports are voluntary, incidence estimates are not available.

(If additional pivotal trial summaries or trial designs are required, these are not specified in the available sources.)
