# Editorial Product Information: Glibenclamide

## Editorial Note
- **Contributing Jurisdictions:** This editorial synthesis is based on official Product Information from Australia (Therapeutic Goods Administration).
- **Purpose:** This document provides a harmonised editorial overview of glibenclamide for healthcare professionals, synthesising clinical, pharmacological, and regulatory data.
- **Disclaimer:** This is an editorial synthesis and does not serve as a substitute for the official Product Information (PI) required for prescribing in any specific jurisdiction. Clinicians should consult the current local PI for definitive prescribing requirements.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Glibenclamide (a second-generation sulphonylurea).
- **Brand Names:** Daonil [Australian].
- **ATC Code:** A10BB01.
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - Uncoated 5 mg tablets (oblong, white, scored on both sides) [Australian].
- **Route of Administration:** Oral.
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** sanofi-aventis australia pty ltd [Australian].

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM):** Indicated as an adjunct to diet to lower blood glucose in patients with non-insulin-dependent diabetes whose hyperglycaemia is not controlled by dietary measures alone. It may be used in patients who have failed to respond to other oral antidiabetics [Australian].

## 3. Dosage and Administration
### General Dosing (Adult)
- **Initial Dose:** Stabilisation should commence with 2.5 mg daily, taken immediately before breakfast.
- **Maintenance:** If control is achieved after 3–5 days, 2.5 mg is continued as the maintenance dose.
- **Titration:** If control is unsatisfactory, the dose may be increased in 2.5 mg increments at 7-day intervals.
- **Maximum Dose:** The standard maximum is 15 mg daily. In exceptional cases, up to 20 mg daily may be used [Australian].

### Administration Instructions
- Tablets must be taken immediately before a meal (usually breakfast).
- If only a light breakfast is consumed, the dose should be deferred until lunchtime.
- **Single vs. Split Dosing:** Daily doses up to 10 mg may be taken as a single dose before breakfast. Doses exceeding 10 mg should be split, with the remainder taken before the evening meal [Australian].

### Special Instructions
- **Transfer from other oral antidiabetics:** Start with a usual dose of 2.5 mg to 5 mg daily. A drug-free transition period may be necessary depending on the half-life of the previous medication to avoid cumulative hypoglycaemic effects.
- **Transfer from Insulin:**
    - If previous insulin dose <20 units: 2.5–5 mg glibenclamide; stop insulin abruptly.
    - If previous insulin dose 20–40 units: 5 mg glibenclamide; stop insulin abruptly.
    - If previous insulin dose >40 units: 5 mg glibenclamide; reduce insulin by 50% and taper gradually while up-titrating glibenclamide [Australian].
- **Missed Dose:** Patients must not correct a missed dose by taking a larger dose later [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
1. Known hypersensitivity to glibenclamide or excipients (e.g., lactose).
2. Type 1 diabetes mellitus (insulin-dependent) or diabetes complicated by ketosis.
3. Diabetic ketoacidosis.
4. Serious metabolic decompensation, including precoma and coma.
5. Severe renal impairment.
6. Severe hepatic dysfunction.
7. Pregnancy and lactation.
8. Concomitant treatment with **bosentan** [Australian].

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
### General Warnings
- **Hypoglycaemia:** Severe, prolonged, and potentially lethal hypoglycaemia can occur. This requires immediate carbohydrate intake and may require hospitalisation.
- **Cardiovascular Mortality:** Some epidemiological studies suggest glibenclamide is associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular mortality compared to metformin or gliclazide, particularly in patients with known coronary artery disease [Australian].
- **G6PD Deficiency:** Use of sulphonylureas in patients with G6PD deficiency can lead to haemolytic anaemia; non-sulphonylurea alternatives should be considered [Australian].

### Precautions
- **Alertness:** Impaired glucose regulation or switching treatments may affect the ability to drive or operate machinery.
- **Stress Situations:** Trauma, surgery, or infection may lead to loss of glycaemic control, necessitating a temporary switch to insulin.
- **Cross-sensitivity:** Potential for cross-sensitivity with other sulphonamides and their derivatives [Australian].

### Monitoring
- Regular blood and urine glucose determinations are mandatory.
- Glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is recommended for long-term monitoring.
- Monitor for "primary failure" (initial lack of efficacy) or "secondary failure" (loss of control after an initial period of effectiveness) [Australian].

## 6. Adverse Effects
- **Hypoglycaemia:** Common and potentially severe; symptoms may resemble a stroke [Australian].
- **Gastrointestinal (1–2%):** Nausea, vomiting, epigastric fullness, abdominal pain, anorexia, and diarrhoea. These are often dose-related. Rare reports of pancreatitis [Australian].
- **Dermatologic (1.5%):** Pruritus, erythema, urticaria, and maculo-papular or bullous eruptions. Rare cases of allergic vasculitis, photosensitivity, and life-threatening shock [Australian].
- **Haematologic (Rare):** Anaemia, leukopaenia, thrombocytopaenia (presenting as purpura), agranulocytosis, and pancytopaenia. Usually reversible upon withdrawal [Australian].
- **Hepatic (Isolated cases):** Elevated liver enzymes, cholestasis, hepatitis, and jaundice; may progress to liver failure [Australian].
- **Ocular:** Temporary visual impairment at the start of treatment due to changes in lens turgidity [Australian].

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Potentiation of Hypoglycaemic Effect
- **Contraindicated:** Bosentan (increased liver enzymes due to inhibition of the bile salt export pump).
- **Pharmacodynamic/Pharmacokinetic:** Insulin, other oral antidiabetics, ACE inhibitors, anabolic steroids, alcohol (acute/chronic), beta-blockers, biguanides, chloramphenicol, clarithromycin, coumarin derivatives, MAO-inhibitors, miconazole, phenylbutazone, quinolones, salicylates, and tetracyclines.
- **Displacement from protein binding:** NSAIDs, oral anticoagulants, and hydantoins [Australian].

### Attenuation of Hypoglycaemic Effect
- Adrenaline, corticosteroids, diuretics (thiazides/loop), glucagon, oestrogens/progestogens, phenothiazines, phenytoin, rifampicin, and thyroid hormones [Australian].

### Other Significant Interactions
- **Colesevelam:** Binds glibenclamide; administer glibenclamide at least 4 hours prior to colesevelam.
- **Ciclosporin:** Glibenclamide may increase ciclosporin plasma levels; monitoring is required.
- **Alcohol:** May lead to disulfiram-like reactions [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** **Category C** [Australian]. Sulphonylureas may enter foetal circulation and cause neonatal hypoglycaemia. Animal studies indicate embryotoxicity. Glibenclamide must be discontinued and replaced with insulin prior to or upon confirmation of pregnancy.
- **Breastfeeding:** Glibenclamide must not be taken by breastfeeding women to prevent potential ingestion by the infant. Patients should switch to insulin or cease breastfeeding [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Safety and efficacy have not been established; not recommended for children [Australian].
- **Elderly:** Increased susceptibility to the hypoglycaemic action of glibenclamide. Dosing should be conservative, as hypoglycaemia can be difficult to recognise in this population [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Contraindicated in severe renal impairment. In mild-to-moderate cases, careful monitoring is required due to increased risk of hypoglycaemia [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Contraindicated in severe dysfunction. Hepatic insufficiency diminishes gluconeogenic capacity, increasing the risk of severe hypoglycaemic reactions [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Stimulates insulin release from pancreatic beta cells (insulin secretagogue). It increases the sensitivity of beta cells to physiological glucose stimulus. Chronic use may also involve extrapancreatic effects, including increased peripheral insulin sensitivity and reduced hepatic glucose production [Australian].
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption:** Nearly complete (84 ± 9%). Peak serum concentrations occur in 2–6 hours. Not affected by food.
    - **Distribution:** 99% protein bound. No significant tissue build-up.
    - **Metabolism:** Completely metabolised in the liver (principally via CYP2C9 and CYP3A4) to weakly active metabolites.
    - **Elimination:** Biphasic; terminal half-life is 2–5 hours (up to 8–10 hours in diabetic patients). Excreted 50% in bile and 50% in urine [Australian].
