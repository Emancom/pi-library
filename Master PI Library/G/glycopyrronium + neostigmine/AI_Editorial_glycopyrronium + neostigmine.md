# Editorial Product Information: glycopyrronium + neostigmine

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **glycopyrronium + neostigmine**
- Chemical class:
  - Glycopyrronium: quaternary ammonium antimuscarinic (anticholinergic) agent.
  - Neostigmine: reversible anticholinesterase (antimuscarinic-inhibitor / cholinesterase inhibitor).
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources.
- Route(s) of administration: Intravenous administration only.
- DO NOT include brand or trade names, dosage form physical descriptions, or PBS/schedule listings.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Reversal of residual non-depolarising (competitive) neuromuscular block (general approved indication as described).  
- If different ratios than the fixed combination are required, prepare using monotherapy products (i.e., alternative ratios are not provided by this fixed product).  
- Regional variations in approved indications: Not specified in available sources beyond the general indication above.

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General adult dosing
- Fixed-concentration product contains a glycopyrronium:neostigmine ratio of 0.5 mg : 2.5 mg per mL (1:5).
- Adult dose guidance: 0.2 mg glycopyrronium per 1 mg neostigmine intravenously.  
  - Equivalently: 0.016 mL/kg IV (equivalent to 0.008 mg/kg glycopyrronium and 0.04 mg/kg neostigmine).

### Paediatric dosing (1 month to 12 years)
- Dose: 0.016 mL/kg IV (equivalent to glycopyrronium 0.008 mg/kg and neostigmine 0.04 mg/kg).

### Maximum / total dose cautions
- Total doses in excess of 2 mL are not recommended because higher neostigmine doses may produce depolarising neuromuscular block.

### Administration instructions
- For intravenous use only. The solution is preservative-free and intended for single patient, single use; discard any residue.
- Administer when the first twitch response is substantially greater than 10% of baseline, or when a second twitch is present on train-of-four stimulation.
- Do not initiate antagonism of neuromuscular blockade with a cholinesterase inhibitor until at least two to four responses to train‑of‑four stimulation are observed.
- Adequacy of reversal must be based on clinical assessment and not on train‑of‑four responses alone unless quantitative numeric neuromuscular monitoring is available.
- The product should be used as supplied and should not be further diluted prior to use.
- Re-administration of muscle relaxant shortly after use of this combination requires caution because increased acetylcholine levels and residual neostigmine may cause altered response to further muscle relaxants (possible resistance to relaxation and prolonged recovery).
- Administration of sugammadex after neostigmine reversal may increase muscle relaxation because sugammadex removes remaining relaxant and reveals the neuromuscular inhibition attributable to neostigmine.

## 5. Contraindications
List of contraindications (include all stated across sources):
- Hypersensitivity to glycopyrronium, neostigmine, or any excipients listed for the product.
- Mechanical obstruction of the intestinal tract (e.g., paralytic ileus, intestinal atony or mechanical gastrointestinal tract obstruction).
- Mechanical obstruction of the urinary tract (e.g., obstructive uropathy, prostatic hypertrophy, recent bladder surgery).
- Concomitant administration with suxamethonium (suxamethonium/succinylcholine) — neostigmine potentiates depolarising neuromuscular block and may prolong apnoea.
- Any other contraindication: Not specified in available sources.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed warnings
- No boxed warnings specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- **Neuromuscular monitoring** must be available for every patient who has had neuromuscular blockade induced and should be used whenever the anaesthetist is considering extubation after non-depolarising neuromuscular blockade.
- Do not initiate cholinesterase antagonist reversal before observing at least two to four responses to train‑of‑four stimulation.
- Re-administration of muscle relaxant soon after this combination warrants caution (possible resistance to muscle relaxants and prolonged neuromuscular recovery).
- Administration of sugammadex after neostigmine reversal can increase muscle relaxation; be aware of possible increased neuromuscular block.
- Neostigmine may prolong the depolarising block of agents such as suxamethonium; prolonged apnoea may occur unless ventilation is maintained.
- Use cautiously in patients with bronchospasm; while neostigmine alone can cause bronchoconstriction, the combination with glycopyrronium tends to bronchodilate, but secretions may be thickened.
- Use with caution in patients with severe bradycardia; further bradycardia may occur.
- Anticholinergic drugs can precipitate ventricular arrhythmias when administered during inhalational anaesthesia, particularly with halogenated agents.
- Glycopyrronium, being quaternary, has limited blood–brain barrier penetration and is less likely to cause postoperative confusion than tertiary antimuscarinics—nevertheless, caution in elderly due to risk of confusion.
- Use cautiously (if at all) in patients with glaucoma—reported increases in intraocular pressure.
- Anticholinesterase administration to patients with recent intestinal anastomosis may cause rupture or leakage.
- Use with caution in patients with unstable cardiovascular status (autonomic neuropathy, arrhythmias, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, hypertension, hyperthyroidism, recent myocardial infarction or coronary occlusion, hypotension).
- Hiatus hernia associated reflux may be aggravated after administration.
- Large doses of quaternary anticholinergics can block end-plate nicotinic receptors—use caution in myasthenia gravis.
- Glycopyrronium can reduce sweating and risk heat prostration in fever/high ambient temperature—use very cautiously in febrile patients and those at risk of heat stroke (children, elderly).
- Use neostigmine with caution in patients with epilepsy, vagotonia, Parkinson’s disease, or Addison’s disease.
- Both active substances are renally excreted; dosage adjustment may be needed in renal impairment and duration of effect may be prolonged.
- Elderly: start at the low end of dosing range and exercise caution because of decreased hepatic/renal/cardiac function and comorbidities.

### Monitoring requirements
- Neuromuscular function monitoring is mandatory when neuromuscular blockade has been used.
- Monitor for clinical signs of residual blockade after anaesthesia and extubation: difficulty maintaining airway, generalized weakness, inadequate ventilatory effort.
- Monitor heart rate and rhythm—be vigilant for bradycardia, tachycardia, arrhythmias.
- Monitor for signs of cholinergic crisis (in neostigmine overdosage) and anticholinergic toxicity (with glycopyrronium excess).
- Monitor renal function when appropriate; both drugs are principally renally excreted.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Pediatric patients: anticholinergic agents may cause paradoxical hyperexcitability; infants, young children, Down syndrome patients, and those with spastic paralysis or brain damage may have increased sensitivity to anticholinergics.
- Patients who have undergone recent intestinal or bladder surgery: use with caution.
- Patients taking multiple antimuscarinic agents may have excessive antimuscarinic effects.
- Concomitant use with corticosteroids may alter anticholinesterase effects (corticosteroids may decrease anticholinesterase effect; anticholinesterase effects may increase after stopping corticosteroids).

### Summary of key serious adverse events (highlighted)
- Anaphylaxis / severe hypersensitivity / angioedema.
- Cardiac arrest, severe arrhythmias (ventricular fibrillation), heart block.
- Respiratory arrest / severe respiratory depression.
- Prolonged or worsened neuromuscular blockade, cholinergic crisis (neostigmine overdose).
- Bronchospasm (neostigmine-associated).
- GI anastomosis rupture/leak (after anticholinesterase in patients with intestinal anastomosis).

## 8. Drug Interactions

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Antimuscarinic additive effects: increased risk of antimuscarinic/anticholinergic adverse effects when combined with other antimuscarinic agents (belladonna alkaloids, synthetic anticholinergics, antiparkinsonism agents), phenothiazines, tricyclic antidepressants, some antihistamines, disopyramide, procainamide, quinidine, narcotic analgesics (pethidine), thioxanthenes, butyrophenones, amantadine, clozapine and nefopam.
- Anticholinergic + corticosteroids: concurrent administration may increase intraocular pressure.
- Anticholinesterase + depolarising muscle relaxants (suxamethonium): neostigmine may prolong Phase I block — do not give concomitantly; contraindicated with suxamethonium.
- Aminoglycosides, local and some general anaesthetics, and antiarrhythmic agents may interfere with neuromuscular transmission; these may affect the effectiveness of reversal and neostigmine dose may need to be increased accordingly.
- Quinine, chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, beta-blockers and lithium may reduce the effectiveness of neostigmine therapy.
- QT interval: In 10 healthy adults anaesthetised with propofol (no muscle relaxants), 30 minutes after neostigmine 50 µg/kg plus glycopyrronium 10 µg/kg, mean QTcB prolongation was 5.3 ms; small changes in QTc reported — monitor in patients at risk of QT prolongation.

### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Corticosteroids may decrease anticholinesterase effects of neostigmine; anticholinesterase effects may increase after stopping corticosteroids.
- Specific cytochrome P450 or transporter-based pharmacokinetic interactions: Not specified in available sources.

### Clinically significant interactions and management
- Avoid suxamethonium/suxamethonium co-administration.
- Exercise caution or avoid combining with other antimuscarinic drugs due to additive anticholinergic side effects; monitor for anticholinergic toxicity.
- When co-administering with drugs that impair neuromuscular transmission (aminoglycosides, certain anesthetics, antiarrhythmics), monitor neuromuscular function and be prepared to adjust neostigmine dosing or provide ventilatory support.
- If corticosteroids are started or stopped, anticipate potential changes in neostigmine efficacy.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

### Pregnancy
- Glycopyrronium: "Clinically the safe use ... has not been established." Animal reproduction studies did not reveal teratogenic effects; diminished conception and pup survival in rats at high doses. Recommendation: do not use in pregnant women or those likely to become pregnant unless expected benefits justify potential risk. [Australian: Pregnancy Category B2 stated in source]
- Neostigmine: Safety in pregnancy not established; anticholinesterase agents may cause uterine irritability and may induce premature labour if given IV near term. Use only if expected benefits outweigh potential risks.
- Overall pregnancy guidance: Avoid unless benefit justifies potential risk.
- If different regional pregnancy categories exist: Not specified in available sources beyond the Australian Category B2 notation.

### Breastfeeding
- Glycopyrronium: Unknown whether excreted in human milk; anticholinergics may suppress lactation. Product not recommended for nursing mothers unless benefits outweigh risks.
- Neostigmine: Evidence indicates only negligible amounts enter breast milk; nevertheless, possibility of adverse effects on the breast‑fed infant should be considered. Product not recommended for nursing mothers unless benefits outweigh risks.

### Fertility
- Effects on fertility: No human data available. Animal studies show diminished rates of conception and survival in rats; high‑dose effects on seminal secretion in dogs. Clinical significance for humans unclear.

## 10. Use in Special Populations

### Paediatric use
- Dosing provided for children 1 month to 12 years: 0.016 mL/kg IV (glycopyrronium 0.008 mg/kg and neostigmine 0.04 mg/kg).
- Paradoxical hyperexcitability may occur in paediatric patients taking large doses of anticholinergics. Infants, young children, Down’s syndrome patients, and children with spastic paralysis or brain damage may have increased sensitivity to anticholinergics and greater potential for side effects.

### Elderly use
- Dose selection should be cautious; start at low end of dosing range owing to higher prevalence of reduced organ function and comorbidities.
- In a study of patients >65 years receiving neostigmine 50 µg/kg + glycopyrronium 10 µg/kg, initial heart rate increase was higher and subsequent falls less; incidence of cardiac dysrhythmias was higher compared with younger healthy adults. Monitor cardiac status closely.

### Renal impairment
- Glycopyrronium and neostigmine are excreted mostly in urine; duration of effect may be prolonged in renal impairment. Dosage adjustment may be necessary for patients with renal failure. Use with caution.

### Hepatic impairment
- Not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action
- Neostigmine: reversible inhibitor of acetylcholinesterase; increases acetylcholine at cholinergic synapses, augmenting transmission at the neuromuscular junction and at muscarinic receptors — reverses non-depolarising neuromuscular blockade.
- Glycopyrronium: antimuscarinic that blocks peripheral muscarinic receptors, reducing salivary and bronchial secretions and counteracting muscarinic side effects (e.g., bradycardia) of anticholinesterase therapy. Being a quaternary ammonium compound, it has limited central nervous system penetration.

### Pharmacodynamics
- The combination reverses non-depolarising neuromuscular blockade by increasing acetylcholine (neostigmine) while glycopyrronium mitigates muscarinic effects (e.g., bradycardia, bronchoconstriction).
- Neostigmine can cause dose-related neuromuscular weakness with features of phase‑1 depolarising blockade at high doses (fasciculations, decreased twitch height, no fade in TOF).
- Recovery times (geometric mean T2 to TOFR > 0.9):
  - After rocuronium: 17.0 min (95% CI 11.6–24.8).
  - After vecuronium: 17.9 min (95% CI 13.1–24.3).
  - After rocuronium in Korean subjects: 14.8 min (95% CI 12.4–17.6).
- In spinal injured patients, net effect assessed was bronchodilation.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Excretion: Both glycopyrronium and neostigmine are excreted mostly in the urine; duration may be prolonged in renal impairment.
- Distribution: Glycopyrronium is quaternary and does not readily cross the blood–brain barrier.
- Absorption, metabolism, half-life values, protein binding, and detailed PK parameters: Not specified in available sources.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Volunteer study (n=14): Healthy volunteers received neostigmine 2.5 mg with glycopyrronium 450 µg; 9 of 14 received repeat dose after 15 minutes. Reported adverse events in the 14 volunteers: muscle weakness (14/14, 100%), blurred vision (13/14, 93%), abdominal cramping (13/14, 93%), dry mouth (11/14, 79%), nausea (11/14, 79%), vomiting (1/14, 7%). These effects were not seen in 7 saline placebo subjects (source reports).
- Pooled literature data (5 trials, n=225) evaluating reversal of muscle relaxants with glycopyrronium + neostigmine reported adverse event frequencies including: nausea 7.6% (17/225), dry mouth 3.1% (7/225), abdominal pain 0.9% (2/225), vomiting 0.9% (2/225), and various cardiac rhythm disturbances and laboratory findings at low frequencies. Full breakdown of these data is presented in the adverse effects table above.
- Elderly cardiac effects: a study of 59 patients >65 years (32 with cardiovascular disease) receiving neostigmine 50 µg/kg with glycopyrronium 10 µg/kg showed a greater initial increase in heart rate, smaller subsequent falls, and higher incidence of dysrhythmias compared with earlier studies in healthy adults.
- Pivotal trial designs, primary endpoints, sample sizes beyond the above summaries and long-term outcome studies: Not specified in available sources.
