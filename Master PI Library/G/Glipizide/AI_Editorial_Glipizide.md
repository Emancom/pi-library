# Editorial Product Information: Glipizide

## Editorial Note
- This editorial document has been developed using Product Information (PI) provided by the following regulatory authority: **Australian (TGA)**.
- The purpose of this document is to provide a harmonised editorial overview of glipizide for use by healthcare professionals, synthesising clinical data into a coherent reference.
- **Disclaimer:** This document is an editorial synthesis and serves as a clinical resource. It is not a substitute for the official, jurisdiction-specific Product Information required for prescribing within any particular region.

## 1. Product Identification
- **Active Ingredient:** Glipizide
- **Chemical Class:** Sulfonylurea (second-generation)
- **Brand Names:** **Minidiab** [Australian]
- **ATC Code:** A10BB07
- **Dosage Forms and Strengths:** 
    - 5 mg tablets: White, round, biconvex, scored [Australian]
- **Route of Administration:** Oral
- **Manufacturer/Sponsor:** Pfizer Australia Pty Ltd [Australian]

## 2. Approved Indications
- **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (NIDDM):** Indicated as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycaemic control in adults with non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus where dietary measures alone have proven insufficient [Australian].
- **Note:** Glipizide should be viewed as a supplement to, not a replacement for, proper dietary management and physical activity.

## 3. Dosage and Administration
- **General Administration:** Tablets should be taken approximately 30 minutes before a meal to maximise the reduction of postprandial hyperglycaemia [Australian].
- **Adult Dosing (Initial):** The recommended starting dose is 5 mg daily, administered before breakfast.
- **Dosing in Special Populations (Initial):** Geriatric patients or those with hepatic impairment should initiate therapy at 2.5 mg daily [Australian].
- **Titration:** Adjustments should occur in increments of 2.5 mg to 5 mg based on blood glucose response. Several days should elapse between titration steps.
- **Maintenance:**
    - Some patients may be controlled on once-daily dosing.
    - **Maximum single dose:** 15 mg.
    - **Divided doses:** Daily requirements exceeding 15 mg should be divided and administered before meals of adequate caloric content.
    - **Maximum daily dose:** 40 mg [Australian].
- **Transfer from Other Agents:** 
    - **From other sulfonylureas:** No transition period is generally required, though patients should be monitored for 1–2 weeks for overlapping effects when transferring from long-acting agents like chlorpropamide.
    - **From insulin:** Stable Type 2 diabetic patients receiving insulin may be transitioned to glipizide under medical supervision [Australian].

## 4. Contraindications
Glipizide is contraindicated in the following scenarios [Australian]:
- Known hypersensitivity to glipizide or other sulfonylurea derivatives.
- Known allergy to sulfonamides.
- Diabetic ketoacidosis (with or without coma); this must be managed with insulin.
- Juvenile-onset, growth-onset, or brittle diabetes mellitus.
- Severe renal or hepatic insufficiency.
- Severe thyroid dysfunction.
- **Pregnancy.**
- Clinical states involving severe metabolic stress: infections, febrile conditions, gangrene, severe trauma, or major surgical procedures.
- **Paediatric use:** Contraindicated in children.

## 5. Warnings and Precautions
- **Boxed Warnings:** None specified in provided sources.
- **G6PD Deficiency:** Use with caution; sulfonylureas can induce haemolytic anaemia in patients with G6PD deficiency. Non-sulfonylurea alternatives should be considered [Australian].
- **Hypoglycaemia:** All sulfonylureas can cause severe hypoglycaemia. Risk is increased by renal/hepatic impairment, malnutrition, strenuous exercise, alcohol consumption, or multi-drug glucose-lowering regimens. Adrenal or pituitary insufficiency increases sensitivity.
- **Beta-Blockers:** Hypoglycaemia may be harder to identify in patients taking beta-adrenergic blocking agents.
- **Loss of Control:** In patients stabilised on a diabetic regimen, exposure to stress (fever, trauma, infection, surgery) may lead to a loss of glycaemic control, requiring temporary insulin administration.
- **Secondary Failure:** The efficacy of oral hypoglycaemics may diminish over time due to disease progression or reduced drug responsiveness.
- **Monitoring:** Periodic monitoring of blood glucose and glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is essential.

## 6. Adverse Effects
Reported adverse effects are generally low in frequency (11.8% of patients in controlled trials) [Australian].

| System Organ Class | Frequency | Adverse Effect Details |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Common (1-10%) | Nausea and diarrhoea (1.4%), constipation (1%), gastralgia (1%), vomiting (>1%). |
| **Dermatologic** | Common (1-10%) | Rash, erythema, urticaria, pruritus (approx. 1 in 70 patients). Rare: Photosensitivity, porphyria cutanea tarda. |
| **Nervous System** | Common (1-10%) | Dizziness, drowsiness, headache (approx. 1 in 50 patients). |
| **Hepatobiliary** | Rare (<0.1%) | Cholestatic jaundice (discontinue if this occurs), hepatitis, impaired hepatic function. |
| **Haematologic** | Rare (<0.1%) | Leucopenia, agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, aplastic anaemia, haemolytic anaemia, pancytopenia. |
| **Endocrine/Metabolic** | Rare (<0.1%) | Hyponatraemia, SIADH, disulfiram-like reactions. |
| **Ocular** | Uncommon | Blurred vision, diplopia, visual impairment (often transient/signs of hypoglycaemia). |

## 7. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacodynamic Interactions (Increased Hypoglycaemic Effect)
- **Potentiating Agents:** NSAIDs, quinolone antibiotics, salicylates, sulfonamides, clofibrate, biguanides, MAOIs, and beta-adrenergic blockers.
- **Antifungals:** Fluconazole and voriconazole may increase glipizide plasma levels/half-life, leading to hypoglycaemia.
- **ACE Inhibitors:** May increase hypoglycaemic effects; dosage reduction of glipizide may be necessary.
- **H2 Receptor Antagonists:** (e.g., cimetidine) may potentiate effects.
- **Alcohol:** May increase hypoglycaemic risk and potentially lead to hypoglycaemic coma.

### Pharmacodynamic Interactions (Decreased Hypoglycaemic Effect)
- **Hyperglycaemic Agents:** Thiazides and other diuretics, corticosteroids, phenothiazines, thyroid products, oestrogens, oral contraceptives, phenytoin, nicotinic acid, sympathomimetics, and isoniazid.

### Other Interactions
- **Miconazole:** Oral miconazole significantly increases hypoglycaemic risk.
- **Tetracycline:** May interfere with urine glucose testing.
- **Barbiturates:** Low risk of interaction due to non-ionic protein binding [Australian].

## 8. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- **Pregnancy:** 
    - **Category C** [Australian]: Glipizide was found to be mildly fetotoxic in rat studies. Sulfonylureas are generally considered unsuitable for pregnancy due to significant metabolic changes; insulin is the preferred agent.
- **Breastfeeding:** Safety is not established. While some sulfonylureas are excreted in breast milk, it is unknown if glipizide is present. A decision should be made to discontinue nursing or the drug, considering the risk of neonatal hypoglycaemia [Australian].
- **Fertility:** No data available [Australian].

## 9. Use in Special Populations
- **Paediatric Use:** Contraindicated in children [Australian].
- **Elderly Use:** Increased sensitivity to hypoglycaemic effects. Dosing must be conservative (starting at 2.5 mg) with careful monitoring [Australian].
- **Renal Impairment:** Metabolism and excretion may be slowed. Conservative dosing is required; contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency [Australian].
- **Hepatic Impairment:** Clearance is reduced and gluconeogenic capacity may be diminished, increasing the risk of prolonged hypoglycaemia. Start at 2.5 mg; contraindicated in severe hepatic insufficiency [Australian].

## 10. Pharmacology
- **Mechanism of Action:** Glipizide stimulates insulin secretion from the beta cells of the pancreatic islets. Its effectiveness is dependent upon functioning beta cells. It may also possess extrapancreatic effects (e.g., improved peripheral insulin sensitivity).
- **Pharmacodynamics:** 
    - **Onset:** Insulinotropic response occurs within 30 minutes of dosing.
    - **Duration:** Blood glucose control may persist for up to 24 hours.
- **Pharmacokinetics:**
    - **Absorption:** Rapid and complete gastrointestinal absorption. Food delays absorption by ~40 minutes but does not affect total bioavailability.
    - **Distribution:** 92–99% protein-bound. Volume of distribution is 5–11 L.
    - **Metabolism:** Extensively metabolised by the liver into inactive hydroxylation products.
    - **Elimination:** Primary excretion via urine (predominantly as metabolites; <4.3% unchanged). Elimination half-life is 2–4 hours.
