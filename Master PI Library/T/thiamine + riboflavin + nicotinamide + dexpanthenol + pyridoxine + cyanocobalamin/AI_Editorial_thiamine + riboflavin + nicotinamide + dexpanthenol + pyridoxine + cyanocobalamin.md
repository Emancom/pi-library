# Editorial Product Information: thiamine + riboflavin + nicotinamide + dexpanthenol + pyridoxine + cyanocobalamin

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **thiamine + riboflavin + nicotinamide + dexpanthenol + pyridoxine + cyanocobalamin**
- Chemical class: Water‑soluble B‑vitamin complex (B‑group vitamins)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Parenteral — intramuscular (recommended deep intragluteal IM) and intravenous (only as a slow diluted infusion with appropriate precautions)

## 3. Approved Indications
- Correction of B‑vitamin deficiencies when oral administration is insufficient or inappropriate (examples given):
  - When rapid saturation of B‑group vitamins is required
  - When oral administration is not feasible or appropriate
  - Alcoholic, debilitated and elderly patients with inadequate diet
  - Recovery from beriberi and Wernicke’s syndrome due to vitamin B1 deficiency  
    - Note: This preparation does not contain sufficient thiamine for acute treatment of Wernicke’s syndrome (caution).
  - Pellagra (vitamin B3 deficiency); may be used when multiple concurrent B‑vitamin deficiencies exist
  - Peripheral neuritis caused by various B‑group deficiencies
  - Pernicious anaemia due to in situ B12 deficiency
  - Angular stomatitis and glossitis in some instances respond to riboflavin or pyridoxine
  - General energy depletion

(Indications presented above reflect the source product information and are intended for use by clinicians to treat B‑vitamin deficiencies when parenteral therapy is indicated.)

## 4. Dosage and Administration

### General dosing (adult)
- Content per 2 mL vial: thiamine hydrochloride 20 mg; riboflavine sodium phosphate 5 mg; nicotinamide 50 mg; dexpanthenol 5 mg; pyridoxine hydrochloride 10 mg; cyanocobalamin 1 mg.
- Typical regimen (general): slow intramuscular (deep intragluteal) injection once weekly for 2–6 weeks or as determined by the treating physician.
- Severe cases: one vial daily until acute symptoms subside.

### Dosing by indication
- Not further specified by individual indication in available sources beyond the general and severe‑case regimens above.
- Note: This product is described as suitable for patients with mild symptoms or those who may not tolerate higher doses of B vitamins. For severe/acute conditions (e.g., acute Wernicke’s), this product may not provide adequate thiamine; alternative high‑dose thiamine regimens should be considered.

### Administration instructions
- Administration route: intramuscular injection is preferred because of reduced risk of anaphylaxis compared with intravenous administration.
- If intravenous administration is used, only give slowly as a diluted infusion (examples: diluted into an appropriate TPN or infusion solution) and monitor closely for any sign of anaphylaxis.
- For patient comfort, warm the solution to body temperature before administration.
- Single‑use only: use once and discard any residue.
- If solution is cloudy or contains particles, do not use.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known hypersensitivity to any component of the preparation (any of the listed B vitamins or excipient) — contraindicated.
- Pre‑existing B hypervitaminosis (vitamin B overdose states) — contraindicated.
- Dexpanthenol: contraindicated in haemophiliacs and in patients with ileus due to mechanical obstruction.
- Cyanocobalamin: should not be administered for megaloblastic anaemia caused by folate deficiency (risk of obscuring correct diagnosis).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions

### Boxed warnings
- No formal boxed warnings specified in available sources.

### General warnings and precautions
- Anaphylactic shock has occurred rarely after parenteral thiamine; most severe reactions have usually followed intravenous administration. Exercise caution with parenteral use and be prepared to manage anaphylaxis.
- Unconfirmed allergic reactions have been reported with dexpanthenol.
- Rare allergic hypersensitivity reactions have been reported with cyanocobalamin (and hydroxocobalamin in other contexts).
- High‑dose B complex injections are generally recommended intramuscularly because of the risk of anaphylaxis with parenteral administration.
- This preparation may not deliver sufficient thiamine for acute management of Wernicke’s encephalopathy; use appropriate high‑dose thiamine regimens for acute Wernicke’s.
- Administration of cyanocobalamin in doses >10 micrograms daily may produce a haematological response in patients with folate deficiency and thereby mask the underlying folate deficiency; use with caution where folate deficiency is possible.

### Specific clinical precautions
- Patients with documented allergic disposition who require parenteral thiamine therapy should, if parenteral therapy is essential, receive a carefully supervised intradermal test dose prior to full administration.
- Monitor patients closely after parenteral administration for early signs of hypersensitivity.
- Large doses of riboflavin can cause bright yellow discolouration of urine; this may interfere with certain laboratory tests (interpretation caution).

### Monitoring requirements
- Monitor for signs of hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis during and after parenteral administration.
- Monitor relevant haematological indices if treating patients in whom folate deficiency is a concern and cyanocobalamin is administered.
- Be aware of potential interference with laboratory assays due to riboflavin‑induced urine discoloration.

## 11. Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action
- The components are B‑group vitamins that function as essential coenzymes/cofactors in carbohydrate, amino acid and lipid metabolism and in the production of cellular energy.
  - Thiamine (B1): required for conversion of pyruvate to acetyl‑CoA; deficiency leads to impaired aerobic respiration and conditions such as beriberi and Wernicke‑Korsakoff syndrome.
  - Riboflavin (B2): precursor of FMN and FAD, involved in oxidative/reductive metabolic reactions; necessary for normal function of pyridoxine and niacin metabolism.
  - Nicotinamide (niacinamide, B3): precursor of NAD and NADP, central to redox reactions; deficiency causes pellagra.
  - Pantothenol (dexpanthenol, B5): contributes to formation of coenzyme A, essential for TCA cycle substrates.
  - Pyridoxine (B6): cofactor in amino acid metabolism, haem synthesis, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism; deficiency associated with peripheral neuritis.
  - Cyanocobalamin (B12): converted intracellularly to active coenzyme forms (methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin) required for folate recycling and certain metabolic pathways; deficiency leads to megaloblastic anaemia and neurological disease.

### Pharmacokinetics
- The pharmacokinetics of parenteral combinations have not been studied as combinations; it is expected each vitamin behaves as when administered separately.
- Cyanocobalamin: the fraction retained in blood and tissues after injection is rapidly converted to active coenzyme forms (methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin). Hydroxocobalamin is noted (in general context) to be better retained than cyanocobalamin, but hydroxocobalamin may elicit antibodies to the transcobalamin‑B12 complex in some patients (observational comment).

### Pharmacodynamics
- B vitamins act synergistically in metabolic pathways; deficiency of one often coexists with deficiency of others. Administration as a complex may be advantageous when multiple deficiencies are suspected.
