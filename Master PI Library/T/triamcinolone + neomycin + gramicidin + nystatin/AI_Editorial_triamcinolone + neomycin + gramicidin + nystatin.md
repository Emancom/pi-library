# Editorial Product Information: triamcinolone + neomycin + gramicidin + nystatin

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **triamcinolone + neomycin + gramicidin + nystatin**
- Chemical class:
  - Triamcinolone acetonide — topical corticosteroid (glucocorticoid)
  - Neomycin — aminoglycoside antibiotic
  - Gramicidin — peptide antibiotic (affects membrane permeability)
  - Nystatin — polyene antifungal agent
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Topical (skin application) only
- DO NOT include brand or trade names, dosage form physical descriptions, or schedule listings

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval: 30 September 1991 [Australian]
- Date of last revision/update of source PI: 13 October 2021 [Australian]
- Withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources
- If other jurisdictions’ registration/revision dates exist: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Relief of the inflammatory and pruritic manifestations of dermatoses that are likely to become, or are already, infected (i.e., combined anti-inflammatory and topical anti-infective therapy for infected or potentially infected inflammatory dermatoses).  
- No other indications specified in available sources.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adults)
- Apply to the affected area 2 to 3 times daily.

### Dosing by indication
- Specific dosing regimens by diagnosis or body site beyond the general recommendation are not specified in available sources.

### Administration instructions
- For cutaneous use only. Avoid contact with the eyes; ophthalmic use is contraindicated.
- Apply sparingly to the affected area.
- Do not bandage, cover, or wrap treated skin unless directed by a clinician; avoid occlusive dressings because they increase percutaneous absorption (notably of triamcinolone and neomycin) and the risk of sensitivity reactions.
- When applied in the inguinal area, instruct patients to apply sparingly and wear loose-fitting clothing (avoid tight-fitting plastic garments such as plastic pants/incontinence garments that could act as occlusive dressings).
- If sensitivity or irritation develops, discontinue use and institute appropriate therapy.
- If no favourable response occurs promptly, discontinue until infection is controlled by other anti-infective measures.
- Patients should be instructed to use the medication only as prescribed and not to use for other disorders.

### Titration schedules
- Not specified in available sources.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
List of all contraindications from available sources:
- Known hypersensitivity to triamcinolone acetonide, neomycin, nystatin, or gramicidin.
- Ophthalmic use (application to the eye is contraindicated).
- Tuberculous lesions of the skin.
- Most viral lesions of the skin (e.g., Herpes simplex), and in particular vaccinia and varicella.
- Note: Because of potential nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity associated with neomycin, use is not recommended in patients with extensive skin damage or other conditions that may permit significant systemic absorption (this is presented as a special precaution in the source but is clinically important to consider as a contraindication/major caution).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
- **Masked infection**: Corticosteroids may mask signs of infection; the anti-infective components can also mask hypersensitivity, leading to delayed recognition of allergic reactions.
- **Allergy and sensitisation**: Delayed-type hypersensitivity to neomycin and sensitivity reactions to gramicidin have been reported; neomycin sensitisation may follow prolonged use.
- **Nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity**: Because neomycin can be absorbed through inflamed or damaged skin, there is potential for nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity when applied to large surface areas or extensively damaged skin. Avoid use in such situations.
- **Occlusion**: Use of occlusive dressings increases percutaneous absorption (especially of triamcinolone and neomycin) and the risk of sensitivity reactions; occlusion should be avoided unless specifically directed by a clinician.
- **Overgrowth of nonsusceptible organisms**: Prolonged use may result in overgrowth of organisms not susceptible to the included antimicrobials, including fungi other than Candida. Corticosteroids can exacerbate microbial infections; continuous monitoring is required and change to appropriate therapy is necessary if superinfection occurs.
- **Lack of response**: If a favourable clinical response does not occur promptly, discontinue application until the infection is controlled by other anti-infective measures.
- **Systemic corticosteroid effects**: Systemic absorption of topical corticosteroids has produced reversible HPA (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal) axis suppression, manifestations of Cushing’s syndrome, hyperglycaemia and glucosuria in some patients.
  - Factors increasing systemic absorption include use of more potent steroids, application to large surface areas, prolonged use, and use under occlusion.
  - Patients receiving large doses or under conditions that may enhance absorption should be periodically evaluated for HPA axis suppression (urinary free cortisol and ACTH stimulation tests) and impairment of thermal homeostasis.
  - If systemic effects occur, consider withdrawal, dose reduction, or substitution with a less potent steroid. Recovery is generally prompt on discontinuation; however, steroid withdrawal signs may rarely require supplemental systemic corticosteroids.
- **Visual disturbance**: Visual disturbance (e.g., blurred vision) has been reported with systemic and topical corticosteroid use. Patients presenting with visual symptoms should be evaluated by an ophthalmologist for possible causes including cataract, glaucoma, or central serous chorioretinopathy.
- **Paediatric patients**:
  - Greater susceptibility to systemic effects, including HPA axis suppression and Cushing’s syndrome, due to larger skin surface area to body weight ratio.
  - Reported adverse effects in children include linear growth retardation, delayed weight gain, low plasma cortisol levels and absence of response to ACTH stimulation.
  - Intracranial hypertension presenting as bulging fontanelles, headaches and bilateral papilloedema has been reported in children receiving topical corticosteroids.
  - Use the least amount for the shortest duration compatible with effective therapy and closely monitor for systemic effects.
- **Monitoring requirements**
  - Repeat KOH smears, cultures or other diagnostic tests if lack of therapeutic response.
  - Evaluate for HPA axis suppression with urinary free cortisol and ACTH stimulation tests when clinically indicated (e.g., large dose, prolonged use, occlusion, paediatric patients).
  - Monitor for signs of nephrotoxicity or ototoxicity if large area application or impaired skin integrity is present.
  - Monitor for local skin reactions and signs of superinfection.
- **Patient counselling points**
  - Use as directed; for skin use only.
  - Avoid eye contact.
  - Do not use for disorders other than that prescribed; continue full course even if symptoms improve early.
  - Report adverse reactions promptly.
  - Avoid covering treated skin unless directed by a clinician; avoid tight/occlusive garments.

## 8. Drug Interactions

- Practical considerations based on components:
  - Because neomycin is an aminoglycoside that can be systemically absorbed from inflamed or damaged skin, there is potential for systemic aminoglycoside‑type toxicity (nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity). Concurrent systemic administration of other nephrotoxic or ototoxic agents is not described in the source, but clinicians should be aware of the potential for additive toxicity when systemic aminoglycosides are used — specific interaction studies or recommendations are not provided.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category: **Category D** [Australian].
- Rationale and precautions:
  - Aminoglycosides (example cited: gentamicin) cross the placenta and may be taken up selectively by the fetal kidney, potentially causing damage to immature nephrons; eighth cranial nerve damage (ototoxicity) also has been reported following in‑utero exposure to some aminoglycosides.
  - Because of chemical similarity, aminoglycosides (including neomycin) should be considered potentially nephrotoxic and ototoxic to the fetus; therapeutic maternal blood levels do not guarantee fetal safety.
  - Use in pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus.

### Breastfeeding
- It is not known whether topical administration results in sufficient systemic absorption to produce detectable quantities in breast milk.
- Systemically administered corticosteroids are excreted into breast milk in quantities not likely to harm the infant; nevertheless, exercise caution when administering this product to a nursing woman.

### Fertility
- Long-term animal studies evaluating carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, or impairment of fertility have not been performed or are not available in the sources.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Use over large surface areas or for prolonged periods can result in sufficient systemic absorption to produce systemic corticosteroid effects; paediatric patients may be more susceptible due to larger skin surface area to body weight ratio.
- Reported paediatric adverse effects include HPA axis suppression, Cushing’s syndrome, growth retardation, delayed weight gain, low plasma cortisol and lack of ACTH stimulation response; intracranial hypertension (bulging fontanelles, headaches, papilloedema) has also been reported.
- When used in children, limit to the least amount for the shortest duration compatible with effectiveness and closely monitor for systemic effects.

### Elderly use
- No specific data available on use in elderly patients in the source documents. Exercise usual clinical caution.

### Renal impairment
- Specific dosing adjustments by renal function (eGFR/stage) are not provided in available sources.
- Note: Neomycin is rapidly excreted unchanged by the kidneys when systemically absorbed (half‑life ~2–3 hours). Caution if significant systemic absorption is likely in patients with pre‑existing renal impairment because of potential nephrotoxicity.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **Triamcinolone acetonide**: topical corticosteroid with anti‑inflammatory, antipruritic and vasoconstrictive actions (mechanism of anti‑inflammatory action not fully elucidated).
- **Nystatin**: binds to sterols in fungal cell membranes, altering permeability and causing leakage of intracellular components; active against Candida species—resistance generally does not develop during therapy.
- **Neomycin**: aminoglycoside antibiotic that inhibits bacterial protein synthesis; primarily active against a number of gram‑negative organisms (not active against Pseudomonas aeruginosa); resistant strains can develop.
- **Gramicidin**: exerts antibacterial activity against many gram‑positive organisms by altering cell membrane permeability.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Vasoconstrictor assays and other lab methods are used to compare topical corticosteroid potency; vasoconstrictor potency correlates to some extent with clinical efficacy.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption:
  - Percutaneous absorption of topical corticosteroids depends on vehicle, skin integrity and occlusion; absorbed corticosteroids follow pharmacokinetic pathways similar to systemic corticosteroids.
  - Nystatin and gramicidin are not absorbed from intact skin or mucous membranes.
  - Neomycin can be absorbed through inflamed or damaged skin.
- Distribution:
  - Corticosteroids are variably bound to plasma proteins when systemically absorbed.
- Metabolism and elimination:
  - Corticosteroids are primarily metabolised in the liver and excreted by the kidneys; some are excreted in bile.
  - Once absorbed, neomycin is rapidly excreted unchanged in the urine; half‑life approximately 2–3 hours.
