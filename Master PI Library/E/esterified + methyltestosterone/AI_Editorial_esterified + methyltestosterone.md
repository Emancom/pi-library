## 5. Contraindications
List includes all contraindications cited in available sources:
- Undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding.
- Known, suspected, or history of breast cancer.
- Known or suspected estrogen‑dependent neoplasia.
- Active deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), or history of these conditions.
- Active or recent (e.g., within the past year) arterial thromboembolic disease (e.g., stroke, myocardial infarction).
- Liver dysfunction or disease.
- Known hypersensitivity to any ingredient of the product.
- Known or suspected pregnancy.
- Methyltestosterone‑specific contraindications:
  - Presence of severe liver damage.
  - Pregnancy and breastfeeding mothers because of the possibility of masculinization of the female fetus or breast‑fed infant.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed warnings
- **[US] Estrogen component increases the risk of endometrial cancer** — women with intact uteri treated with unopposed estrogens are at increased risk; evaluate persistent or recurrent abnormal vaginal bleeding.
- **[US] Cardiovascular and other serious risks** — hormone therapy with estrogens (with or without progestins) has been associated with increased risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, invasive breast cancer, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis in randomized trials; use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with treatment goals and individual risk profile.

### General warnings and precautions
- Cardiovascular disease:
  - Estrogen (with or without progestin) therapy should not be used for primary or secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease.
  - Increased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) events and stroke has been observed in large randomized trials when estrogen is combined with progestin.
  - Manage cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, obesity) appropriately while on therapy.
- Venous thromboembolism (VTE):
  - Estrogen therapy (with progestin) was associated with an increased rate of VTE (DVT and PE).
  - If feasible, discontinue estrogens at least 4–6 weeks before surgeries associated with increased thromboembolism risk or during prolonged immobilization.
- Malignant neoplasms:
  - Endometrial cancer: unopposed estrogens increase endometrial cancer risk; adding a progestin reduces risk of endometrial hyperplasia.
  - Breast cancer: combined estrogen‑progestin therapy has been associated with an increased risk of invasive breast cancer; therapies may increase the frequency of abnormal mammograms requiring further evaluation.
- Dementia:
  - In women 65 years and older, combined estrogen‑progestin therapy increased the risk of probable dementia in clinical study populations; applicability to younger postmenopausal women is unknown.
- Hepatic disease:
  - Do not use in patients with active liver dysfunction or severe liver damage (methyltestosterone specifically contraindicated in severe hepatic damage).
- Monitoring and surveillance:
  - Close clinical surveillance of women receiving estrogens is advised.
  - Evaluate unexplained persistent or recurrent vaginal bleeding; perform endometrial sampling when indicated.
  - Yearly breast examinations by a healthcare provider and monthly breast self‑examination are recommended; schedule mammography according to age, risk, and prior results.
  - Discontinue therapy if a VTE, myocardial infarction, or stroke develops or is suspected.
- Androgen‑related effects (methyltestosterone):
  - Androgenic effects include virilization features (e.g., facial/body hair changes, deepening of voice, clitoral enlargement); risk of fetal or infant masculinization when administered during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
  - Androgens may cause sodium, water, and other electrolyte retention; monitor patients with cardiac, renal, or hepatic dysfunction accordingly.
  - In children, exogenous androgens accelerate linear growth but may cause disproportionate bone maturation and premature epiphyseal closure.

### Monitoring requirements
- Regular clinical assessment for:
  - Signs/symptoms of VTE, CHD, stroke.
  - Breast and pelvic examination, mammography as appropriate.
  - Investigation of abnormal uterine bleeding (including endometrial sampling if indicated).
  - Surveillance for signs of liver dysfunction (specific LFT monitoring instructions not specified in available sources).
  - For methyltestosterone: monitor for signs of virilization and electrolyte/fluid retention (specific laboratory monitoring schedules not specified in available sources).

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Estrogen component is at least partially metabolized by CYP3A4; coadministration with inducers or inhibitors of CYP3A4 may alter estrogen exposure.
  - CYP3A4 inducers (may reduce estrogen concentrations and potentially reduce efficacy or alter bleeding profile): St. John’s Wort, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, rifampin. Management: monitor for reduced clinical effect; consider alternative therapy or dosing adjustment where clinically appropriate. [US]
  - CYP3A4 inhibitors (may increase estrogen concentrations and risk of adverse effects): erythromycin, clarithromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, ritonavir; grapefruit juice may also increase estrogen levels. Management: monitor for adverse effects and consider alternatives or dose adjustment. [US]

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Estrogens may increase coagulability and thus have additive thrombotic risk with other procoagulant states or medications that increase thromboembolism risk (e.g., selective estrogen receptor modulators, high‑risk prothrombotic conditions).
- Androgens may exacerbate fluid retention and have additive effects with medications that promote sodium/water retention (e.g., corticosteroids); monitor patients with cardiac or renal disease.

### Clinically significant interactions and management notes
- When coadministering with CYP3A4 inducers, expect possible decreased estrogen effect (e.g., return of vasomotor symptoms, changes in uterine bleeding). Consider monitoring clinical response.
- When coadministering with CYP3A4 inhibitors or grapefruit juice, monitor for increased estrogen‑related adverse effects (e.g., nausea, breast tenderness, thrombotic events).
- Specific interaction guidance for methyltestosterone with other drugs (beyond hepatic cautions) is not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Esterified estrogens: provide estrogenic activity by serving as circulating estrogen conjugates (predominantly estrone sulfate and related equine estrogens) that can be converted to active estrogens (e.g., estradiol) and act via nuclear estrogen receptors to modulate gene transcription in estrogen‑responsive tissues. Estrogens exert negative feedback on pituitary gonadotropin (LH, FSH) secretion.
- Methyltestosterone: a synthetic androgen that binds androgen receptors and exerts androgenic effects (development/maintenance of male secondary sexual characteristics; anabolic effects on protein metabolism).

### Pharmacokinetics
- Estrogens:
  - Distribution: Widely distributed with higher concentrations in sex hormone target tissues; circulate largely bound to sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) and albumin.
  - Metabolism: Metabolized in the liver through interconversions (estradiol ⇄ estrone ⇄ estriol); undergo sulfate and glucuronide conjugation and enterohepatic recirculation. A significant portion circulates as sulfate conjugates (e.g., estrone sulfate) in postmenopausal women.
  - Elimination: Estradiol, estrone, and estriol and their conjugates are excreted in the urine.
  - Drug metabolism considerations: Partial metabolism via CYP3A4; inducers/inhibitors of CYP3A4 can alter estrogen plasma concentrations.
- Methyltestosterone:
  - Oral methyltestosterone is less extensively inactivated on first pass than unmodified testosterone, which makes it orally active relative to testosterone.
  - Metabolism: Inactivated primarily in the liver and metabolized to various 17‑keto steroids; excreted mainly in the urine as glucuronide and sulfate conjugates; about 6% excreted in feces in unconjugated form (testosterone-related data).
  - Protein binding: Testosterone in plasma is largely bound to sex‑hormone binding globulin; free fraction determines activity and half‑life.
  - Half‑life: Synthetic androgens (including methyltestosterone) have longer half‑lives than testosterone; reported testosterone half‑life ranges widely in literature (10–100 minutes) but synthetic androgen kinetics differ.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Estrogens reduce elevated LH and FSH levels in postmenopausal women and restore estrogenic effects on target tissues.
- Androgens increase nitrogen retention, stimulate erythropoiesis, and produce virilizing and anabolic effects via androgen receptor‑mediated gene transcription.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Women’s Health Initiative (WHI):
  - Large randomized program enrolling ~27,000 predominantly healthy postmenopausal women to evaluate risks and benefits of oral conjugated estrogens (CE) alone and CE combined with medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) compared with placebo for prevention of chronic disease outcomes.
  - CE/MPA substudy enrolled 16,608 women (average age 63 years; range 50–79) and was stopped early because benefit did not outweigh risks according to predefined rules.
  - Key findings (CE/MPA vs placebo; average follow‑up ~5.2 years):
    - Coronary heart disease events: 37 vs 30 per 10,000 women‑years (treated vs placebo).
    - Stroke: 29 vs 21 per 10,000 women‑years.
    - Venous thromboembolism: 34 vs 16 per 10,000 women‑years.
    - Invasive breast cancer: 41 vs 33 cases per 10,000 women‑years.
    - Absolute excess risks per 10,000 women‑years in CE/MPA group: 7 more CHD events, 8 more strokes, 8 more pulmonary emboli, 8 more invasive breast cancers; absolute reductions included 6 fewer colorectal cancers and 5 fewer hip fractures; overall absolute excess events in the global index 19 per 10,000 women‑years.
- Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS):
  - Substudy of WHI enrolling 4,532 postmenopausal women ≥65 years to assess probable dementia incidence with CE/MPA vs placebo.
  - After ~4 years follow‑up: probable dementia diagnosed in 40 women in the hormone group (45 per 10,000 women‑years) vs 21 in placebo (22 per 10,000 women‑years); relative risk 2.05 (95% CI 1.21–3.48).
- Heart and Estrogen/Progestin Replacement Study (HERS):
  - Randomized trial in postmenopausal women with established coronary heart disease (n=2,763; average age 66.7 years) evaluating CE/MPA (0.625 mg/2.5 mg) for secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease.
  - Over average follow‑up of 4.1 years, CE/MPA did not reduce overall CHD event rate; more CHD events occurred in the treated group during year 1 but not in subsequent years.
  - HERS II open‑label extension (additional ~2.7 years; total follow‑up 6.8 years) showed comparable CHD event rates between groups overall.

- Notes on applicability:
  - The WHI and WHIMS findings reflect outcomes observed with conjugated estrogens with or without medroxyprogesterone acetate in the studied doses and populations; other estrogen/progestin combinations, doses, and routes were not fully studied and should be assumed to carry similar risks in the absence of comparable data.
