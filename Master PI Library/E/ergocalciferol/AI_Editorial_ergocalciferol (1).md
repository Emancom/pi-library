# Editorial Product Information: ergocalciferol

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **ergocalciferol**
- Chemical class: Vitamin D sterol (ergocalciferol; vitamin D2); synthetic calcium-regulating steroid derivative
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral
- Strengths: Not all strengths listed in available sources; one marketed strength in source material equals 1.25 mg ergocalciferol (equivalent to 50,000 USP Units / International Units). Note: 1 USP unit = 1 IU; 1 mcg vitamin D = 40 USP Units.

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources.
- Dates of marketing start for specific product listings (as provided in source material): 02/18/2019; 08/28/2019; 03/17/2021; 12/06/2021. (These are product marketing start dates reported in the available source.)
- Date of last revision/update of source PI: Revised: 7/2026 (as reported in the available source).
- Withdrawals, suspensions, restrictions: Not specified in available sources.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of hypoparathyroidism.
- Treatment of refractory rickets (vitamin D–resistant rickets).
- Treatment of familial hypophosphatemia.

(These indications are reported in the available source PI.)

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General principles
- Individualize dosage carefully; the therapeutic-to-toxic range is narrow. Frequent monitoring of serum calcium (and phosphate where appropriate) is required during therapy and during dose titration.
- Time to onset: there is a lag of approximately 10–24 hours between administration and initiation of clinical action due to required hepatic and renal hydroxylation to active metabolites.

### Dosing by indication (adult)
- Vitamin D–resistant rickets: 12,000 to 500,000 USP Units daily.
- Hypoparathyroidism: 50,000 to 200,000 USP Units daily concomitantly with calcium lactate 4 g, six times per day.

Note: Pediatric doses must be individualized; specific pediatric dosing schemes are not provided in the available sources.

### Administration instructions
- Administer orally.
- Ensure adequate dietary calcium for clinical response.
- Evaluate total vitamin D intake from fortified foods, supplements, self-administered and prescription sources and adjust therapeutic dose accordingly.
- When high therapeutic doses are used, follow progress with frequent blood calcium determinations and other appropriate monitoring (see Section 6).

### Titration and monitoring schedule
- Blood calcium and phosphorus determinations should be made every 2 weeks or more frequently if necessary when treating hypoparathyroidism, or when high doses are used.
- In the treatment of hypoparathyroidism, X-rays of the bones should be taken every month until the condition is corrected and stabilized.

### Missed dose instructions
- Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Hypercalcemia.
- Malabsorption syndrome.
- Abnormal sensitivity to the toxic effects of vitamin D.
- Hypervitaminosis D.

(These contraindications are reported in the available source PI.)

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
- **Narrow therapeutic index**: The range between therapeutic and toxic doses is narrow. Individualize dosing and monitor closely. In vitamin D–resistant rickets in particular, the margin between therapeutic and toxic doses is especially narrow; progress should be followed with frequent blood calcium determinations when high doses are employed.
- Hypersensitivity to vitamin D may be a contributing factor in infants with idiopathic hypercalcemia; vitamin D must be strictly restricted in these cases.
- When treating hypoparathyroidism, intravenous calcium, parathyroid hormone, and/or dihydrotachysterol may be required adjunctively.
- In patients with hyperphosphatemia (as frequently seen in renal osteodystrophy), maintenance of normal serum phosphate is essential to reduce risk of metastatic calcification; dietary phosphate restriction and/or intestinal phosphate binders (e.g., aluminum gels) may be necessary.
- Formulation excipient caution: formulations containing FD&C Yellow No. 5 (tartrazine) may cause allergic-type reactions including bronchial asthma in susceptible individuals; although the overall incidence of tartrazine sensitivity in the general population is low, it is more frequently seen in patients who also have aspirin hypersensitivity.
- Carcinogenesis, mutagenesis, impairment of fertility: No long-term animal studies are available to evaluate these risks for ergocalciferol in the available source.
- Pregnancy: Animal reproduction studies have shown fetal abnormalities associated with hypervitaminosis D. Avoid vitamin D intake in excess of the recommended dietary allowance during normal pregnancy unless the prescriber judges the potential benefit to outweigh hazards. (See Section 9.)
- Nursing mothers: Exercise caution; large maternal doses can result in appearance of vitamin D metabolites (including 25-hydroxycholecalciferol) in breast milk and cause hypercalcemia in the breastfed infant. Monitor infant serum calcium if maternal large doses are given.
- Pediatric use: Doses must be individualized; close monitoring is required when used in infants and children because excess vitamin D can impair growth and cause hypercalcemia.
- Geriatric use: Data are insufficient to determine whether older adults respond differently from younger patients; absorption of orally administered vitamin D may be attenuated in elderly individuals. Dose selection in elderly patients should be cautious, generally starting at the low end of the dosing range, accounting for the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, concomitant disease, or other drug therapy.

### Monitoring requirements
- Serum calcium: baseline and then every 2 weeks or more frequently during dose titration and while on high doses.
- Serum phosphorus: baseline and as clinically indicated, especially in renal disease and familial hypophosphatemia.
- Renal function tests and urinalysis (to detect hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis) as clinically indicated.
- Bone radiographs: monthly during the treatment of hypoparathyroidism until the condition is corrected and stabilized.
- Infant serum calcium monitoring when the mother is treated with large doses and is breastfeeding.

### Overdose and management
- Hypervitaminosis D presents with hypercalcemia and associated signs (anorexia, nausea, weakness, weight loss, vague aches, constipation, mental status changes, anemia, mild acidosis) and end-organ damage (renal impairment, nephrocalcinosis, vascular and soft tissue calcification). The effects of administered vitamin D may persist for two or more months after cessation of treatment.
- Initial management: immediate withdrawal of vitamin D, institution of a low-calcium diet, generous fluid intake, along with symptomatic and supportive treatment.
- In hypercalcemic crisis (with dehydration, stupor, coma, and azotemia): intravenous saline hydration to restore volume and increase urinary calcium excretion; a loop diuretic (furosemide or ethacrynic acid) may be added to the saline infusion to further promote renal calcium excretion.
- Other measures that have been reported include dialysis, and administration of citrates, sulfates, phosphates, corticosteroids, EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid), and mithramycin via appropriate regimens. Supportive care and treatment of complications (e.g., renal insufficiency) are required.
- Recovery is the usual outcome when no permanent damage has occurred; deaths from renal or cardiovascular failure have been reported.
- Toxic doses: the toxic oral dose of ergocalciferol in dogs has been reported as 4 mg/kg; the lethal dose in animals is unknown from the available source.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- Pharmacokinetic interactions
  - Mineral oil: interferes with absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, including ergocalciferol. Concurrent use of mineral oil may reduce the oral bioavailability of ergocalciferol.
- Pharmacodynamic interactions
  - Thiazide diuretics: when thiazide diuretics are given to hypoparathyroid patients who are concurrently receiving ergocalciferol, hypercalcemia may develop or be exacerbated.
- Other clinical considerations
  - When treating hypoparathyroidism, concomitant administration of calcium (e.g., calcium lactate as specified in dosing), parathyroid hormone replacement, or other vitamin D analogues (e.g., dihydrotachysterol) may be needed to control hypocalcemia; manage concomitant therapies under specialist guidance.
  - In conditions associated with hyperphosphatemia (e.g., renal osteodystrophy), use of intestinal phosphate binders (e.g., aluminum gels) or dietary phosphate restriction is recommended to control serum phosphate and reduce risk of metastatic calcification; this is a therapeutic adjunct rather than a classic drug-drug interaction.
- Jurisdictional interaction notes: Not specified differences by region in available sources.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Pregnancy category (as reported in available source): Category C.
- Rationale and clinical guidance:
  - Animal reproduction studies in several species reported fetal abnormalities associated with hypervitaminosis D, including a syndrome characterised by supravalvular aortic stenosis, elfin facies, and mental retardation.
  - Avoid vitamin D intake in excess of the recommended dietary allowance during normal pregnancy; use higher doses only if, in the prescriber's judgment, potential benefits in a specific, unique case outweigh the significant hazards to the fetus.
  - The safety of doses exceeding 400 USP Units (IU) daily during pregnancy has not been established in the available source.

### Breastfeeding
- Caution is advised when administering ergocalciferol to nursing women.
- Large maternal doses can result in the appearance of 25-hydroxycholecalciferol (and other metabolites) in breast milk and have been associated with hypercalcemia in breastfed infants; monitoring of the infant's serum calcium concentration is required in such cases.

### Fertility
- Impairment of fertility: No long-term animal studies are available to evaluate effects on fertility in the available source.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Dosing must be individualized for pediatric patients.
- Infants and children are particularly susceptible to the toxic effects of vitamin D; hypervitaminosis D can cause a decline in the average rate of linear growth, increased bone mineralization (described in severe cases as dwarfism), and hypercalcemia.
- Monitor serum calcium and growth parameters closely when used in children.

### Elderly use
- Clinical studies did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged 65 and over to determine whether they respond differently from younger patients; reported clinical experience has not identified clear differences, but absorption of orally administered vitamin D may be attenuated in elderly individuals.
- Use caution with dose selection in the elderly; generally start at the low end of the dosing range and monitor hepatic, renal, and cardiac function as well as other comorbidities.

### Renal impairment
- Specific dosing adjustments by eGFR or disease stage are not provided in the available sources.
- Patients with renal disease are at higher risk of hyperphosphatemia and metastatic calcification; maintain normal serum phosphate through dietary restriction and/or phosphate binders to reduce risk.

### Hepatic impairment
- Specific dosing adjustments for hepatic impairment are not provided in the available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- **Ergocalciferol** is a vitamin D precursor that undergoes sequential hydroxylation: first in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, then in the kidneys to the biologically active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D metabolite.
- Active vitamin D metabolites promote the active absorption of calcium and phosphorus by the small intestine, thereby elevating serum calcium and phosphate levels sufficiently to permit bone mineralization.
- Vitamin D metabolites also mobilize calcium and phosphate from bone and likely increase renal tubular reabsorption of calcium and possibly phosphate.
- Parathyroid hormone regulates renal conversion to active metabolites.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption: Not fully specified in available sources; mineral oil is known to reduce absorption of fat-soluble vitamins including ergocalciferol.
- Distribution: Not fully specified in available sources.
- Metabolism: Ergocalciferol is hydroxylated in the liver (to 25-hydroxyvitamin D) and in the kidneys (to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D).
- Elimination: Not specified in available sources.
- Physicochemical notes: Ergocalciferol is a white, colourless crystal that is insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents, and slightly soluble in vegetable oils; it is affected by air and by light.
- Time to clinical effect: There is a lag of 10–24 hours reflecting the requirement for metabolic activation in the liver and kidneys.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Elevation of serum calcium and phosphate through increased intestinal absorption and bone mobilization; effects on renal handling of calcium and phosphate are mediated in part by active vitamin D metabolites, with regulation by parathyroid hormone.
