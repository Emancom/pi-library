# Editorial Product Information: estradiol + progesterone

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **estradiol + progesterone**
- Chemical class: Sex steroid hormones — estrogen (estradiol) and progestogen (progesterone)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: Oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Date of first registration/approval by jurisdiction: Not specified in available sources
- Date of last revision/update of each source PI: Not specified in available sources
- Any withdrawal, suspension, or restriction history: Not specified in available sources

## 3. Approved Indications
- Continuous combined hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for estrogen deficiency symptoms in postmenopausal women with an intact uterus and with at least 12 months since last menses.
- Note on initiation timing:
  - Natural menopause: treatment should commence 12 months after last natural menstrual bleed.
  - Surgically-induced menopause: treatment may be started immediately.
- Not specified in available sources whether other indications are approved.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult)
- Recommended dose: one oral unit containing 1 mg estradiol and 100 mg progesterone once daily (use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with treatment goals and risks).
- Take once daily in the evening with food.

### Dosing by indication / initiation
- Continuous combined regimen for postmenopausal symptoms in women with intact uterus.
- Women switching from a continuous sequential or cyclical preparation: complete the current cycle (e.g., the 28-day cycle) then change to the continuous combined regimen.
- Patients changing from another continuous combined preparation may start at any time.

### Titration schedules
- Not specified in available sources beyond recommendation to use the lowest effective dose.

### Administration instructions
- Take one dose each evening with food.
- Continuous daily dosing; do not omit the progestogen component in women with an intact uterus.

### Missed dose instructions
- If a dose is forgotten, take it as soon as possible if within 12 hours.
- If more than 12 hours have elapsed, omit the missed dose and resume the regular dosing schedule with the next scheduled dose; do not double-dose.
- Missed doses >12 hours may increase likelihood of breakthrough bleeding or spotting.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known, past or suspected breast cancer.
- Known or suspected estrogen-dependent malignant tumours (e.g., endometrial cancer).
- Undiagnosed genital bleeding.
- Untreated endometrial hyperplasia.
- Previous or current venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism).
- Known thrombophilic disorders (examples given: protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency).
- Active or recent arterial thromboembolic disease (e.g., angina, myocardial infarction).
- Acute liver disease or a history of liver disease as long as liver function tests have failed to return to normal.
- Porphyria.
- Known hypersensitivity to estradiol, progesterone, or any excipient.
- Any other contraindication specifically listed in local labelling should be followed (Not specified in available sources beyond the above).

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed / prominent warnings
- **Estrogens and progestogens should not be used for the prevention of cardiovascular disease or dementia.**
  - Randomised data (Women’s Health Initiative and substudies) demonstrated increased risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, invasive breast cancer, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis with combined estrogen-progestogen regimens versus placebo in postmenopausal women aged 50–79 years, and an increased risk of probable dementia when initiated in women aged ≥65 years in the memory sub-study.

### General warnings and precautions
- HRT should be initiated only for symptoms that adversely affect quality of life; use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with treatment goals and individual risk profile.
- Reassess benefit–risk at least annually and continue HRT only while benefits outweigh risks.
- Evidence on risks in premature menopause is limited; absolute risk is lower in younger women and benefit–risk balance may be more favourable.

### Medical examination / follow-up
- Prior to initiation or reinstitution, take a complete personal and family medical history and perform appropriate physical examination (including pelvic and breast exams as indicated).
- Periodic follow-up examinations are recommended; imaging (e.g., mammography) according to accepted screening practices and individualized clinical needs.
- Counsel women on breast changes to report.

### Conditions requiring supervision or caution (may recur or be aggravated)
- Leiomyoma (uterine fibroids) or endometriosis.
- Risk factors for thromboembolic disorders (see Venous thromboembolism).
- Risk factors for estrogen-dependent tumours (e.g., first-degree family history of breast cancer).
- Hypertension.
- Liver disorders (including history of liver adenoma).
- Diabetes mellitus with or without vascular involvement.
- Cholelithiasis.
- Migraine or severe headache.
- Systemic lupus erythematosus.
- History of endometrial hyperplasia.
- Epilepsy.
- Asthma.
- Otosclerosis.

### Reasons for immediate withdrawal of therapy
- Discovery of any contraindication.
- Jaundice or significant deterioration in liver function.
- Significant increase in blood pressure.
- New onset of migraine-type headache.
- Pregnancy.

### Specific risks and clinical precautions
- Endometrial hyperplasia/cancer:
  - Estrogen-only therapy increases risk of endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma in women with an intact uterus. Continuous combined estrogen–progestogen therapy or cyclic progestogen for at least 12 days per 28-day cycle reduces this excess risk.
  - Breakthrough bleeding/spotting is common during early months; new onset or persistent bleeding after treatment cessation should be investigated (e.g., endometrial biopsy) to exclude malignancy.
- Breast cancer:
  - Combined estrogen–progestogen therapy increases the risk of breast cancer with risk rising with duration of use and becoming apparent after about 3 (range 1–4) years; excess risk declines after stopping but may persist depending on duration of prior use.
  - HRT increases mammographic density, possibly reducing radiologic detection sensitivity.
- Ovarian cancer:
  - Epidemiological data suggest a small increased risk of ovarian cancer with estrogen-only and combined HRT; may appear within 5 years of use and diminish over time after stopping.
- Venous thromboembolism (VTE):
  - HRT increases risk of VTE (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism) approximately 1.3–3-fold; risk is highest in the first year of use.
  - Recognised VTE risk factors include older age, major surgery, prolonged immobilisation, obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2), pregnancy/postpartum, systemic lupus erythematosus, cancer.
  - For elective surgery with prolonged immobilisation, consider stopping HRT 4–6 weeks before surgery and not restarting until the woman is fully mobilised.
  - In women with first-degree relative with early thrombosis, screening for thrombophilia may be offered after counselling; identification of severe thrombophilic defects that segregate with family thrombotic events contraindicates HRT.
  - If VTE develops after initiating therapy, discontinue HRT.
- Coronary artery disease (CAD) and ischaemic stroke:
  - No evidence that combined or estrogen-only HRT prevents myocardial infarction. Combined HRT may slightly increase CAD risk; both combined and estrogen-only regimens are associated with up to ~1.5-fold increased risk of ischaemic stroke. Absolute risk increases with age.
- Cognition / dementia:
  - Initiation of HRT in women aged ≥65 years has been associated with increased risk of probable dementia in a large trial sub-study.
- Hepatic impairment:
  - Contraindicated in acute liver disease or history of liver disease while liver function tests remain abnormal; estrogens may cause fluid retention — use caution in hepatic dysfunction.
- Thyroid function and binding proteins:
  - Estrogens increase thyroid binding globulin (TBG), corticoid binding globulin (CBG), and sex-hormone binding globulin (SHBG), increasing total circulating levels of thyroid and other protein-bound hormones while free hormone concentrations remain unchanged. Interpret total hormone assays accordingly.
- Hypertriglyceridemia:
  - Exogenous estrogens may cause large increases in triglycerides in rare cases, precipitating pancreatitis in women with pre-existing marked hypertriglyceridemia.
- Hereditary or acquired angioedema:
  - Exogenous estrogens may induce or exacerbate angioedema.
- ALT elevations with certain HCV antivirals:
  - In trials of certain hepatitis C combination regimens, clinically significant ALT elevations were observed more frequently in women using ethinylestradiol-containing products; limited data suggest estradiol-containing products had similar rates to non-users but caution is warranted when co-administering with specified HCV regimens (see Drug Interactions).

### Monitoring requirements
- Baseline and periodic breast examinations and mammography per local guidance and individualized risk.
- Monitor blood pressure.
- Monitor liver function tests if clinically indicated.
- Monitor triglycerides in women with pre-existing hypertriglyceridemia.
- If on concomitant medications affecting CYP450, monitor for reduced efficacy or increased adverse effects (see Drug Interactions).
- If co-administered with lamotrigine, monitor seizure control and lamotrigine plasma concentrations where available.

## 8. Drug Interactions
### Pharmacokinetic interactions
- Metabolism:
  - Both estradiol and progesterone are metabolised via cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP450). Co-administration with inducers or inhibitors can change plasma concentrations and clinical effect.
- Enzyme inducers (may increase metabolism of estrogens/progestogens → decreased effectiveness / altered bleeding profile):
  - Examples: anticonvulsants (phenobarbital, phenytoin, carbamazepine), anti-infectives (rifampicin, rifabutin), antiretrovirals (nevirapine, efavirenz), griseofulvin, herbal St John’s Wort (Hypericum perforatum).
  - Note: ritonavir and nelfinavir, although recognised as inhibitors in some contexts, may exhibit inducing properties when co-administered with steroid hormones.
- Enzyme inhibitors (may increase exposure to progesterone):
  - Examples: ketoconazole and other CYP3A4 inhibitors — may increase progesterone bioavailability and incidence of progesterone-related adverse effects (nausea, breast tenderness, headache).
- Lamotrigine:
  - Estrogen-containing hormonal contraceptives have been shown to significantly decrease lamotrigine plasma concentrations via induction of lamotrigine glucuronidation. While specific data for HRT are not available, a similar interaction is expected with estradiol + progesterone; monitor seizure control and consider lamotrigine dose adjustment.
- Ciclosporin:
  - Progesterone may raise plasma concentrations of ciclosporin; monitor ciclosporin levels and adjust dose as appropriate.

### Pharmacodynamic interactions
- Co-administration with certain hepatitis C antiviral regimens:
  - In trials with ombitasvir/paritaprevir/ritonavir and dasabuvir (± ribavirin), and in some trials with glecaprevir/pibrentasvir or sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir, significant ALT elevations were observed more frequently in women using ethinylestradiol-containing products. Limited data indicate women using estradiol-containing products had similar ALT rates to non-users in some datasets, but numbers were small. Caution is advised when co-administering estradiol-containing HRT with these HCV regimens.
- Anticoagulants:
  - Women on chronic anticoagulant therapy require careful benefit–risk consideration prior to initiation of HRT. If VTE occurs, discontinue HRT.
- Other:
  - Estrogens can alter levels of various binding proteins (TBG, CBG, SHBG) and thus may affect total circulating hormone measurements and assays for thyroid and other protein-bound hormones.

### Management of interactions
- When enzyme inducers are co-administered, counsel patient on possible reduced HRT efficacy (return of menopausal symptoms, change in bleeding); consider alternative therapies or higher monitoring.
- When co-administered with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, monitor for increased progesterone-related adverse effects.
- Monitor lamotrigine-treated patients closely for loss of seizure control and adjust lamotrigine dosing if needed.
- Monitor ciclosporin plasma levels if co-administered and adjust dose per ciclosporin therapeutic monitoring.
- For planned elective surgery with prolonged immobilisation, stop HRT 4–6 weeks prior to surgery to reduce VTE risk.

## 9. Use in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
### Pregnancy
- Use in pregnancy: **Not indicated during pregnancy.** If pregnancy occurs, discontinue therapy immediately.
- Animal data: High maternal doses of estrogens produced urogenital malformations in offspring in animal studies; high doses of progestogens produced masculinisation of female fetuses in animal studies. Relevance to clinical use is uncertain.
- Epidemiological data: Most available epidemiological studies of inadvertent fetal exposure to combinations of estrogens and progestogens show no clear teratogenic or fetotoxic effect, but data are limited.
- Pregnancy category:
  - [Australian] Category D (as stated in available sources).
  - Other jurisdictional pregnancy category systems: Not specified in available sources.

### Breastfeeding
- Not indicated during lactation.
- Effects on milk production or infant safety: Not specified in available sources.

### Fertility
- **Not indicated for use in women with childbearing potential.**
- Effects on fertility if used prior to conception: Not specified in available sources.

## 10. Use in Special Populations
### Paediatric use
- Not indicated in paediatric populations. Safety and efficacy in patients under 18 years have not been established.

### Elderly use
- Clinical experience is limited in women >65 years.
- HRT does not improve cognitive function; evidence suggests increased risk of probable dementia when HRT is initiated in women aged ≥65 years.
- Consider age-related baseline absolute risk increases for VTE, stroke, CAD and malignancy when assessing therapy.

### Renal impairment
- Estrogens may cause fluid retention; exercise caution and monitor patients with cardiac or renal dysfunction.
- Specific dosing adjustments by eGFR or renal stage: Not specified in available sources.

### Hepatic impairment
- Contraindicated in acute liver disease or history of liver disease while liver function tests remain abnormal.
- Specific dosing adjustments by severity of hepatic impairment: Not specified in available sources.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Estradiol is the principal endogenous estrogen; progesterone is a natural progestogen. Together in combined therapy, estradiol relieves estrogen deficiency symptoms of menopause while progesterone opposes estrogen-induced endometrial proliferation in women with an intact uterus.
- Detailed molecular mechanism: Not specified in available sources beyond the above general description.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Metabolism: Both estradiol and progesterone are metabolised via cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP450).
- Absorption, distribution, elimination (detailed parameters such as Tmax, Cmax, half-life, bioavailability): Not specified in available sources.
- Effect on plasma binding proteins: Estrogens increase thyroid-binding globulin (TBG), corticoid-binding globulin (CBG), and sex-hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), increasing total (but not free) circulating levels of their respective bound hormones.

### Pharmacodynamics
- Estrogens increase circulating total thyroid and other protein-bound hormones by increasing binding proteins, without changing free hormone concentrations.
- Effects on lipid, carbohydrate metabolism, coagulation and fibrinolysis parameters have been observed with sex steroid therapy; specific quantitative effects for this product: Not specified in available sources.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Pivotal trial summary (as reported in available sources):
  - A one-year, Phase 3 trial evaluated estradiol + progesterone capsules. Total enrolled approximately 1,835 postmenopausal women (1,684 received various estradiol+progesterone dose regimens; 151 received placebo).
  - In the trial, six new cases of breast cancer were diagnosed among active-treated subjects across dose groups; specifically, two cases occurred in a subgroup treated with 0.5 mg estradiol/100 mg progesterone (n=424) and two cases in the subgroup treated with 1 mg estradiol/100 mg progesterone (n=415). No new breast cancer cases were reported in the placebo group (n=151) in the report excerpt provided.
  - Reported treatment-emergent adverse events occurring in ≥3% of the 1 mg estradiol/100 mg progesterone arm and more common than placebo included: breast tenderness (10.4%), headache (3.4%), pelvic pain (3.1%), vaginal haemorrhage (3.4%), and vaginal discharge (3.4%); nausea was reported at 2.2% in that arm.
  - Full trial design details (primary endpoints, statistical results, full adverse event listing) beyond the above summary: Not specified in available sources.

- Other clinical trial evidence and long-term safety data referenced in the source text included large randomized trials of HRT in general (e.g., Women’s Health Initiative and Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study) demonstrating increased risks described in Warnings; details of those trials are summarized in the Warnings section but specific numeric results beyond those described above are not reproduced here.
