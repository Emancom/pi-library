# Editorial Product Information: efavirenz + emtricitabine + tenofovir

## 1. Product Identification
- Active ingredient (INN/generic name): **efavirenz + emtricitabine + tenofovir**
- Chemical class:
  - Efavirenz: non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)
  - Emtricitabine: nucleoside analogue reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI)
  - Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate: nucleoside analogue reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) (as presented in available sources)
- ATC code: Not specified in available sources
- Route(s) of administration: oral

## 2. Regulatory History
- Initial U.S. approval: 2006.
- Date of last revision/update of provided PI: Revised 11/2025.
- Withdrawals, suspensions, restrictions: Not specified in available sources for any jurisdiction other than the details above.
- If registration/approval dates for jurisdictions other than the United States are required: Not specified in available sources.

## 3. Approved Indications
- Treatment of HIV‑1 infection as a complete antiretroviral regimen or in combination with other antiretroviral agents for:
  - Adults
  - Pediatric patients weighing at least 40 kg
- Jurisdictional note: The provided source documents describe the above indication for use in adults and pediatric patients ≥40 kg [US]. Information about additional or differing indications in other jurisdictions is not specified in available sources.

## 4. Dosage and Administration
### General dosing (adult and pediatric ≥40 kg)
- One tablet once daily containing efavirenz 600 mg, emtricitabine 200 mg, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate 300 mg.
- Take orally on an empty stomach; dosing at bedtime may improve tolerability of nervous system symptoms.

### Dosing by special circumstance
- Coadministration with rifampin:
  - If coadministered with rifampin in patients weighing ≥50 kg: take the combination tablet once daily and add an additional 200 mg of efavirenz per day (i.e., an extra efavirenz 200 mg) to compensate for the interaction.
  - For patients <50 kg: Not specified in available sources (use caution; consult full local guidance if available).
- Renal impairment:
  - Not recommended in patients with estimated creatinine clearance below 50 mL/min.
- Hepatic impairment:
  - Not recommended in patients with moderate to severe hepatic impairment (Child‑Pugh B or C). Careful monitoring recommended in mild hepatic impairment.

### Administration instructions
- Administer on an empty stomach, preferably at bedtime to reduce nervous system adverse effects.
- Prior to initiation and periodically during treatment, perform recommended baseline and follow‑up testing (see Section 6 Monitoring).

### Titration and missed dose
- Titration schedules: Not specified in available sources.
- Missed dose instructions: Not specified in available sources.

## 5. Contraindications
- Known clinically significant hypersensitivity (e.g., Stevens‑Johnson syndrome, erythema multiforme, or toxic skin eruptions) to efavirenz or a component of the combination.
- Concomitant administration with voriconazole (contraindicated).
- Concomitant administration with elbasvir/grazoprevir (contraindicated).
- Additional region‑specific contraindications: Not specified in available sources beyond those listed above.

## 6. Warnings and Precautions
### Boxed/Prominent warnings
- **Posttreatment acute exacerbation of hepatitis B:** Severe acute exacerbations of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection have been reported in patients coinfected with HBV and HIV‑1 who discontinued products containing emtricitabine and/or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, components of this combination. Closely monitor hepatic function clinically and by laboratory testing for several months after stopping therapy in patients coinfected with HIV‑1 and HBV. If appropriate, initiation of anti‑hepatitis B therapy may be warranted.

### General warnings and precautions
- Rash:
  - New onset rash is common. Discontinue the product for severe rash associated with blistering, desquamation, mucosal involvement, or fever. For life‑threatening cutaneous reactions (e.g., Stevens‑Johnson syndrome), consider alternative therapy.
- Hepatotoxicity:
  - Cases of hepatitis, including fulminant hepatitis and hepatic failure requiring transplantation or resulting in death, have been reported. Monitor liver enzymes before and during treatment; consider discontinuation for persistent elevations (see Monitoring). Not recommended in moderate or severe hepatic impairment.
- Psychiatric symptoms:
  - Serious psychiatric adverse experiences (including severe depression, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, psychosis) have been reported. Immediate medical evaluation is recommended for patients who develop new or worsening psychiatric symptoms.
- Nervous system symptoms:
  - Central nervous system effects (dizziness, impaired concentration, somnolence, abnormal dreams, hallucinations, etc.) are common, typically starting within 1–2 days and resolving within 2–4 weeks. Dosing at bedtime may reduce severity.
  - Late‑onset neurotoxicity (e.g., ataxia, encephalopathy) has been reported months to years after therapy initiation; some events have occurred in association with CYP2B6 polymorphisms and increased efavirenz exposure.
- Renal impairment:
  - New onset or worsening renal impairment including acute renal failure and Fanconi syndrome has been reported. Prior to initiation and during treatment assess serum creatinine, estimated creatinine clearance, urine glucose and urine protein in all patients. In patients with chronic kidney disease also assess serum phosphorus. Avoid concurrent or recent use of nephrotoxic drugs.
- Embryo‑fetal toxicity:
  - Fetal harm may occur when administered during the first trimester. Avoid pregnancy during treatment and for 12 weeks after discontinuation. Pregnancy testing and effective contraception are recommended for males and females of reproductive potential prior to and during therapy.
- Bone mineral density:
  - Decreases in bone mineral density have been observed. Consider assessment of bone mineral density in patients with a history of pathological fracture or other risk factors for osteoporosis or bone loss.
- Convulsions:
  - Use with caution in patients with a history of seizures.
- Lactic acidosis and severe hepatomegaly with steatosis:
  - Lactic acidosis and severe hepatomegaly with steatosis, including fatal cases, have been reported with nucleoside analogue reverse transcriptase inhibitors.
- Immune reconstitution syndrome:
  - May occur after initiation of antiretroviral therapy and may necessitate further evaluation and treatment.
- Fat redistribution/accumulation:
  - Changes in body fat distribution have been observed.
- Drug interactions and QTc:
  - Risk of clinically significant drug interactions exists. Efavirenz has been associated with QTc prolongation; consider alternatives when coadministered with drugs that have known risk of Torsade de Pointes or in patients at higher risk for Torsade de Pointes.

### Monitoring requirements
- Test for hepatitis B virus infection prior to or when initiating therapy.
- Baseline and periodic monitoring of:
  - Serum creatinine and estimated creatinine clearance
  - Urine glucose and urine protein
  - Serum phosphorus in patients with chronic kidney disease
  - Liver function tests (transaminases) prior to and during treatment
  - Pregnancy testing in adolescents and adults of childbearing potential prior to initiation
  - Clinical assessment for psychiatric or severe nervous system symptoms throughout treatment
- After discontinuation in HBV‑coinfected patients: monitor hepatic function clinically and by laboratory follow‑up for at least several months.

## 8. Drug Interactions
- General:
  - Concomitant use with certain drugs may result in significant interactions leading to loss of therapeutic effect, development of resistance, or clinically significant adverse reactions due to increased exposure of efavirenz or concomitant drugs. Review concomitant medications prior to and during therapy.
- Specific interactions and management notes:
  - Rifampin:
    - Reduces efavirenz exposure; if coadministered with rifampin in patients ≥50 kg, add an extra 200 mg efavirenz per day to the combination tablet regimen.
  - HIV‑1 protease inhibitors:
    - Coadministration with lopinavir/ritonavir or darunavir/ritonavir increases tenofovir concentrations; monitor for tenofovir toxicity.
    - Coadministration with atazanavir or atazanavir/ritonavir is not recommended.
  - Voriconazole:
    - Concomitant administration is contraindicated.
  - Elbasvir/grazoprevir:
    - Concomitant administration is contraindicated.
  - Nephrotoxic drugs:
    - Avoid concurrent or recent use with nephrotoxic agents because of risk of worsening renal impairment and Fanconi syndrome.
  - Drugs that prolong QTc or carry risk of Torsade de Pointes:
    - Efavirenz has been associated with QTc prolongation; consider alternatives when coadministering with medications that have known risk of Torsade de Pointes or in patients at elevated risk for torsades.
- Efavirenz assay interference:
  - Mention of efavirenz assay interference is present in the full prescribing information; details not specified in the provided excerpt.
- Region‑specific interaction management:
  - Not specified in available sources beyond the above; where local product labels differ, consult jurisdictional guidance.

## 11. Pharmacology
### Mechanism of action
- Efavirenz: non‑nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) that inhibits HIV‑1 reverse transcriptase.
- Emtricitabine and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate: nucleoside (nucleotide) analogue reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) that inhibit HIV‑1 reverse transcriptase.

### Pharmacokinetics
- Absorption/distribution/metabolism/elimination: Detailed quantitative pharmacokinetic parameters (e.g., Cmax, Tmax, half‑life, clearance, volume of distribution) are not specified in the provided sources.
- Noted pharmacologic points from available sources:
  - Dosing at bedtime may improve tolerability of nervous system symptoms, suggesting CNS pharmacodynamic effects occurring soon after dosing.
  - Genetic polymorphisms (CYP2B6) have been associated with increased efavirenz plasma levels and late‑onset neurotoxicity in some patients.
  - QTc prolongation has been observed with efavirenz exposure; clinical significance varies by context and concomitant medication use.

### Pharmacodynamics
- HIV‑1 activity via inhibition of reverse transcriptase through complementary mechanisms of NNRTI and NRTIs.
- Effects on QTc: QTc prolongation observed; monitor when combined with other QT‑prolonging agents.

## 12. Clinical Trials
- Key trials and findings reported in provided sources:
  - Controlled clinical trial programs:
    - In pooled or specific controlled trials, 1,008 adult subjects treated with efavirenz‑containing regimens were compared with 635 control subjects. Nervous system adverse events and psychiatric events were more frequent in efavirenz‑treated subjects versus controls.
    - Nervous system symptoms (any grade) occurred in 53% of efavirenz‑treated subjects vs 25% of controls; common specific events included dizziness (28.1%), insomnia (16.3%), impaired concentration (8.3%), somnolence (7.0%), abnormal dreams (6.2%), and hallucinations (1.2%).
    - Serious psychiatric events in efavirenz‑treated subjects included severe depression (2.4%), suicidal ideation (0.7%), and nonfatal suicide attempts (0.5%); 1% discontinued due to psychiatric symptoms.
  - Study AI266006 (Study 006; Phase 3, randomized, open‑label; NCT00002410):
    - Compared EFV‑containing regimens with control regimens (total N reported in analysis = 1,266 in pooled arms).
    - Median follow‑up and evaluation periods varied among arms (e.g., median follow‑up 180 weeks for an EFV‑zidovudine‑lamivudine arm in the analysis cited).
    - Treatment with efavirenz was associated with increased occurrence of selected psychiatric symptoms compared with controls in multifactorial analysis; other factors contributing to psychiatric symptoms included history of injection drug use, psychiatric history, and baseline psychiatric medication use.
  - Pediatric data:
    - In a pediatric cohort of 182 subjects treated with efavirenz, rash occurred in 32%; two pediatric subjects experienced Grade 3 rash and four experienced Grade 4 rash; median time to rash onset was 28 days.
  - Adverse reaction incidence:
    - Rash: adults — 26% new‑onset rash with 600 mg efavirenz compared with 17% in controls; Grade 4 rash in adults 0.1%; blistering/desquamation/ulceration occurred in ~0.9%.
    - Discontinuation rate for rash in adult trials approximately 1.7%.
- Additional trial details (specific endpoints, virologic suppression rates, statistical analyses) are not specified in the provided sources.
